## What it is

Pneumonia is an infection of the lungs. The tiny air sacs become inflamed and can fill with fluid or pus, which makes breathing harder and lowers the oxygen reaching your blood. It can be caused by bacteria, viruses or, less often, fungi.[^2]

It ranges from a mild illness you recover from at home to a life-threatening one. Babies and young children, adults over 65 and people with long-term health conditions or weakened immunity are most at risk.[^2][^3] Worldwide, pneumonia is the leading infectious cause of death in children, and it killed more than 700,000 children under five in 2019.[^1]

## Symptoms

Symptoms can start suddenly or build over a few days:[^2][^3]

- A cough, which may bring up yellow, green or bloodstained phlegm.
- Fever, sweating and chills.
- Shortness of breath or fast breathing.
- Chest pain that is worse when you breathe in or cough.
- Tiredness, aching and loss of appetite.

Older adults may have few of these and instead become confused or unusually sleepy.[^2] In young children, watch for fast breathing or the lower chest pulling in with each breath.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The most common bacterial cause is Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus). Viruses are also frequent causes, including flu, RSV and the virus that causes COVID-19.[^1][^2] Pneumonia can also follow inhaling food, drink or vomit into the lungs, or develop during a hospital stay.[^3]

The risk is higher if you smoke, drink heavily, have a lung or heart condition, diabetes or a weakened immune system, have trouble swallowing, or have recently been in the hospital.[^2][^4]

## Diagnosis

A doctor will listen to your chest and check your breathing rate and oxygen level. A chest X-ray confirms pneumonia, and blood and phlegm tests may be used to identify the cause in people who are sicker or in the hospital.[^2][^5] Doctors use severity scores to help decide who can safely be treated at home.[^5]

## Treatment

- **Bacterial pneumonia** is treated with antibiotics, taken as prescribed.[^3][^5]
- **Viral pneumonia** does not respond to antibiotics. Antiviral medicines help in some cases, such as flu.[^2] [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help) explains the difference.
- **At home**, rest, drink plenty of fluids and use fever and pain relief as directed on the label.[^3]
- **In the hospital**, treatment may include oxygen, fluids and antibiotics given into a vein.[^2]

Most people get better in two to four weeks, although tiredness and a cough can linger for longer.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Vaccines are the best protection: pneumococcal, flu, COVID-19 and RSV vaccines for those eligible, along with routine childhood vaccines.[^1][^4] Who is eligible differs by country, so check your national schedule or see [Vaccines for Adults](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide).

Washing your hands, not smoking and limiting alcohol also lower your risk.[^2][^4] For babies, breastfeeding, good nutrition and clean indoor air are protective.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

Get medical advice the same day if you have a cough with a fever and breathlessness or chest pain, if you are coughing up blood, or if a cough has lasted more than three weeks.[^3] Do not wait if you are over 65, pregnant or have a long-term condition. [Fever: When to Worry](/articles/fever-when-to-worry) covers temperatures that need attention.

**Call your local emergency number** if you or your child is struggling to breathe, has blue, gray or pale lips or skin, becomes confused or very drowsy, or is breathing very fast.[^3]
