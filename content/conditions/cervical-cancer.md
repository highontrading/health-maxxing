## What it is

Cervical cancer develops in the cervix, the lower part of the uterus that opens into the vagina. Almost all cases are caused by long-lasting infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus passed on through sexual contact.[^1]

Almost everyone who is sexually active gets HPV at some point, and the immune system usually clears it. When an infection persists, it can cause abnormal cells that turn into cancer slowly, typically over 15 to 20 years. That long window is what makes cervical cancer so preventable.[^1]

It is the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, with about 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths in 2022. About 94% of those deaths were in low- and middle-income countries, where vaccination and screening are harder to get.[^1]

## Symptoms

Abnormal cells and early cervical cancer usually cause no symptoms.[^4] When symptoms do appear, they include:[^1][^6]

- vaginal bleeding that is unusual for you: after sex, between periods or after menopause
- periods that are heavier or longer than usual
- a change in vaginal discharge
- pain during sex
- pain in the lower back, pelvis or lower abdomen

These symptoms usually have other causes, but they should always be checked.[^6]

## Causes and risk factors

A persistent HPV infection is more likely to progress to cancer with:[^1][^2]

- a weakened immune system; women living with HIV are six times more likely to develop cervical cancer
- smoking
- other sexually transmitted infections
- long-term use of hormonal contraceptives
- having had several children

Not being screened is the other big risk.[^2] HPV is so common that having it is nothing to be ashamed of.

## Diagnosis

Screening uses a sample from the cervix. An HPV test checks it for high-risk HPV, and a Pap test, also called a smear test, looks for abnormal cells.[^4] If a result is abnormal, the cervix is examined with a magnifying instrument (colposcopy) and a tissue sample may be taken. Cancer is confirmed by biopsy, and scans show whether it has spread.[^3]

Schedules vary by country:

- The WHO recommends an HPV test every 5 to 10 years from age 30.[^1]
- US guidance covers ages 21 to 65: a Pap test every three years, or from age 30 an HPV test every five years.[^5]
- The NHS invites people aged 25 to 64.[^7]

[Breast and Cervical Screening](/articles/breast-and-cervical-screening) compares the programs.

## Treatment

Abnormal cells found by screening can be removed or destroyed in a short procedure, which stops cancer from developing.[^6] For cancer, treatment depends on the stage:[^2][^3]

- **Early cancer:** surgery, ranging from removing part of the cervix, which can preserve fertility, to hysterectomy.
- **Locally advanced cancer:** radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy.
- **Advanced or recurrent cancer:** chemotherapy, often with targeted therapy or immunotherapy.

Cervical cancer can be cured when it is diagnosed early and treated promptly.[^1] In US data, the five-year relative survival rate is about 91% when the cancer is still confined to the cervix.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

- **HPV vaccination.** The WHO recommends vaccinating girls aged 9 to 14, before they become sexually active. Many countries also vaccinate boys and offer catch-up doses to older teenagers and young adults. Check your national schedule or our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide).[^1]
- **Screening,** even if you have been vaccinated, because the vaccine does not protect against every cancer-causing type of HPV.[^7]
- **Condoms and not smoking.** Condoms lower the chance of catching HPV but do not fully protect against it, and smoking makes it harder for the body to clear the virus.[^6]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have bleeding after sex, between periods or after menopause, a change in vaginal discharge, or pain during sex or in your pelvis.[^6] Do not wait for your next screening appointment. Screening is for people without symptoms.

If you are overdue for screening, book it. Get urgent care for very heavy vaginal bleeding, or bleeding with dizziness or fainting.
