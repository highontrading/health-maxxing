## What it is

A vaginal yeast infection, also called thrush or vulvovaginal candidiasis, is an overgrowth of Candida. This yeast normally lives in small amounts in the vagina, mouth and gut without causing trouble. The usual culprit is Candida albicans.[^1][^4]

It is very common. An estimated 75% of women have at least one episode in their lives, and 40% to 45% have two or more.[^1] It is not classed as a sexually transmitted infection, although sex can sometimes trigger it.[^2]

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms are:[^1][^2]

- itching and irritation around the vulva and vagina
- thick, white discharge that looks like cottage cheese and does not usually smell
- soreness or stinging during sex or when you urinate
- redness, swelling or small cracks in the skin of the vulva

None of these is unique to yeast.[^1] A discharge that smells fishy or looks gray, yellow or green points to other conditions, such as bacterial vaginosis or trichomoniasis, which need different treatment.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Yeast infections develop when the normal balance of yeast and bacteria in the vagina shifts. That is more likely if you:[^2][^4]

- are taking antibiotics, or have taken them recently
- are pregnant
- have diabetes that is not well controlled
- have a weakened immune system, for example from HIV, chemotherapy or steroid medicines
- use hormonal contraceptives or hormone therapy

Often there is no obvious trigger.[^1] Douching and perfumed washes do not help. They can irritate the skin and upset the natural balance.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Because the symptoms overlap with other conditions, a first episode should be confirmed by a health professional, not assumed. Diagnosis is based on your symptoms, an examination and, where needed, a swab of vaginal discharge that is examined under a microscope or sent for culture.[^1] The acidity (pH) of the vagina stays normal in a yeast infection, which helps to tell it apart from bacterial vaginosis.[^1]

Culture is especially useful when infections keep coming back or do not respond to treatment, because less common types of Candida need different medicines.[^1]

## Treatment

Yeast infections are treated with antifungal medicines called azoles:[^1][^2]

- **Vaginal creams or pessaries** (tablets placed in the vagina), such as clotrimazole or miconazole, used for one to seven days depending on the product. Many are sold in pharmacies and should be used as directed on the label.
- **An oral tablet** of fluconazole.

Both approaches work well. Azoles relieve symptoms in 80% to 90% of people who complete treatment.[^1] Symptoms should clear within 7 to 14 days.[^2]

Some situations need a different plan:[^1]

- **Pregnancy:** only vaginal treatments, used for seven days, are recommended. Oral fluconazole is avoided.
- **Recurrent infections** (three or more in a year, by the CDC's definition): a longer first course followed by maintenance treatment for six months.
- **Partners** do not usually need treatment unless they have symptoms.

Oil-based creams and pessaries can weaken latex condoms and diaphragms.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

- Wash the vulva with water or a plain, unperfumed emollient, and skip perfumed soaps, douches and vaginal deodorants.[^2]
- Dry properly after washing, wear cotton underwear and avoid tight clothing.[^2]
- Take antibiotics only when they are needed. [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help) explains why.
- If you have diabetes, keeping your blood sugar under control helps.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or sexual health clinic, instead of treating yourself, if:[^2]

- you have these symptoms for the first time
- you are pregnant or breastfeeding
- you are under 16 or over 60
- treatment has not worked, or the infection keeps coming back
- you have diabetes or a weakened immune system

Also get checked if you have sores or blisters, bleeding between periods, pain in your lower abdomen, a fever or a discharge with a strong smell, because these suggest a different infection.
