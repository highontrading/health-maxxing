## What it is

Insomnia means regularly having trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, even though you have enough opportunity to sleep, and feeling the effects during the day.[^3]

Short-term insomnia lasts days or weeks and is usually set off by stress or a change in routine. Chronic insomnia means poor sleep on three or more nights a week for more than three months.[^3] About 3 in 10 adults report insomnia symptoms, and up to about 1 in 10 have chronic insomnia.[^5]

## Symptoms

At night: lying awake for a long time, waking repeatedly, or waking early and not getting back to sleep. By day: tiredness, poor concentration, irritability, low mood and worry about sleep itself.[^3][^4]

## Causes and risk factors

Insomnia usually has more than one cause. Common contributors include:[^3][^4]

- stress, anxiety and depression
- irregular schedules, shift work and jet lag
- caffeine, alcohol and nicotine
- pain, hot flashes and other health problems
- some medicines
- other sleep disorders, such as [sleep apnea](/conditions/sleep-apnea) or restless legs

It is more common in women and in older adults.[^3]

Whatever starts it, insomnia is often kept going by understandable coping habits: going to bed early, lying in, napping and spending long hours awake in bed. These teach the brain to link bed with being alert, which is the link behavioral treatment sets out to break.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on your sleep history. Your clinician may ask you to keep a sleep diary for a week or two and will look for conditions and medicines that could be contributing.[^3] Most people do not need an overnight sleep study. It is used when another sleep disorder, such as sleep apnea, is suspected.[^3]

## Treatment

For chronic insomnia, the first-line treatment is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), not sleeping pills. Both the American College of Physicians and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommend it.[^1][^2]

CBT-I is a structured program, usually delivered over several weeks, that combines:[^1]

- **Sleep restriction:** matching your time in bed to the sleep you actually get, then extending it gradually
- **Stimulus control:** using bed only for sleep and sex, and getting up when you cannot sleep
- **Cognitive therapy:** tackling the worries and beliefs that keep you alert
- **Relaxation and sleep education**

It can be delivered in person, in groups or through digital programs.[^2] Sleep hygiene advice is worth following, but the sleep medicine guideline advises against relying on it as the only treatment for chronic insomnia.[^1]

Medication may be added, usually for a short period and after a discussion of benefits and harms, when CBT-I alone has not worked.[^2] Sleeping pills can cause next-day drowsiness and dependence, which is why doctors now prescribe them sparingly.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

Habits that help: go to bed and get up at the same times every day, exercise regularly during the day, avoid caffeine, alcohol and nicotine in the hours before bed, wind down for an hour beforehand, and keep the bedroom dark and quiet.[^4] If you cannot sleep, get up and do something calm until you feel sleepy.[^1]

For more, see [Insomnia: Why You Can't Sleep and What Actually Works](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works) and [sleep hygiene habits](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have slept badly for months, if changing your habits has not helped, or if tiredness is making daily life hard to cope with.[^4] Mention loud snoring, pauses in breathing or an urge to move your legs at night, which point to other sleep disorders.

Do not drive when you feel sleepy.[^4] If sleeplessness comes with thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^6]
