## What it is

Gout is a type of inflammatory arthritis. It happens when urate (uric acid), a normal waste product, builds up in the blood and forms needle-shaped crystals in and around a joint. The immune system reacts to the crystals, and the result is a sudden, very painful attack called a flare.[^1]

Many people with high urate never develop gout.[^1]

## Symptoms

A flare usually comes on fast, often overnight: severe pain, swelling, warmth and redness in one joint. The big toe is the classic site, but ankles, knees, feet, wrists and fingers can be affected.[^1][^2] Flares typically settle within one to two weeks.[^1]

Without long-term treatment, flares tend to become more frequent and involve more joints. Urate can collect as firm lumps under the skin, called tophi, and joints can be permanently damaged.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Urate rises when the body makes too much or, more often, when the kidneys clear too little. Gout is more common in men, in women after menopause and with increasing age.[^1]

Other risk factors include a family history, overweight, high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, and medicines such as diuretics (water tablets) and low-dose aspirin. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without advice. Alcohol, sugary drinks and foods rich in purines, such as red meat, organ meats and some seafood, raise urate too.[^1]

Gout has an unfair reputation as a disease of overindulgence. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) notes that diet changes alone usually lower urate only a little, and that genes play a large part.[^3]

## Diagnosis

Doctors often diagnose gout from the pattern of symptoms plus a blood urate test. Urate can be normal during a flare, so the test may be repeated a couple of weeks later.[^4] If there is doubt, fluid drawn from the joint with a needle is examined under a microscope for crystals, which also helps rule out a joint infection. Ultrasound or other scans are sometimes used.[^4]

## Treatment

There are two separate jobs: calming flares and lowering urate.

For a flare, the ACR recommends an anti-inflammatory medicine started as early as possible: a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), colchicine or a corticosteroid. Which is chosen depends on your kidneys, stomach, heart and other medicines. Resting and icing the joint help too.[^3]

To prevent flares, urate-lowering medicine is taken every day, usually for life. Allopurinol is the ACR's preferred first choice, with febuxostat and other medicines as alternatives. The ACR strongly recommends this treatment for people with tophi, joint damage from gout or two or more flares a year.[^3]

The dose is adjusted to a blood target. The ACR aims for urate below 6 mg/dL (360 µmol/L). NICE in the UK uses the same target and suggests a lower one, below 300 µmol/L, for people with tophi or continuing flares.[^3][^4] Starting urate-lowering treatment can set off flares at first, so a preventive anti-inflammatory is often given for the first three to six months.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Medicine does most of the work, but habits help: limiting [alcohol](/articles/alcohol-and-your-health), sugary drinks and large portions of purine-rich foods, and losing weight gradually, without crash diets, if you have overweight.[^2][^3] The ACR advises against vitamin C supplements for gout because the benefit is too small.[^3]

Keep taking urate-lowering medicine when you feel well and during flares. Stopping it lets urate climb again. [Arthritis: Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid, Explained](/articles/arthritis-osteo-vs-rheumatoid) covers other common joint conditions.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor for any first attack of sudden joint pain and swelling, and if flares keep returning. Repeated flares are a reason to discuss urate-lowering treatment.[^4]

Get urgent help if a hot, swollen joint comes with a fever or chills, or if you feel sick and the pain keeps getting worse. This can mean an infection inside the joint, which needs emergency treatment.[^2]
