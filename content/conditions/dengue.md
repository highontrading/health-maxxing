## What it is

Dengue is a viral infection spread by the bite of infected Aedes mosquitoes. It is found in tropical and subtropical regions around the world, mostly in towns and cities. About half of the world's population now lives in areas at risk, and the WHO estimates 100 to 400 million infections a year.[^1] Reported cases reached record highs in 2023 and 2024.[^1]

There are four closely related dengue viruses. Infection with one gives lasting protection against that type only, and a second infection with a different type carries a higher risk of severe disease.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

Most people who catch dengue have no symptoms or a mild illness.[^1] When symptoms occur, they usually start 4 to 10 days after the bite and last 2 to 7 days: a high fever, often around 40 °C (104 °F), with severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea, vomiting, swollen glands and a rash.[^1]

A small proportion of people go on to develop severe dengue, in which blood vessels leak and bleeding, shock or organ damage can follow. The warning signs often appear as the fever goes away, just when people expect to be recovering:[^1][^2]

- severe abdominal pain
- persistent vomiting
- bleeding gums or nose, or blood in vomit or stool
- rapid breathing
- extreme tiredness, restlessness or weakness
- pale, cold skin

Severe dengue is a medical emergency.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

You get dengue from mosquito bites, not directly from other people. The risk comes from living in or traveling to places where dengue circulates, including much of Asia, the Pacific, the Americas, Africa and the Caribbean.[^1][^3] Local outbreaks have also been reported in parts of southern Europe and the southern United States.[^1][^2]

Severe dengue is more likely if you have had dengue before, and in infants and pregnant women.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Dengue is confirmed with a blood test.[^4] Tests that detect the virus itself work in the first week of illness, and antibody tests are used later.[^2] Doctors also follow blood counts to watch for signs that the illness is turning severe. Always tell the doctor where you have traveled.

## Treatment

There is no specific antiviral treatment for dengue. Care is supportive: rest, plenty of fluids, and acetaminophen (paracetamol), used as directed on the label, for fever and pain.[^1] Avoid ibuprofen, aspirin and other anti-inflammatory painkillers, which can raise the risk of bleeding.[^1]

People with warning signs are cared for in hospital, where fluids given into a vein and close monitoring are the mainstay. Early recognition and good medical care greatly reduce deaths from severe dengue.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

Avoiding mosquito bites is the main protection. The mosquitoes that carry dengue bite during the day:[^1]

- wear clothing that covers your arms and legs
- use an insect repellent containing DEET, picaridin (icaridin) or IR3535
- use window screens, and a bed net if you sleep during the day
- empty, cover or clean containers that collect water, where the mosquitoes breed

Dengue vaccines exist, but they are recommended only for specific groups. The WHO recommends one, TAK-003, for children aged 6 to 16 in places with high dengue transmission. Availability and eligibility differ by country, so check national guidance or ask a travel clinic.[^1]

If you have dengue, keep avoiding bites during the first week of illness, so that mosquitoes do not carry the virus on to other people.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you develop a fever while in, or soon after returning from, an area where dengue is found, and say where you have been.[^3] [Fever: What It Means and When to Worry](/articles/fever-when-to-worry) covers fever care in general.

Go to an emergency department or call your local emergency number straight away if any warning sign appears, particularly in the day or two after the fever settles.[^1][^2]
