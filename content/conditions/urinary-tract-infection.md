## What it is

A urinary tract infection (UTI) is an infection in any part of the urinary system. Most are bladder infections (cystitis), which are uncomfortable but usually not serious. If bacteria travel up to the kidneys, the result is a kidney infection (pyelonephritis), which needs prompt treatment.[^1][^2]

UTIs are among the most common infections. At least 40 to 60% of women have one at some point, and about 1 in 4 women have a repeat infection.[^1] They are less common in men.[^5]

## Symptoms

A bladder infection typically causes:[^2]

- Pain or a burning feeling when you urinate.
- Needing to urinate more often or more urgently than usual, including at night.
- Cloudy urine, or blood in the urine.
- Pain in the lower abdomen.

A kidney infection adds fever, chills, nausea or vomiting, and pain in the back or side just under the ribs.[^1][^2] In older, frail adults, a UTI may show up as new confusion or agitation.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

Most UTIs are caused by bacteria that normally live in the bowel, usually E. coli, entering the urethra and reaching the bladder. Women are more prone because the urethra is shorter and closer to the anus.[^1]

Other risk factors are sexual activity, menopause, pregnancy, diabetes, a urinary catheter, anything that blocks the flow of urine (such as [kidney stones](/conditions/kidney-stones) or an [enlarged prostate](/conditions/benign-prostatic-hyperplasia)), and not emptying the bladder fully.[^1][^2]

## Diagnosis

Doctors diagnose a UTI from your symptoms, often with a dipstick test on a urine sample. A urine culture, which identifies the bacteria and the antibiotics that work against them, is used when infections keep returning or the picture is less straightforward.[^1][^3]

Bacteria in the urine without any symptoms is common, particularly in older adults. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (2019) recommends against treating it in most people, with pregnancy the main exception.[^4]

## Treatment

Bladder infections are treated with a short course of antibiotics, and symptoms usually start to ease within a few days.[^1][^2] Which antibiotic you get depends on the bacteria likely to be involved and your medical history.[^1] Drinking plenty of fluids and using pain relief as directed on the label help in the meantime.[^2]

Kidney infections need a longer course, and sometimes hospital treatment with antibiotics given into a vein.[^1]

For frequent infections, defined as two in six months or three in a year, the options include a low-dose preventive antibiotic, an antibiotic taken after sex and, after menopause, vaginal estrogen.[^3] Cranberry products may reduce repeat infections in some women, but they do not treat an active infection.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

The evidence for most everyday prevention tips is modest, but they are low-risk:[^1][^2]

- Drink plenty of fluids so that you urinate regularly.
- Go when you need to, and empty your bladder fully.
- Urinate soon after sex.
- Wipe from front to back.
- If you get repeated UTIs, consider avoiding spermicides and diaphragms.

Antibiotics work best when they are used only when needed. See [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have UTI symptoms for the first time, if they have not improved within two days, or if they come back after treatment. Men, pregnant women, children and older or frail adults with symptoms should always be assessed.[^2]

**Get medical help the same day** if you have a fever or shivering, pain in your back or side, or blood in your urine. These suggest a kidney infection.[^2]

**Call your local emergency number** for confusion, drowsiness, difficulty speaking, very fast breathing or not passing urine all day. These can be signs of sepsis, a life-threatening reaction to infection.[^2]
