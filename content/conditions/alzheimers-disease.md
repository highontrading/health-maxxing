## What it is

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive brain disease and the most common cause of dementia, the umbrella term for a decline in memory and thinking that is severe enough to interfere with daily life. It may contribute to 60 to 70% of dementia cases.[^1] About 57 million people were living with dementia in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases every year.[^1]

In Alzheimer's, two proteins build up abnormally in the brain: amyloid, which forms plaques between nerve cells, and tau, which forms tangles inside them. Nerve cells lose their connections and die.[^2] It is not a normal part of aging.[^2]

## Symptoms

The first sign is usually trouble remembering recent events and conversations. The Alzheimer's Association lists ten warning signs, including repeating questions, difficulty planning or handling money, losing track of dates and places, trouble finding words, misplacing things, poorer judgment, and withdrawal or personality change.[^3]

Symptoms worsen gradually over years. In the later stages people need help with dressing, eating and moving around, and may not recognize family.[^6]

The difference from normal aging is the effect on daily life. Forgetting a name and recalling it later is typical. Forgetting how to do things you have always done is not.[^3] See [Dementia or Normal Aging?](/articles/dementia-early-signs-vs-normal-aging).

## Causes and risk factors

Age is the strongest risk factor: most people with Alzheimer's are 65 or older.[^2] Family history and a gene variant called APOE ε4 raise risk, and rare inherited gene changes cause early-onset disease in a small number of families.[^7]

Some risk can be changed. The 2024 Lancet Commission identified 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia, among them hearing loss, high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, physical inactivity, diabetes, depression and social isolation. It estimated that addressing all 14 could in theory prevent or delay about 45% of cases, an estimate that rests largely on observational studies.[^4]

## Diagnosis

There is no single test. Assessment usually includes a history from you and someone who knows you well, memory and thinking tests, and blood tests to rule out treatable causes of memory problems such as thyroid disease or vitamin B12 deficiency.[^5][^6] A brain scan (CT or MRI) helps exclude other causes such as strokes or tumors.[^6]

In specialist clinics, PET scans or spinal fluid tests can detect amyloid and tau directly, and blood tests for these proteins are beginning to enter practice.[^7]

## Treatment

There is no cure. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and memantine can modestly ease symptoms for a time.[^5] NICE also recommends group cognitive stimulation therapy for people with mild to moderate dementia.[^5]

Two antibody medicines that clear amyloid, lecanemab and donanemab, are approved in the United States and some other countries for early Alzheimer's. In trials they slowed decline modestly, and they require regular infusions and MRI scans to check for brain swelling or bleeding. Eligibility and availability vary by country.[^2][^7]

Support for carers, attention to hearing and eyesight, and planning ahead for future care matter as much as medication.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

Nothing guarantees prevention, but what protects the heart appears to protect the brain: staying active, controlling blood pressure and cholesterol, not smoking, limiting alcohol, treating hearing loss and staying socially connected.[^4] [Dementia Prevention](/articles/dementia-prevention-what-we-know) walks through the evidence.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if changes in memory or thinking are affecting daily life, or if family members have noticed a change. Several conditions that mimic dementia are treatable.

Confusion that comes on suddenly, over hours or days, is not typical of Alzheimer's. It may be delirium caused by an infection or a medicine, or a stroke, and needs urgent medical assessment.[^5]
