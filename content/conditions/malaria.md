## What it is

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites, which are passed to people through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It does not spread directly from person to person.[^1]

Malaria is preventable and curable. Even so, the WHO estimates more than 250 million cases and about 600,000 deaths a year. About 95% of the deaths occur in the WHO African Region, most of them in children under 5.[^1]

Five species of the parasite infect humans. Plasmodium falciparum, the most common in Africa, is the deadliest. Plasmodium vivax is the main species in most countries outside sub-Saharan Africa and can lie dormant in the liver, causing relapses months later.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

Symptoms usually begin 10 to 15 days after the bite, though some infections show up much later.[^1][^2] Early malaria looks like many other illnesses: fever, chills, headache, muscle aches and tiredness, sometimes with nausea, vomiting or diarrhea.[^2][^4]

That ordinary start is the danger. Untreated falciparum malaria can progress to severe illness and death within 24 hours.[^1] Signs of severe malaria include extreme tiredness, confusion or reduced consciousness, repeated seizures, difficulty breathing, dark or bloody urine, yellowing of the eyes and skin, and abnormal bleeding.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The risk comes from being bitten in an area where malaria is transmitted: much of sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Asia, the Pacific, and Central and South America.[^3] Rarely, malaria is passed on through blood transfusion or contaminated needles.[^1]

Infants and children under 5, pregnant women, people with HIV and travelers are at higher risk of severe infection.[^1] Protection built up by living in a malaria area fades after you move away, so people returning to visit family are at risk too.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Malaria is diagnosed with a blood test: either a blood sample examined under a microscope or a rapid diagnostic test. The WHO recommends confirming every suspected case this way.[^1] If the first test is negative and malaria is still suspected, it is repeated.[^2]

## Treatment

Malaria always needs prompt treatment with prescription medicines. The choice depends on the type of parasite, drug resistance where the infection was caught, the person's age and weight, and pregnancy:[^1]

- Artemisinin-based combination therapies are the most effective treatment for falciparum malaria.
- Chloroquine is used for vivax malaria only in places where the parasite is still sensitive to it.
- Primaquine is added to prevent relapses of P. vivax and P. ovale infection.
- Severe malaria is treated in hospital with medicines given by injection.

## Prevention and self-care

- **Avoid bites.** Malaria mosquitoes bite mainly between dusk and dawn. Sleep under an insecticide-treated net, use a repellent containing DEET, icaridin or IR3535, cover your arms and legs in the evening, and use window screens.[^1]
- **Take preventive medicine when you travel.** See a doctor or travel clinic before your trip, ideally four to six weeks ahead. The right antimalarial depends on where you are going, and it has to be taken before, during and after the trip exactly as prescribed.[^2][^3] No preventive measure works 100% of the time.
- **Vaccines.** The WHO recommends two malaria vaccines, RTS,S and R21, for children living in areas where malaria is common.[^1] They are not used as travel vaccines.

## When to see a doctor

Treat any fever during or after travel to a malaria area as an emergency, even if you took preventive tablets and even if months have passed. The CDC advises seeking immediate medical care for a fever or flu-like illness while traveling and for up to one year after returning.[^2] Ask for a malaria test the same day and say where you have been. [Fever: What It Means and When to Worry](/articles/fever-when-to-worry) covers fever in general.

Call your local emergency number for confusion, unusual drowsiness, seizures, difficulty breathing, yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, or vomiting that stops you keeping medicine down.[^1]
