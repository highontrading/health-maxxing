## What it is

A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury. It happens when a bump, blow or jolt to the head, or a hit to the body, makes the brain move rapidly inside the skull and temporarily changes how it works.[^1]

You do not have to be knocked out to have one. Standard CT and MRI scans usually look normal, because the problem is one of function, not visible structural damage.[^3]

## Symptoms

Symptoms can appear straight away or emerge over the following hours or days.[^1] They fall into four groups:[^1]

- **Physical:** headache, dizziness, nausea, balance problems, blurred vision, sensitivity to light or noise
- **Thinking:** feeling foggy or slowed down, trouble concentrating, memory gaps around the event
- **Emotional:** irritability, anxiety, sadness
- **Sleep:** sleeping more or less than usual, trouble falling asleep

## Causes and risk factors

Falls are the most common cause of traumatic brain injury, especially in young children and older adults. Sports, road crashes and assaults account for many of the rest.[^4]

People who take blood-thinning medicines, or who have a bleeding disorder, are at higher risk of bleeding inside the skull and should always be checked after a head injury.[^2][^5]

## Diagnosis

A clinician diagnoses concussion from the story of the injury and an examination of memory, concentration, balance, vision and nerve function.[^3] Most people do not need a CT scan. It is used when signs suggest a more serious injury, such as bleeding or a skull fracture.[^5]

## Treatment

The advice has changed: strict rest in a dark room until every symptom has gone is no longer recommended. The 2022 international consensus advises relative rest for the first 24 to 48 hours, with less screen time, followed by a gradual return to light activity such as walking, provided symptoms flare no more than mildly.[^3]

After that, return to school or work in steps, then to sport through a graded program in which each stage takes at least 24 hours. Contact training and competition wait until you are back to normal learning or work and have been cleared by a health professional.[^3]

For headache, simple pain relief such as paracetamol (acetaminophen), taken as directed on the label, can help. The NHS advises against aspirin after a head injury because it could make bleeding more likely.[^2] Someone should stay with the injured person for the first 24 hours, and alcohol and driving are off limits until you have recovered.[^2]

Most people feel better within a couple of weeks.[^1] Symptoms that last longer than about four weeks deserve a follow-up assessment.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Seat belts, helmets for cycling and other wheeled or snow sports, and a fall-proofed home all reduce head injuries. Helmets protect against skull fractures and severe brain injury, but no helmet is concussion-proof.[^1]

In sport the rule is simple: if in doubt, sit them out.[^3] A second blow before the brain has recovered can cause more severe and longer-lasting problems.[^1] [Concussion: Signs, Recovery and When to Return](/articles/concussion-signs-and-recovery) walks through the steps.

## When to see a doctor

Anyone with a suspected concussion should be assessed by a health professional and should not return to play the same day.[^3]

After a head injury, call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department for any of the following:[^1][^2]

- a headache that keeps getting worse
- repeated vomiting
- a seizure or loss of consciousness
- increasing drowsiness or trouble waking
- growing confusion or unusual behavior
- weakness, numbness or slurred speech
- one pupil larger than the other
- clear fluid from the nose or ears

Get checked urgently, too, if the injured person takes blood thinners.[^2]
