## What it is

Shingles is a painful rash caused by the varicella-zoster virus, the same virus that causes chickenpox. After chickenpox, the virus stays dormant in nerve cells for life. Years or decades later it can reactivate and travel along a nerve to the skin.[^1]

About 1 in 3 people in the United States develop shingles in their lifetime, and the risk rises with age.[^1]

## Symptoms

The first sign is often pain, burning, tingling or itching in one patch of skin, sometimes several days before anything is visible.[^1] A rash then appears as a band or cluster on one side of the body or face, most often around the chest or waist. It forms fluid-filled blisters that scab over in 7 to 10 days and clear within two to four weeks.[^1] The rash looks red on lighter skin and can be harder to see on brown and Black skin.[^2]

The most common complication is postherpetic neuralgia: nerve pain that lingers for months, occasionally years, after the rash has healed. It affects roughly 10% to 18% of people who get shingles and becomes more likely with age.[^1] Shingles in or around the eye can damage vision.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Anyone who has had chickenpox can get shingles. The risk is higher from about age 50, and in people whose immune system is weakened by illness, such as cancer or HIV, or by medicines such as steroids, chemotherapy or drugs given after a transplant.[^1][^4]

You cannot catch shingles from someone else. But fluid from the blisters can give chickenpox to a person who has never had chickenpox or the chickenpox vaccine. Until the rash has crusted over, keep it covered and avoid contact with pregnant women who are not immune to chickenpox, premature babies and people with weakened immunity.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Doctors usually recognize shingles from the one-sided pattern of the rash and the pain that goes with it. When the picture is unclear, fluid from a blister or a scraping of the rash can be sent to a laboratory for testing.[^5]

## Treatment

Antiviral medicines (acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir) can shorten the illness and make it less severe. They work best when started as soon as possible after the rash appears, ideally within three days.[^1][^2]

Pain is managed with over-the-counter pain relievers used as directed on the label or, when it is severe, with prescription medicines. Cool compresses, calamine lotion and loose clothing can ease discomfort.[^1][^2] Nerve pain that persists after the rash is treated with medicines used for other kinds of nerve pain, including certain antidepressants and anti-seizure drugs, and with numbing skin patches.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

Vaccination is the best protection. The recombinant shingles vaccine, given as two doses, is more than 90% effective at preventing shingles in adults aged 50 and older with healthy immune systems.[^3]

Who is offered it depends on where you live. US guidance recommends it for all adults from age 50 and for younger adults with weakened immunity, while some other countries start routine vaccination at older ages.[^2][^3] Check your national schedule; our [guide to adult vaccines](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) explains how.

If you have shingles, keep the rash clean, dry and covered, avoid scratching or picking at the blisters, and wash your hands often.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor as soon as you suspect shingles, ideally within three days of the rash appearing, because antivirals work best early.[^2]

Get urgent, same-day care if the rash is on your face, near your eye or on the tip of your nose; if you have eye pain, redness or vision changes; if you are pregnant or have a weakened immune system; or if the rash is widespread.[^2] Call your local emergency number for a severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, weakness of the face or sudden hearing loss.
