## What it is

Atrial fibrillation (AFib or AF) is the most common sustained heart rhythm problem.[^3] Instead of beating steadily, the heart's two upper chambers (the atria) quiver chaotically, so the lower chambers beat irregularly and often too fast.[^1]

AF can come in episodes that stop on their own or can be continuous.[^1] It is rarely an immediate threat to life. It matters because blood can pool in the quivering atria and form clots, and a clot that travels to the brain causes a [stroke](/conditions/stroke). AF raises the risk of stroke about fivefold.[^2] Over time it can also weaken the heart and lead to [heart failure](/conditions/heart-failure).[^1]

## Symptoms

- An irregular, often fast heartbeat, or a fluttering or pounding in the chest.
- Tiredness and getting worn out easily during exercise.
- Shortness of breath.
- Dizziness or lightheadedness.
- Chest discomfort.[^1][^4]

Some people feel nothing, and AF is found at a routine check or only after a stroke.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

AF becomes more common with age. Other risk factors include [high blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure), coronary artery disease, heart valve problems and heart failure, obesity, diabetes, [sleep apnea](/conditions/sleep-apnea), an overactive thyroid, heavy drinking and a family history.[^1][^3]

## Diagnosis

An irregular pulse is a clue, but AF has to be confirmed on an electrocardiogram (ECG).[^3] Because episodes come and go, you may be asked to wear a portable monitor for a day to several weeks.[^1] An echocardiogram checks the heart's structure, and blood tests look for triggers such as thyroid disease.[^1]

Smartwatches can flag a possibly irregular rhythm, but an alert is not a diagnosis and needs confirming by a clinician. [Resting Heart Rate and HRV](/articles/resting-heart-rate-and-hrv) explains what wearables can and cannot tell you.

## Treatment

The 2023 US guideline sets out three aims:[^3]

- **Preventing stroke.** Doctors estimate your stroke risk with a score such as CHA2DS2-VASc and, if it is high enough, prescribe an anticoagulant ("blood thinner"). Direct oral anticoagulants, such as apixaban or rivaroxaban, are preferred over warfarin for most people. Aspirin is not recommended as a substitute.
- **Controlling the heart rate** with medicines such as beta blockers or certain calcium channel blockers.
- **Restoring a normal rhythm** where appropriate, using cardioversion (a controlled electric shock), antiarrhythmic medicines or catheter ablation, which destroys the small areas of heart tissue that trigger AF.

The same guideline treats lifestyle and risk-factor management as a core part of care, not an optional extra.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Losing excess weight, exercising regularly, cutting back on alcohol, not smoking and treating high blood pressure and sleep apnea all reduce how often AF occurs and how severe it is.[^3]

If you are prescribed an anticoagulant, take it every day. Missed doses leave you unprotected against stroke, and you should not stop it without talking to your doctor, even if your heartbeat feels normal again.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you notice an irregular pulse or palpitations that keep coming back, or if a wearable repeatedly reports an irregular rhythm.[^4]

**Call your local emergency number** for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, or any sign of stroke: face drooping, arm weakness or slurred speech.[^2][^4]
