## What it is

Insulin is the hormone that moves glucose (sugar) out of your blood and into your cells. In type 2 diabetes, cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually cannot make enough to compensate, so blood glucose stays too high.[^2] Over years, that damages blood vessels and nerves, raising the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, vision loss and foot problems.[^1]

It is by far the most common form of diabetes: more than 95% of people with diabetes have type 2. The number of people living with diabetes worldwide rose from 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 2022.[^1]

## Symptoms

Symptoms creep in slowly, and many people have none for years. When they appear, they include increased thirst, passing urine more often, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing sores, repeated infections and numbness or tingling in the feet or hands.[^2] Because it can be silent, type 2 diabetes is often picked up on a routine blood test, sometimes after complications have already started.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Genes and environment both play a part. Risk rises with age and is higher if you have overweight or obesity, have a parent or sibling with diabetes, are not physically active, or have had prediabetes or diabetes during pregnancy (gestational diabetes).[^2] It is also more common in some ethnic groups, including people of South Asian, Black African, Hispanic and Native American descent.[^2][^6]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by blood test. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria are:[^3]

| Test | Diabetes if |
| --- | --- |
| HbA1c (average glucose over 2 to 3 months) | 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or higher |
| Fasting plasma glucose | 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher |
| 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test | 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher |
| Random glucose, with classic symptoms | 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher |

If you have no clear symptoms, the result is confirmed with a second test.[^3] [Blood Sugar Basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explains what each test measures.

## Treatment

Treatment aims to keep glucose, blood pressure and cholesterol in a range that protects your heart, kidneys, eyes and nerves. It starts with support around food, physical activity and weight, and most people also need medication.[^6]

Metformin has long been the usual first medicine. Others include SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists (and the related drug tirzepatide), DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, pioglitazone and insulin. The ADA advises matching the choice to the person: for people with heart disease, heart failure or chronic kidney disease, it recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor or a GLP-1 receptor agonist with proven benefit for those organs.[^4]

Type 2 diabetes tends to progress, so needing more treatment over time is expected, not a personal failure. For some people, substantial weight loss soon after diagnosis returns glucose to the non-diabetes range without medication, known as remission. In the UK DiRECT trial, 46% of people offered an intensive weight-management program were in remission after a year, compared with 4% receiving usual care.[^7]

## Prevention and self-care

Eating well, regular physical activity, keeping a healthy weight and not smoking help prevent or delay type 2 diabetes.[^1] If your glucose is already creeping up, see [prediabetes](/conditions/prediabetes), the stage where structured lifestyle programs have the strongest evidence.

If you have diabetes, go to your regular checks: HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney tests, eye screening and foot examinations. They catch complications early, when they are most treatable.[^6]

## When to see a doctor

Ask for a blood test if you have symptoms. If you have none, the US Preventive Services Task Force (2021) recommends screening adults aged 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity, and the ADA recommends testing all adults from age 35.[^5][^3]

Call your local emergency number if someone with diabetes has very high glucose with vomiting, fast breathing, confusion or drowsiness.[^8] Severe low blood sugar (confusion, a seizure or passing out), which can happen with insulin or sulfonylureas, is also an emergency.[^4]
