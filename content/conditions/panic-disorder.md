## What it is

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes and brings strong physical symptoms, often with no obvious danger or trigger.[^1] Plenty of people have one at some point and never develop a disorder.[^1]

Panic disorder means the attacks are repeated and unexpected, and are followed by persistent worry about having another, or by changes in behavior to avoid one.[^1][^2] It usually starts in young adulthood and is more common in women than in men.[^3]

## Symptoms

During an attack you may have a pounding or racing heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, tingling, nausea, a sense of unreality, and a fear of losing control or dying.[^1] Most attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes.[^4]

A panic attack is frightening but not physically dangerous, although it can feel like a heart attack.[^4] Over time, fear of the next attack can become the bigger problem. Some people start to avoid crowds, public transport or being far from home, a pattern called agoraphobia.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not fully understood. Panic disorder can run in families, and both genes and life experience appear to contribute.[^1][^3] Major stress, a history of trauma or other adverse experiences, and an anxious temperament raise risk.[^1][^5]

## Diagnosis

Because the symptoms overlap with heart, lung and thyroid problems, a doctor will usually examine you and may arrange tests, such as a heart tracing (ECG) or blood tests, before diagnosing panic disorder.[^3] The diagnosis itself rests on your description of the attacks and their effect on your life.

## Treatment

Panic disorder responds well to treatment. NICE recommends cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as the psychological treatment, and an SSRI antidepressant as the first-choice medicine. Either can work, and the choice depends on what you prefer.[^2]

CBT for panic teaches you how the cycle of fear works. It then uses gradual, planned exposure to the bodily sensations and situations you fear, so that they lose their power.[^1]

NICE advises against benzodiazepines (sedatives) for panic disorder, because they are linked with poorer long-term outcomes.[^2] Practice differs between countries, and in the United States they are sometimes prescribed for short periods.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

During an attack, it helps to remind yourself that it will pass, to breathe slowly, and to stay where you are if it is safe to do so. Leaving in a hurry tends to strengthen the fear.[^4]

Between attacks, regular exercise and regular meals help, and so does cutting back on caffeine, alcohol and smoking, all of which can make panic attacks worse.[^4] These steps support treatment. They do not replace it. [Anxiety: What It Is, What It Isn't and What Helps](/articles/understanding-anxiety) explains how the different anxiety disorders fit together.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have had repeated panic attacks, if you are worried about the next one, or if you have started avoiding places or activities because of them.

Symptoms alone cannot reliably tell a panic attack from a heart problem. Call your local emergency number for chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness or fainting, especially if it is the first time or it feels different from your usual attacks. [Heart Attack and Stroke: Warning Signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs) lists what to look for.

If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^6]
