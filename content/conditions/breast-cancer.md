## What it is

Breast cancer starts when cells in the breast grow out of control, most often in the milk ducts or the milk-producing lobules. It can be non-invasive, meaning confined to the ducts (ductal carcinoma in situ, or DCIS), or invasive, meaning it has grown into surrounding tissue and can spread to lymph nodes and other organs.[^2][^3]

It is the most common cancer in women worldwide. In 2022, 2.3 million women were diagnosed and 670,000 died.[^1] In the United States, about 1 in 8 women develops breast cancer during her life.[^2] Men account for about 0.5% to 1% of cases.[^1]

## Symptoms

Early breast cancer often causes no symptoms.[^4] Signs to look for are:[^1][^4]

- a new lump or thickening in the breast or armpit, often painless
- a change in the size, shape or appearance of a breast
- dimpling, redness, pitting or other changes in the skin
- a change in how the nipple or the skin around it looks
- unusual or bloody discharge from the nipple

Most breast lumps are not cancer, but every new lump should be checked.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

There is no single cause. Being female and getting older are the strongest risk factors, and about half of breast cancers develop in women with no identifiable risk factor other than their sex and being over 40.[^1] Other risk factors include:[^1][^2]

- inherited gene changes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 (about 5% to 10% of breast cancers are hereditary), or a family history of breast or ovarian cancer
- dense breast tissue
- starting periods early, reaching menopause late, and having a first child later in life or not at all
- combined hormone therapy after menopause
- radiation treatment to the chest at a young age
- alcohol, excess weight after menopause and physical inactivity

## Diagnosis

Assessment combines a breast examination, imaging (a mammogram, ultrasound and sometimes MRI) and a biopsy, in which a needle removes a small sample of tissue. Only a biopsy can confirm cancer.[^2][^3] The sample is tested for hormone receptors and a protein called HER2, which guide treatment, and the cancer is staged by its size and whether it has spread.[^3]

Screening looks for cancer before symptoms appear, and programs differ. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends a mammogram every two years from age 40 to 74, while the NHS invites women every three years from 50 to 71.[^5][^6] [Breast and Cervical Screening](/articles/breast-and-cervical-screening) explains the trade-offs.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the type and stage of the cancer, its receptor status and your preferences. Most people have a combination of:[^2][^3]

- **Surgery** to remove the tumor (lumpectomy) or the whole breast (mastectomy), usually with a check of the lymph nodes in the armpit.
- **Radiotherapy,** commonly after a lumpectomy.
- **Chemotherapy,** before or after surgery.
- **Hormone-blocking therapy,** such as tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, for hormone receptor-positive cancers, often taken for several years.
- **Targeted therapy or immunotherapy** for cancers with particular features, such as HER2-positive disease.

In US data, the five-year relative survival rate is about 99% for cancer still confined to the breast.[^2] Access to care matters too: five-year survival exceeds 90% in high-income countries, compared with 66% in India and 40% in South Africa.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

You cannot change your age or your genes, but limiting alcohol, staying physically active and keeping a healthy weight after menopause all lower risk.[^1][^2] People at high risk, such as carriers of BRCA gene changes, can discuss extra screening, risk-reducing medicines or surgery with a specialist.[^2]

Know how your breasts normally look and feel so that you notice changes, and take up screening when invited.[^4]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor promptly about any new lump in the breast or armpit, a change in the shape or skin of a breast, or a change in the nipple, including discharge.[^1] Do this even if a recent mammogram was normal.
