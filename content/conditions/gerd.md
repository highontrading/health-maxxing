## What it is

A ring of muscle at the bottom of your esophagus (food pipe) normally closes once food has passed into the stomach. When it relaxes at the wrong moment or is weak, stomach contents flow back up. That is reflux, and an occasional episode is normal.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is the diagnosis when reflux causes repeated, troublesome symptoms or damages the esophagus. About 20% of people in the US have it.[^1]

## Symptoms

The two classic symptoms are heartburn, a burning feeling behind the breastbone that tends to come on after meals or when you lie down, and regurgitation, when sour or bitter fluid rises into your throat or mouth.[^2] Other symptoms include nausea, chest pain and pain or difficulty when swallowing. Reflux can also show up as a long-lasting cough, hoarseness or a sore throat.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

GERD is more likely if you have overweight or obesity, are pregnant, smoke or are regularly exposed to smoke. A hiatal hernia, where the top of the stomach pushes up through the diaphragm, also makes reflux easier.[^1]

Some medicines can cause or worsen it, including certain asthma medicines, calcium channel blockers for blood pressure, sedatives, some antidepressants and anti-inflammatory painkillers.[^1] Food triggers vary from person to person.

## Diagnosis

If you have classic heartburn and regurgitation and no warning signs, tests are usually not needed at first. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends an eight-week trial of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) once a day, then trying to stop it if symptoms have settled.[^2]

An endoscopy, in which a thin camera examines the esophagus and stomach, is advised first if you have warning signs such as trouble swallowing, weight loss or bleeding. It is also advised if symptoms do not respond to treatment or return when it stops. If doubt remains, a small probe can measure acid in the esophagus over a day or more.[^2]

## Treatment

The ACG guideline supports these lifestyle measures: losing weight if you have overweight, not eating within two to three hours of bedtime, raising the head of the bed if symptoms come at night, not smoking and avoiding your own trigger foods.[^2]

Three kinds of medicine are used. Antacids neutralize acid for quick, brief relief. H2 blockers reduce acid production. PPIs reduce it more strongly and are the most effective treatment, and they work best taken 30 to 60 minutes before a meal.[^1][^2]

Observational studies have linked long-term PPI use to problems such as fractures, kidney disease and infections. The ACG's reading is that these studies do not establish cause and effect, and that for people who need a PPI the benefits outweigh the possible risks. It still advises the lowest dose that controls symptoms.[^2]

Surgery to tighten the valve (fundoplication) is an option for some people whose symptoms are not controlled or who prefer not to take long-term medicine.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

The lifestyle measures above are also the best prevention. If you use pharmacy remedies, take them as directed on the label. [Acid Reflux and Heartburn](/articles/acid-reflux-and-heartburn) goes through the practical fixes.

Long-standing, untreated GERD can inflame and narrow the esophagus. In some people it changes the lining (Barrett's esophagus), which carries a small increased risk of esophageal cancer.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have heartburn most days for three weeks or more, if pharmacy medicines are not helping, or if food sticks when you swallow, you keep vomiting or you are losing weight without trying.[^3]

Chest pain is not always heartburn. Call your local emergency number for chest pain or pressure, especially with breathlessness, sweating or pain spreading to the arm or jaw. See [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs). Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools also needs emergency care.[^1]
