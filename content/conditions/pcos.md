## What it is

Polycystic ovary syndrome is a common hormonal and metabolic condition. The WHO estimates that it affects 10% to 13% of women of reproductive age, and that up to 70% of those who have it do not know.[^1]

Its core features are irregular or absent ovulation, raised levels of androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which women also make in small amounts) and ovaries that contain many small follicles. These are egg sacs that have stalled in development, not true cysts.[^4]

The name is changing. In May 2026 an international consensus published in The Lancet renamed the condition polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS.[^3] Only the name has changed, and "PCOS" will stay on medical records for some time.

## Symptoms

Symptoms vary from person to person:[^1][^4]

- irregular, infrequent or absent periods
- excess hair on the face or body
- acne or oily skin
- thinning hair on the scalp
- weight gain, especially around the middle
- difficulty getting pregnant

PCOS is a leading cause of infertility, although ovulation problems can often be treated.[^1][^2]

## Causes and risk factors

The root cause is unknown, but PCOS runs in families.[^1] Insulin resistance, raised androgen levels and an irregular cycle appear to work together.[^4] Insulin resistance means the body responds poorly to insulin and makes more of it, which can lead to type 2 diabetes; see [Insulin Resistance and Prediabetes](/articles/insulin-resistance-and-prediabetes). High androgen levels can stop the ovaries releasing an egg, and cause unwanted hair growth and acne.[^4]

Whatever your age or weight, PCOS raises the risk of type 2 diabetes. It is also linked with high blood pressure, high cholesterol, heart disease, anxiety and depression.[^1][^2]

## Diagnosis

No single test confirms PCOS. Under the 2023 international guideline, an adult needs two of these three features, once other causes are ruled out:[^2]

1. Irregular ovulation, which shows up as irregular or absent periods.
2. Signs of high androgens, either visible (excess hair, severe acne) or on a blood test.
3. Polycystic-looking ovaries on ultrasound or, as an alternative, a raised blood level of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH).

If the first two are present, no scan or AMH test is needed. In teenagers, both of the first two are required, and ultrasound and AMH are not used because they are unreliable at that age.[^2] Blood tests also rule out other hormone conditions with similar symptoms.[^5]

## Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be treated.[^1] The plan depends on what matters most to you:[^2]

- **Healthy eating and regular physical activity** for everyone, at any weight. No specific diet has been shown to beat another.
- **The combined contraceptive pill,** first-line for irregular cycles, acne and excess hair.
- **Metformin** for metabolic health.
- **Anti-androgens and hair removal** for persistent excess hair. Anti-androgens require reliable contraception.
- **Letrozole,** the first-choice medicine to trigger ovulation, with other medicines and IVF as further options.

The guideline also recommends yearly blood pressure checks, blood sugar tests and screening for anxiety and depression.[^2] Our [PCOS explainer](/articles/pcos-explained) covers each option in depth.

## Prevention and self-care

PCOS cannot be prevented. For women who are overweight, even a small amount of weight loss can make periods more regular and improve insulin and cholesterol levels.[^4]

Report long gaps between periods or unusual bleeding. Before menopause, PCOS raises the risk of cancer of the uterine lining two- to six-fold, though the absolute risk stays low.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your periods are irregular, very infrequent or have stopped, if you notice new or increasing hair growth on your face or body, persistent acne or thinning scalp hair, or if you have been trying to conceive for 12 months without success (6 months if you are 35 or older).

Mention low mood or anxiety too; both are treatable.[^2] Hair growth that comes on very quickly, or a deepening voice, needs prompt assessment because it can point to a different cause.[^2]
