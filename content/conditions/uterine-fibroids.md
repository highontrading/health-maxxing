## What it is

Fibroids are non-cancerous growths of muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in or around the uterus. They can be as small as a pea or as large as a melon, and you can have one or several.[^1]

They are very common. About 2 in 3 women develop at least one fibroid at some point, most often between the ages of 30 and 50.[^1] Fibroids are more common in Black women, and tend to appear earlier and cause more severe symptoms.[^3] They are almost never cancerous.[^5]

Doctors describe fibroids by where they grow: within the muscle wall of the uterus, on its outer surface, or just under the lining, bulging into the cavity.[^1]

## Symptoms

Many fibroids cause no symptoms and are found by chance. The NHS estimates that only about 1 in 3 women with fibroids has symptoms.[^1] When they occur, they depend on the size and position of the fibroids:[^1][^2]

- heavy or long-lasting periods, which can lead to iron deficiency anemia
- pressure, fullness or pain in the pelvis or lower abdomen
- needing to urinate often, or difficulty emptying the bladder
- constipation
- lower back pain
- pain or discomfort during sex

Some fibroids, particularly those that distort the cavity of the uterus, can make it harder to become pregnant or cause problems during pregnancy.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is unknown, but fibroids depend on the hormone estrogen. They tend to grow during the reproductive years and shrink after menopause.[^1] The risk is higher with:[^1][^3]

- age, up to menopause
- African or African-Caribbean ancestry
- a family history of fibroids
- obesity
- starting periods at an early age

Having had children lowers the risk.[^1]

## Diagnosis

A doctor may feel an enlarged or irregular uterus during a pelvic examination. An ultrasound scan, through the abdomen or the vagina, is the main test used to confirm fibroids.[^1][^4] An MRI scan or a hysteroscopy, in which a thin camera is passed through the cervix, may be used to plan treatment. A blood count checks for anemia if your periods are heavy.[^4]

## Treatment

Fibroids that cause no problems do not need treatment.[^1] Otherwise, the choice depends on your symptoms, the size and position of the fibroids, and whether you want to become pregnant in the future:[^1][^2][^4]

- **Medicines for heavy bleeding:** tranexamic acid, anti-inflammatory painkillers, a hormone-releasing IUD or other hormonal contraceptives.
- **Medicines that shrink fibroids,** called GnRH agonists and antagonists, usually short term or before surgery.
- **Uterine artery embolization,** a procedure that blocks the blood supply to the fibroids.
- **Myomectomy,** surgery that removes the fibroids and keeps the uterus, often chosen by people who want a future pregnancy.
- **Hysterectomy,** removal of the uterus, the only treatment that rules out fibroids coming back.

After treatments that keep the uterus, new fibroids can grow.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no proven way to prevent fibroids. If your periods are heavy, keep a record of how often you change protection and ask about a blood test for [iron deficiency anemia](/conditions/iron-deficiency-anemia). [Period Pain and Heavy Periods](/articles/period-pain-and-heavy-periods) explains what counts as heavy and how it is treated.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have heavy, long or painful periods, bleeding between periods, pelvic pain or pressure, urinary problems, tiredness or breathlessness that could be anemia, or difficulty getting pregnant.[^1][^2] Any bleeding after menopause should be checked promptly.

Get urgent care if you soak through a pad or tampon every hour for several hours, feel dizzy or faint with heavy bleeding, or have sudden, severe pelvic pain.
