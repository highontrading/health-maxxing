## What it is

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infection caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It most often affects the lungs but can involve almost any part of the body, including the lymph nodes, bones and brain.[^1][^2]

There are two states. In TB infection (also called latent TB), the bacteria are in your body but inactive: you feel well and cannot pass it on. In TB disease, the bacteria multiply and make you ill.[^2] About a quarter of the world's population is thought to have been infected, and roughly 5 to 10% of those infected will develop TB disease at some point.[^1]

TB is curable and preventable. Even so, more than 10 million people fall ill with it each year and over a million die.[^1]

## Symptoms

TB disease in the lungs develops gradually, over weeks:[^1][^3]

- A cough that lasts more than three weeks, sometimes bringing up blood.
- Chest pain.
- Fever and drenching night sweats.
- Unintended weight loss and loss of appetite.
- Tiredness and weakness.

TB elsewhere in the body causes symptoms there, such as swollen glands, aching joints or a persistent headache.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

TB spreads through the air when someone with TB disease of the lungs coughs, sneezes or speaks. Catching it usually takes prolonged close contact, such as living in the same household. It does not spread by shaking hands or sharing food and drink.[^2]

Infection is more likely to progress to disease in people with HIV, diabetes or undernutrition, and in people who smoke or drink heavily.[^1] Medicines that suppress the immune system and very young age also raise the risk.[^2][^4]

## Diagnosis

- **TB disease** is diagnosed with a chest X-ray and tests on phlegm samples. The WHO recommends rapid molecular tests as the first test, because they give fast results and can detect drug resistance.[^1]
- **TB infection** is detected with a skin test or a blood test. Neither can tell inactive infection from active disease, so a positive result leads to further checks.[^2]

## Treatment

TB disease is treated with a combination of antibiotics. The standard course for TB that responds to the usual medicines lasts six months: four antibiotics for two months, then two of them for four more.[^5] Shorter four-month courses are an option for some people.[^1]

Taking every dose for the full course is essential. Stopping early, even when you feel better, lets the bacteria return and can make them resistant to treatment.[^1] Drug-resistant TB is harder to treat and needs different medicines.[^1]

People with TB infection who are at higher risk of becoming ill may be offered a shorter preventive course of one or two antibiotics.[^1][^2] [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help) explains why resistance matters.

## Prevention and self-care

The BCG vaccine is given to babies and young children in countries where TB is common. It protects children against the most severe forms of TB, but gives adults limited protection against TB in the lungs.[^1][^2]

If you have TB disease of the lungs, you are usually no longer infectious after about two weeks of treatment. Until your care team confirms that, stay home, cover your mouth when you cough and keep rooms well ventilated.[^2][^3]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have a cough that has lasted more than three weeks, cough up blood, or have unexplained fevers, night sweats or weight loss.[^3] Ask about testing if you have spent a lot of time with someone who has TB disease, even if you feel well.[^2]

**Seek emergency care** if you cough up a large amount of blood or have severe difficulty breathing. Do the same for a severe headache with a stiff neck, confusion, drowsiness or a seizure, which can mean TB has spread to the brain.[^3]
