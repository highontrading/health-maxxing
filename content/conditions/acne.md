## What it is

Acne is a skin condition in which hair follicles (pores) become plugged with oil and dead skin cells. The plugs show up as whiteheads and blackheads. When the follicle becomes inflamed, they turn into tender bumps, pus-filled pimples or deep, painful lumps.[^2] It mostly affects the face, chest, shoulders and back, where oil glands are most numerous.

It is extremely common: the NHS estimates that about 95% of people aged 11 to 30 are affected to some extent. It can also persist or first appear in adulthood, particularly in women.[^3]

## Symptoms

Spots range from blackheads and whiteheads to inflamed pimples and, in severe acne, large nodules and cysts that can leave scars.[^2] On brown and Black skin, healed spots often leave dark marks that can take months to fade.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Acne starts when a follicle makes too much oil (sebum), dead skin cells build up and block the pore, and bacteria that normally live on the skin multiply inside it, triggering inflammation.[^2]

Hormones called androgens, which rise at puberty, enlarge the oil glands, which is why acne peaks in the teenage years. It also runs in families, can flare around periods or during pregnancy, and can be set off by some medicines (including steroids, lithium and some epilepsy drugs), by greasy cosmetics, and by pressure or friction from helmets, straps or backpacks.[^2][^3]

Acne is not caused by dirty skin, and washing more often will not clear it.[^3] The role of food is less settled. Some studies link high-glycemic diets (lots of sugar and refined starch) and possibly milk with worse acne, but the American Academy of Dermatology's 2024 guideline found too little evidence to recommend any particular diet.[^4]

## Diagnosis

A doctor diagnoses acne by looking at your skin, and grades it as mild, moderate or severe from the type and number of spots and any scarring.[^3] Tests are rarely needed. If acne comes with irregular periods or excess facial or body hair, blood tests may check for a hormonal condition such as [polycystic ovary syndrome](/conditions/pcos).[^3]

## Treatment

Treatment depends on severity and takes patience: it can take up to three months to show its effect.[^3] Options that doctors and pharmacists use include:[^4][^5]

- **Treatments applied to the skin:** benzoyl peroxide, retinoids such as adapalene and tretinoin, azelaic acid, salicylic acid and antibiotic gels or lotions. Guidelines favor combining products that work in different ways, and pairing any antibiotic with benzoyl peroxide to limit bacterial resistance.
- **Antibiotic tablets** such as doxycycline for moderate to severe acne, kept to the shortest course that works.
- **Hormonal treatments** for women, including combined contraceptive pills and spironolactone.
- **Isotretinoin,** a tablet for severe or scarring acne, or acne that has not responded to other treatment. It causes serious birth defects, so it is prescribed under strict pregnancy-prevention rules and monitoring.

Some of these are sold without a prescription. [Acne: What Causes It and What Clears It](/articles/acne-causes-and-treatments) explains how to use them.

## Prevention and self-care

You cannot always prevent acne, but you can avoid making it worse:[^1]

- Wash gently twice a day and after sweating, with a mild cleanser and your fingertips rather than a scrub.
- Choose products labeled non-comedogenic, meaning they do not clog pores.
- Do not squeeze or pick. It slows healing and raises the risk of scars.
- Protect your skin from the sun, since several acne treatments make it more sensitive to sunlight.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or dermatologist if pharmacy products have not helped after two to three months, if you have painful lumps under the skin, if spots are leaving scars or dark marks, or if acne is affecting your mood or confidence.[^3] Acne that appears suddenly in adulthood with irregular periods or new hair growth also deserves a check.

Treating acne early is the best way to prevent permanent scarring.[^1]
