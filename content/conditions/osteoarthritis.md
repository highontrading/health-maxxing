## What it is

Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis.[^4] It affects the whole joint: the smooth cartilage that cushions the ends of the bones breaks down, the bone underneath changes shape, and the joint lining can become inflamed.[^4] The knees, hips and hands are affected most often.[^1]

In 2019 about 528 million people worldwide were living with osteoarthritis, more than double the number in 1990. Roughly 73% of them were over 55, and 60% were women.[^1]

## Symptoms

The main symptom is joint pain that gets worse with activity and settles with rest. Stiffness after sitting, or first thing in the morning, is typical and usually eases within about 30 minutes.[^2][^5] Other signs are swelling, a grating or crackling sensation, reduced movement and, in the hands, bony lumps on the finger joints.[^4]

Symptoms usually develop slowly and vary from day to day.[^5]

## Causes and risk factors

Osteoarthritis is not simply "wear and tear", and regular exercise is good for affected joints, not harmful.[^2] Risk rises with age and is higher in women, in people carrying excess weight, after a joint injury such as a torn ligament or cartilage, and with repeated heavy stress on a joint.[^1][^4] It also runs in families.[^5]

## Diagnosis

Osteoarthritis can usually be diagnosed without tests. NICE says a clinical diagnosis is appropriate in someone aged 45 or over who has activity-related joint pain and either no morning stiffness or morning stiffness that lasts no longer than 30 minutes.[^2]

X-rays and blood tests are not routinely needed. They help when symptoms are unusual or when another condition, such as [rheumatoid arthritis](/conditions/rheumatoid-arthritis) or [gout](/conditions/gout), is possible.[^2]

## Treatment

There is no cure yet, but symptoms can be managed well. Guidelines agree on the foundations:[^2][^3]

- **Exercise.** Muscle strengthening and aerobic activity, tailored to you, are the core treatment. Pain may flare a little at first and usually improves as you keep going.
- **Weight loss** if you are overweight. The more you lose, the greater the benefit for knee and hip symptoms.
- **Information and support** so that you can manage the condition yourself.

Medicines are add-ons, used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. NICE recommends anti-inflammatory gels (topical NSAIDs) first for knee osteoarthritis, with anti-inflammatory tablets if needed. It advises against routinely offering paracetamol (acetaminophen) or weak opioids, and against strong opioids.[^2] A steroid injection into the joint can give short-term relief.[^2][^3]

Both NICE and the American College of Rheumatology advise against glucosamine, and NICE also advises against hyaluronan injections.[^2][^3]

Walking aids can help. Joint replacement is considered when symptoms seriously affect quality of life and other treatments have not worked well enough.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Staying active, keeping a healthy weight and avoiding serious joint injuries all lower your risk.[^1] If you already have osteoarthritis, the same habits are the treatment, and supportive footwear and pacing your activities can ease bad days.[^5]

For more, see [Arthritis: Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid, Explained](/articles/arthritis-osteo-vs-rheumatoid) and [knee pain exercises](/articles/knee-pain-causes-and-exercises).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if joint pain or stiffness lasts more than a few weeks or limits what you can do.

Get urgent medical help if a joint suddenly becomes hot, swollen and very painful, particularly if you also have a fever or feel unwell. This can mean an infection inside the joint, which needs immediate treatment.[^6] Do the same if you cannot put weight on a leg after a fall.
