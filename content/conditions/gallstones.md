## What it is

Your gallbladder is a small pouch under the liver that stores bile, a fluid that helps you digest fat. Gallstones are hard, pebble-like pieces that form when substances in bile, usually cholesterol or bilirubin, harden. They can be as small as a grain of sand or as large as a golf ball.[^1]

They are very common, affecting about 10% to 15% of people in the US.[^1] Most never cause symptoms and need no treatment.[^2]

## Symptoms

Trouble starts when a stone blocks one of the bile ducts. The result is a gallbladder attack, also called biliary colic: steady, often severe pain in the upper right or middle of the belly, sometimes spreading to the right shoulder or back. Attacks often follow a heavy meal, tend to come in the evening or at night and can last several hours.[^1] Nausea and vomiting are common.[^4]

If the blockage persists, complications can follow: inflammation of the gallbladder, infection of the bile ducts, jaundice or inflammation of the pancreas (pancreatitis).[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Stones form when bile contains too much cholesterol or bilirubin, or when the gallbladder does not empty properly.[^1]

Gallstones are more common in women, in people over 40 and in those with a family history. Other risk factors are obesity, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, diabetes, medicines that contain estrogen, liver cirrhosis, Crohn's disease and blood conditions such as sickle cell disease. In the US they are particularly common in Native American and Mexican American people.[^1]

## Diagnosis

An ultrasound scan of the abdomen is the best test for finding gallstones.[^1] Blood tests look for signs of infection, a blocked duct or an inflamed pancreas. If a stone in the bile duct is suspected but not seen on ultrasound, an MRI scan of the ducts or an ultrasound done through an endoscope may be used.[^3]

Many gallstones are found by chance on a scan done for another reason.[^2]

## Treatment

Silent stones are generally left alone. NICE advises reassuring people whose stones cause no symptoms that they do not need treatment unless symptoms develop.[^3]

For stones that cause attacks or complications, the usual treatment is surgery to remove the gallbladder (cholecystectomy), most often by keyhole surgery. It is one of the most common operations performed on adults, and you can live normally without a gallbladder, because bile then flows straight from the liver into the intestine.[^1]

Stones stuck in the bile duct can be removed with a flexible tube passed through the mouth, a procedure called ERCP.[^1] Tablets that dissolve small cholesterol stones exist, but they take months or years to work and stones often return, so they are kept for people who cannot have surgery.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

You cannot fully prevent gallstones, but you can lower the odds. Keep to a healthy weight, and if you are losing weight, do it gradually, because rapid weight loss makes stones more likely. An eating pattern with plenty of fiber and healthy fats, and less sugar and refined carbohydrate, is also linked to lower risk.[^1] [Weight Loss That Lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts) covers a sustainable pace.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor after any suspected gallbladder attack, even if the pain has passed, because more attacks are likely to follow.[^1]

Get medical help straight away if belly pain lasts several hours, or comes with fever or chills, yellowing of the skin or eyes, tea-colored urine and pale stools, or repeated vomiting. These point to a blocked or infected duct or pancreatitis, which need urgent treatment.[^1] If you are unsure how serious something is, see [Emergency, Urgent or Wait?](/articles/emergency-or-not).
