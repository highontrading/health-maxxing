## What it is

Fatty liver disease means too much fat is stored in liver cells. This page covers the most common form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), in which liver fat goes with at least one metabolic risk factor, such as excess weight, raised blood sugar, high blood pressure or abnormal blood fats, and is not explained by heavy drinking. Until 2023 it was called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).[^3]

Roughly a quarter to a third of adults worldwide have it.[^2] In most, the fat does the liver little harm. In a minority it triggers inflammation and cell damage, called MASH (formerly NASH), which can scar the liver. Over years, scarring can progress to cirrhosis, liver failure or liver cancer.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

There are usually none. Some people feel tired or have a dull ache in the upper right side of the belly.[^1] Symptoms such as yellowing of the skin or eyes, itching and swelling of the legs or belly appear only once scarring is advanced.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

MASLD is closely tied to insulin resistance. It is most common in people with overweight or obesity (especially around the waist), type 2 diabetes, high triglycerides or high blood pressure, the same cluster that makes up [metabolic syndrome](/conditions/metabolic-syndrome).[^2] Polycystic ovary syndrome, sleep apnea and genes add to the risk, and it can also develop in people who are not overweight.[^2]

## Diagnosis

MASLD is often found by chance, through mildly raised liver enzymes on a blood test or a fatty-looking liver on an ultrasound scan. Normal liver blood tests do not rule it out.[^2]

The key question is how much scarring there is. Doctors start with a score called FIB-4, calculated from your age and routine blood results. If it suggests higher risk, the next step is a scan that measures liver stiffness (elastography) or a specialized blood test, and occasionally a biopsy. Other causes, such as alcohol, viral hepatitis and certain medicines, are checked for too.[^2]

## Treatment

The core treatment is improving metabolic health. US liver guidance says losing 3% to 5% of body weight reduces liver fat, while losses of 10% or more are generally needed to improve inflammation and scarring. A Mediterranean-style eating pattern and regular exercise help, and exercise benefits the liver even without weight loss.[^2]

Doctors also treat diabetes, cholesterol and blood pressure, because heart disease is the leading cause of death in people with MASLD. Statins are safe to use.[^2]

Medicines aimed at the liver itself are now arriving. In 2024 resmetirom became the first drug approved in the US for MASH with moderate to advanced scarring.[^6] In its phase 3 trial, MASH resolved after a year in about 26% to 30% of people taking it, compared with about 10% on placebo.[^5] The GLP-1 medicine semaglutide also improved MASH in a phase 3 trial published in 2025.[^7]

## Prevention and self-care

- Move regularly and aim for gradual, sustained weight loss if you have overweight. [Weight Loss That Lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts) and the [Mediterranean diet guide](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide) are good starting points.
- Go easy on alcohol, which adds to liver damage. The guidance advises avoiding it completely if you have significant scarring.[^2]
- Coffee appears to be on your side: drinking three or more cups a day is associated with less advanced liver disease, though this comes from observational studies.[^2]
- Talk to your doctor before taking herbal or dietary supplements, because some can harm the liver.[^4]

## When to see a doctor

If you have type 2 diabetes, obesity or metabolic syndrome, ask whether your liver has been assessed. The guidance recommends checking people at high risk for scarring.[^2] Follow up any abnormal liver blood test.

Yellowing skin or eyes, a swelling belly or swollen legs suggest advanced liver disease and need urgent medical review.[^1] Call your local emergency number for vomiting blood, black stools, or confusion and unusual drowsiness.[^8]
