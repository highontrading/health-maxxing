## What it is

Influenza is a contagious infection of the nose, throat and sometimes the lungs, caused by influenza viruses. Types A and B cause the seasonal epidemics that arrive each winter in temperate regions and can circulate all year in the tropics.[^1]

Flu is not a bad cold. The WHO estimates about a billion cases a year worldwide, 3 to 5 million of them severe, and 290,000 to 650,000 deaths from flu-related respiratory illness.[^1]

## Symptoms

Flu hits suddenly, usually about two days after exposure: fever or chills, a dry cough, sore throat, muscle aches, headache, a runny or stuffy nose and deep tiredness.[^1][^3] Not everyone gets a fever. Children may also have vomiting or diarrhea.[^3]

Most people recover from the fever and other symptoms within a week without medical treatment, though the cough can last two weeks or more.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Flu spreads mainly through droplets released when people who have it cough, sneeze or talk, and less often through contaminated hands and surfaces.[^1][^2] You can pass it on from about a day before your symptoms start, and you are most contagious in the first few days of illness.[^2]

Anyone can become seriously ill, but complications such as [pneumonia](/conditions/pneumonia) are more likely in:[^1][^2]

- adults aged 65 and older
- children under 5, especially those under 2
- pregnant women
- people with long-term conditions such as asthma, heart disease, diabetes or kidney disease
- people with a weakened immune system

## Diagnosis

During flu season, doctors often diagnose flu from symptoms alone. When a firm answer would change treatment, a swab from the nose or throat can be tested, often for COVID-19 at the same time.[^2] [Cold, Flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid) explains why symptoms alone cannot tell them apart.

## Treatment

Most healthy people need only rest, fluids and fever or pain relievers used as directed on the label.[^5] Do not give aspirin to children or teenagers with flu.[^4]

Prescription antiviral medicines, such as oseltamivir, can shorten the illness and lower the risk of complications. They work best when started within two days of the first symptoms, and are recommended promptly for people who are very ill or at higher risk.[^4] Antibiotics do not treat flu. They are used only if a bacterial infection develops on top of it.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

A yearly vaccine is the best protection, because flu viruses change from season to season.[^1] The WHO recommends annual vaccination for pregnant women, children aged 6 months to 5 years, older adults, people with chronic conditions and health workers. US guidance recommends it for almost everyone from 6 months of age.[^1][^2] Who is offered a vaccine varies by country, so check your national schedule or our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide).

Protection is partial and varies by season. The WHO notes that the vaccine can be less effective at preventing illness in older adults, but that it still makes the illness less severe and reduces complications and deaths.[^1]

Washing your hands, covering coughs and sneezes, and staying home when you are sick also limit the spread.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

Contact a doctor early, ideally within two days of symptoms starting, if you are pregnant, aged 65 or over, or have a long-term condition or weakened immune system, or if the person who is ill is a young child.[^4]

Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department for:[^3]

- difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
- persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen
- confusion, persistent dizziness or difficulty waking
- seizures
- not passing urine, or severe weakness
- fever or cough that improves and then returns or gets worse

In children, also act on fast or labored breathing, bluish lips or face, no urine for eight hours, or a fever above 40 °C (104 °F) that medicine does not bring down. Any fever in a baby under 12 weeks needs urgent assessment.[^3]
