## What it is

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue that runs along the sole of the foot, from the heel bone to the toes, and supports the arch. Plantar fasciitis is irritation of this band where it attaches to the heel. It is the most common cause of pain on the bottom of the heel.[^1]

## Symptoms

The hallmark is sharp pain under the heel with the first few steps after getting out of bed or after sitting for a while.[^1] It tends to ease as you move around, then return after long periods on your feet. Pain typically comes on after exercise, not during it.[^1][^2] You may also find it hard to raise your toes off the floor.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

Plantar fasciitis develops when the fascia is strained again and again beyond what it can tolerate. It is most common between the ages of 40 and 60.[^2] Things that raise the risk include:[^1][^2]

- a recent increase in walking, running or standing
- exercising on hard surfaces
- tight calf muscles
- carrying excess weight
- a very high arch
- shoes with poor cushioning or support

Many people with heel pain turn out to have a bony heel spur on X-ray, but the spur is rarely the cause of the pain. Only about 1 in 20 people with a heel spur have foot pain.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Doctors diagnose plantar fasciitis from your description and an examination of the foot. Tests are not usually needed. An X-ray or other imaging may be arranged if symptoms are unusual or are not improving, to rule out other causes such as a fracture or arthritis.[^1]

## Treatment

Patience is part of the treatment. More than 90% of people improve within 10 months of starting simple measures.[^1] The mainstays are:

- **Relative rest.** Cut back on the activities that set off the pain, and switch to exercise that does not load the feet, such as swimming.[^1][^2]
- **Stretching.** Regular stretches for the calf and for the plantar fascia itself are among the best-supported treatments.[^1][^3]
- **Footwear and supports.** Wear shoes with cushioned heels and good arch support, try heel pads or insoles, and avoid walking barefoot on hard floors.[^2]
- **Ice and pain relief.** An ice pack wrapped in a towel, held on the area for up to 20 minutes, plus over-the-counter painkillers taken as directed on the label.[^2]
- **Night splints or taping**, which a physical therapist may suggest for stubborn pain.[^3]

If pain persists for months, options include a steroid injection and shock wave therapy.[^1] A steroid injection can relieve pain for a while, but repeated injections can cause the fascia to rupture, so they are used sparingly.[^1]

Surgery is rarely needed. It is considered only after about 12 months of consistent non-surgical treatment.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

Build up running or walking gradually, replace worn-out shoes, stretch your calves and keep to a healthy weight.[^1] If you run, see [Running Injuries: How to Avoid the Usual Suspects](/articles/running-injuries-prevention).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or podiatrist if heel pain is severe, keeps coming back, or has not improved after about two weeks of self-care. Go sooner if you have tingling or loss of feeling in the foot, or if you have diabetes, because foot problems can be more serious.[^2]

Go to an emergency department or urgent care center if you cannot walk, if the pain is severe enough to make you feel faint or sick, if your foot or ankle has changed shape, or if you heard a snap, pop or grinding noise at the moment of an injury.[^4]
