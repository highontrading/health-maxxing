## What it is

Atopic dermatitis is the most common type of eczema: a long-term condition that makes skin dry, itchy and prone to inflamed rashes.[^1] It tends to come in flares, with calmer spells in between. It usually begins in early childhood and often improves with age, but it can last into adult life or start then.[^1][^2] It is not contagious.[^1]

Two things drive it: a skin barrier that lets moisture out and irritants in, and an immune system that overreacts to them.[^1]

## Symptoms

The main symptom is itch, which can be intense enough to wreck sleep. Skin is dry, and during flares patches become inflamed, bumpy or weepy. On lighter skin they look red. On brown and Black skin they are more often dark brown, purple or gray, and can be harder to spot.[^2] Repeated scratching leaves skin thickened and cracked.

Where it appears shifts with age: often the face and scalp in babies, and the creases of the elbows and knees, the neck, wrists and hands in older children and adults.[^1][^2]

## Causes and risk factors

There is no single cause. Genes that affect the skin barrier, an overactive immune response and the environment all play a part.[^1] Atopic dermatitis often runs in families alongside [asthma](/conditions/asthma) and [hay fever](/conditions/allergic-rhinitis).[^2]

Common triggers for flares include soaps and detergents, rough fabrics such as wool, cold dry weather, heat and sweat, stress, skin infections, and allergens such as house dust mites, pet fur and pollen.[^2] Food allergy is more common in children with eczema, but food is seldom the whole story, so do not cut out foods without medical advice.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Doctors diagnose eczema from the look of your skin and your history: the itch, where the rash appears, and any personal or family history of allergic conditions. No single test confirms it.[^1][^2] Allergy or patch testing is sometimes used to look for specific triggers or for contact dermatitis, a different kind of eczema.[^1]

## Treatment

There is no cure, but treatment controls symptoms for most people.[^1] The main options are:[^4][^5]

- **Moisturizers (emollients)** used every day, the foundation of care.
- **Steroid creams and ointments** during flares, with the strength matched to the body site and severity.
- **Non-steroid creams and ointments,** including calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) and newer PDE4 and JAK inhibitors.
- **Phototherapy,** controlled doses of ultraviolet light given in a clinic.
- **Systemic treatments** for moderate to severe eczema that creams cannot control: injected biologic medicines such as dupilumab, JAK inhibitor tablets, and older immune-suppressing medicines.

Infected eczema may also need antibiotics or antiviral medicine.[^2]

Many people worry about using steroid creams. Side effects such as skin thinning are mainly a risk with strong products used for long periods, so ask your doctor or pharmacist how much to apply and for how long.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Atopic dermatitis cannot reliably be prevented, but daily habits reduce flares:[^2][^3]

- Moisturize at least twice a day, including straight after bathing.
- Keep baths and showers short and lukewarm, and use fragrance-free cleansers instead of soap.
- Learn your triggers and avoid the ones you can.
- Keep nails short, and choose soft, breathable fabrics such as cotton.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if itching disrupts sleep or daily life, if moisturizers and pharmacy treatments are not controlling the rash, or if you are unsure what the rash is.

Get same-day medical advice if skin becomes painful, weeps, or develops yellow crusts or pus-filled spots, or if you feel feverish. These are signs of infection.[^2] Eczema that suddenly worsens with clusters of blisters or small open sores may be eczema herpeticum, a herpes virus infection that needs emergency treatment.[^2]
