## What it is

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition. When someone who has it eats gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley and rye, the immune system attacks the lining of the small intestine and flattens the tiny projections (villi) that absorb nutrients.[^1] It is not a food allergy or an intolerance.[^4]

About 1% of people worldwide have celiac disease, and many of them have not been diagnosed.[^1]

## Symptoms

Digestive symptoms include long-lasting diarrhea, bloating, gas, belly pain, constipation and pale, foul-smelling stools.[^1] Many people, adults especially, have few gut symptoms and instead notice tiredness, iron-deficiency anemia, weight loss, mouth ulcers, tingling in the hands and feet, or an itchy, blistering rash called dermatitis herpetiformis.[^1][^4]

Children may grow slowly, reach puberty late or be unusually irritable. Some people have no symptoms and are found only through testing.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Celiac disease needs both gluten and a genetic predisposition. Almost everyone with it carries the gene variants HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8, but most people with those genes never develop it.[^2]

Risk is higher if you have a parent, sibling or child with celiac disease (about 1 in 10 are affected), or if you have type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down syndrome or Turner syndrome.[^5][^3]

## Diagnosis

Testing has two steps. The first is a blood test for antibodies called tTG-IgA. In adults, a positive result is then confirmed with an endoscopy, in which small samples are taken from the lining of the small intestine. Some children with very high antibody levels can be diagnosed without a biopsy.[^2]

One rule matters above all: keep eating gluten until testing is finished. Going gluten-free first can make the tests falsely negative. NICE advises eating gluten in more than one meal every day for at least six weeks before testing.[^3]

A genetic test can rule celiac disease out, if neither gene variant is present, but it cannot confirm it.[^2]

## Treatment

The only treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, ideally started with help from a dietitian.[^2] That means no wheat, barley or rye, or foods made from them. Rice, corn, potatoes, meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit, vegetables and beans are naturally gluten-free.[^1] Oats do not contain gluten, but they are often contaminated with it, so choose oats labeled gluten-free. A small number of people react even to those.[^4]

Symptoms often improve within days to weeks, although the gut lining takes longer to heal, sometimes years in adults.[^1] Follow-up includes repeat antibody tests and checks for shortfalls in iron, folate, vitamin B12 and vitamin D.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no known way to prevent celiac disease. Living with it is mostly about detail: reading labels, asking how food is prepared and avoiding crumbs from shared toasters, boards and fryers. In the US, foods labeled gluten-free must contain less than 20 parts per million of gluten.[^1]

Sticking to the diet matters even if you feel fine. Untreated celiac disease can lead to anemia, weakened bones and, rarely, cancers of the intestine.[^4] [Food Intolerance vs. Food Allergy](/articles/food-intolerance-vs-food-allergy) explains how celiac disease differs from gluten sensitivity.

## When to see a doctor

Ask for a celiac blood test, before changing your diet, if you have lasting digestive symptoms, unexplained anemia, tiredness or weight loss, or if a close relative has been diagnosed.[^3]

If you already have a diagnosis and symptoms continue despite the diet, see your doctor or dietitian. Hidden gluten is the most common reason.[^2]
