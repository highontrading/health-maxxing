## What it is

Strep throat is an infection of the throat and tonsils caused by bacteria called group A Streptococcus. Most sore throats are caused by viruses. Strep accounts for only about 20% to 30% of sore throats in children and 5% to 15% in adults.[^2]

The distinction matters because strep throat is treated with antibiotics and viral sore throats are not.[^1] It is most common in children aged 5 to 15.[^1]

## Symptoms

Strep throat tends to come on quickly, with a sore throat, pain on swallowing and fever. The tonsils look red and swollen, sometimes with white patches or streaks of pus. There may be tiny red spots on the roof of the mouth and tender, swollen glands in the neck.[^1] Children can also have a headache, stomach pain, nausea or vomiting. Some people develop scarlet fever, a rough rash that feels like sandpaper.[^1][^5]

A cough, runny nose, hoarse voice and pink eye are not typical of strep. They point to a virus.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The bacteria spread in respiratory droplets when someone with the infection coughs, sneezes or talks, and by touching contaminated surfaces or sharing cups and cutlery. Symptoms usually begin two to five days after exposure.[^1]

Risk is highest in school-age children and the adults who live or work with them, and wherever people are in close quarters, such as schools, daycare centers and military training facilities.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Looking at a throat is not enough to tell strep from a viral infection.[^1] A rapid strep test uses a throat swab and gives a result within minutes. If it is negative in a child or teenager, US guidelines recommend confirming with a throat culture, which takes a day or two. In adults that back-up is usually unnecessary.[^2] Testing is generally not advised when symptoms clearly point to a virus.[^2]

UK practice relies more on symptom scoring systems to judge who is likely to benefit from antibiotics.[^4]

## Treatment

Strep throat is treated with antibiotics. Penicillin or amoxicillin is the first choice, with alternatives for people who are allergic to penicillin.[^1][^2] Courses last 5 to 10 days, depending on the antibiotic and national guidance. Take it exactly as prescribed, even once you feel better.[^2][^4]

Antibiotics shorten symptoms modestly, make you less contagious and help prevent complications. These include abscesses around the tonsils and rheumatic fever, a rare inflammatory condition that can damage the heart valves.[^1][^2]

For comfort, use acetaminophen (paracetamol) or ibuprofen as directed on the label, drink plenty of fluids and eat soft or cool foods.[^3]

Because antibiotics do nothing for the viral majority of sore throats, a test or a clinical assessment should come before a prescription. [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help) explains why that matters.

## Prevention and self-care

There is no vaccine against strep throat. Wash your hands often, cover coughs and sneezes, and do not share cups or cutlery with someone who is ill.[^1] If you have strep throat, stay home from work, school or daycare until you have no fever and have taken antibiotics for at least 12 to 24 hours.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor for a sore throat with fever and swollen neck glands but no cough or cold symptoms, a sore throat with a rash, or a sore throat that has not improved after a week.[^3] People with a weakened immune system should seek advice early.[^3]

Call your local emergency number for difficulty breathing, noisy high-pitched breathing, drooling or being unable to swallow, or severe symptoms that are getting worse quickly.[^3] Severe pain on one side of the throat with trouble opening your mouth can signal an abscess and needs same-day care.

After a strep infection, see a doctor promptly if you notice dark urine, swelling, painful joints or a new fever in the following weeks. Rarely, strep is followed by kidney inflammation or rheumatic fever.[^1]
