## What it is

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) has two parts. Obsessions are unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images or urges that cause intense anxiety. Compulsions are repetitive behaviors or mental acts that a person feels driven to perform to ease that anxiety or to stop something bad from happening.[^1] The relief is brief, so the cycle repeats.

OCD is not a personality quirk or a liking for tidiness. It is diagnosed when obsessions or compulsions take up a lot of time, typically more than an hour a day, or cause real distress or problems in daily life.[^1] It affects roughly 1 to 3 in every 100 people at some point in life and usually begins in childhood, the teens or early adulthood.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

Common obsessions include fear of germs or contamination, fear of harming yourself or others, unwanted taboo thoughts about violence, sex or religion, and a need for symmetry or perfect order.[^1]

Common compulsions include excessive washing or cleaning, repeated checking (of locks or the stove, for example), counting, arranging things in a precise way, and silently praying or repeating words.[^1]

The intrusive thoughts of OCD are unwanted and disturbing to the person having them, which is exactly why they cause so much distress. Most people with OCD recognize that their fears do not make sense, but they cannot simply reason them away.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is unknown. OCD is more likely if a parent or sibling has it, and brain imaging studies show differences in certain brain circuits.[^1] In a small number of children, symptoms appear suddenly after a streptococcal infection.[^1] OCD often occurs alongside anxiety, depression or tic disorders.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on a conversation with a doctor or mental health professional about your thoughts and behaviors, how much time they take and how much distress they cause. A physical examination may be done to rule out other causes.[^4]

Shame keeps many people from mentioning their symptoms, and years can pass between the first symptoms and a diagnosis.[^2] Clinicians have heard these thoughts before. Describing them plainly is the fastest route to help, and [How to Get More Out of a Doctor's Appointment](/articles/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor) can help you prepare.

## Treatment

Two treatments have strong evidence, used alone or together:[^1][^3]

- **Exposure and response prevention (ERP)**, a form of cognitive behavioral therapy. With a therapist, you gradually face the situations or thoughts that set off your obsessions while resisting the compulsion, and the anxiety fades with practice.
- **SSRI antidepressants.** In OCD they often need higher doses than are used for depression, and they can take 8 to 12 weeks to show their effect. Clomipramine is an alternative.

For severe OCD that has not improved, specialists may add another medicine or offer more intensive therapy or brain stimulation treatments.[^1][^3]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no known way to prevent OCD, but early treatment limits how much of your life it takes over. Family and friends can help by supporting treatment, and by learning from the care team how to avoid being drawn into rituals or repeated reassurance.[^3]

Sleep, exercise and stress management support treatment. They do not replace it.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or mental health professional if obsessions or compulsions take more than an hour a day, cause distress, or interfere with work, school or relationships.

OCD often comes with depression, and some people have thoughts of suicide.[^3] If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^5]
