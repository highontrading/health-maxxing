## What it is

Osteoporosis means "porous bone". Bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt. When breakdown outpaces rebuilding, bones lose density and strength and break more easily.[^1]

It is often called a silent disease, because nothing hurts until a bone breaks, most often in the hip, spine or wrist.[^1] It is common. In the United States, about one in two women and up to one in four men over 50 will break a bone because of osteoporosis.[^3]

## Symptoms

Most people have no symptoms. The first sign is often a fracture after a minor fall, or even a bump or a cough, that would not break a healthy bone.[^1] Fractures of the spinal bones can happen without a fall and may show up only as back pain, lost height or a stooped upper back.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Bone mass peaks in early adulthood and then slowly declines. In women, bone loss speeds up in the years after menopause, as estrogen falls.[^1] Risk is higher with:[^1][^4]

- older age and female sex
- a parent who broke a hip
- low body weight
- smoking and heavy drinking
- low calcium or vitamin D intake and little physical activity
- long-term use of steroid tablets and some other medicines
- conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, an overactive thyroid, celiac disease, early menopause and low testosterone

Men get osteoporosis too.[^3]

## Diagnosis

A bone density scan of the hip and spine, called a DXA scan, is the standard test. The result is a T-score, which compares your bone density with that of a healthy young adult:[^4]

| T-score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| -1.0 or higher | Normal |
| Between -1.0 and -2.5 | Low bone mass (osteopenia) |
| -2.5 or lower | Osteoporosis |

A hip or spine fracture from a minor fall can establish the diagnosis on its own. Doctors also estimate your 10-year fracture risk with tools such as FRAX, which combine bone density with other risk factors.[^4]

The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all women aged 65 and older, and younger women who have been through menopause and are at increased risk. It found too little evidence to recommend for or against screening men.[^2]

## Treatment

Treatment aims to prevent fractures. Bisphosphonates, which slow bone breakdown, are the usual first choice. Other options include denosumab injections and, for people at very high risk, bone-building medicines such as teriparatide and romosozumab.[^4]

Stopping denosumab without a follow-on treatment can lead to rapid bone loss and spinal fractures, so never skip or stop it without a plan from your prescriber.[^4]

Getting enough calcium and vitamin D and lowering your risk of falls are part of treatment too.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

- **Move.** Weight-bearing exercise such as walking, climbing stairs and dancing, plus resistance training, helps maintain bone.[^1]
- **Eat for bone.** Adults need about 1,000 mg of calcium a day, rising to 1,200 mg for women over 50 and everyone over 70, ideally from food. Vitamin D helps the body absorb it.[^5]
- **Avoid tobacco and limit alcohol.**[^1]
- **Prevent falls.** Balance training and a home free of trip hazards protect fragile bones.[^4]

[Osteoporosis and Bone Health](/articles/osteoporosis-and-bone-health) and [balance and fall prevention](/articles/balance-and-fall-prevention) go deeper.

## When to see a doctor

Ask about a bone density test if you are a woman aged 65 or older, if you have been through menopause and have risk factors, if you have broken a bone after a minor fall as an adult, if you have lost height, or if you take long-term steroid tablets.[^2][^4]

Call your local emergency number for a suspected hip fracture: severe hip or groin pain after a fall, and not being able to stand or put weight on the leg. Sudden severe back pain also needs urgent assessment, especially with leg weakness, numbness or loss of bladder control.
