## What it is

Tinnitus is the perception of a sound that has no source outside your body, so other people cannot hear it. It is often described as ringing, but it can also be buzzing, hissing, humming, roaring or clicking.[^1]

It is a symptom, not a disease, and it is common: surveys suggest 10% to 25% of adults have it. For most people it is mild. For some it interferes with sleep, concentration and mood.[^1]

## Symptoms

The sound may be in one ear, both ears or inside your head. It can be constant or come and go, soft or loud, and it is usually more noticeable when things are quiet. Tinnitus that lasts three months or longer is called chronic.[^1]

One type is different. Pulsatile tinnitus is a rhythmic whooshing in time with your heartbeat, often related to blood flow near the ear, and it should always be checked.[^1][^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Most people with tinnitus have some degree of hearing loss, even if they have not noticed it. A leading theory is that when the inner ear is damaged, the brain circuits that process sound change their activity, and you hear that change as noise.[^1]

Common triggers include loud noise at work or play, age-related hearing loss, a build-up of earwax, ear infections and head or neck injuries. Some medicines can cause it, including high doses of aspirin and similar painkillers, certain antibiotics, some cancer drugs and antimalarial medicines.[^1] Less common causes are Meniere's disease, jaw joint problems, a benign growth on the hearing nerve and blood vessel disorders.[^1]

## Diagnosis

A doctor will ask about the sound and examine your ears, head and neck. US specialist guidance recommends a hearing test when tinnitus is in one ear only, has lasted six months or more, or comes with hearing difficulty.[^2]

Scans are not routine. The same guidance advises against imaging unless tinnitus is one-sided or pulsatile, or comes with uneven hearing loss or neurological signs.[^2]

## Treatment

If there is a fixable cause, such as earwax, an infection or a medicine, dealing with it may settle the tinnitus.[^1] For persistent tinnitus there is no cure yet, but several treatments reduce its impact:

- **Hearing aids**, if you have hearing loss. Bringing back outside sound often makes tinnitus less noticeable.[^2]
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**. It does not remove the sound, but it changes how you respond to it and has the best evidence for reducing distress. The US guideline recommends it for persistent, bothersome tinnitus, and UK guidance supports CBT-based therapy too.[^2][^4]
- **Sound therapy**, using background sound or noise generators, helps some people.[^2]

The US guideline recommends against medicines such as antidepressants, anticonvulsants and anti-anxiety drugs as a treatment for tinnitus itself, and against supplements including ginkgo biloba, melatonin and zinc.[^2] Anxiety, depression or insomnia that come with tinnitus are still worth treating in their own right.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

Protect your hearing. Long or repeated exposure to sound at or above 85 decibels can cause permanent damage, so turn the volume down, step away from the source or wear earplugs.[^6] The WHO estimates that more than 1 billion young adults are at risk of avoidable hearing loss from unsafe listening.[^5]

If you already have tinnitus, the NHS suggests avoiding complete silence (soft background sound helps), finding ways to relax and working on sleep.[^3] See [Insomnia: Why You Can't Sleep and What Actually Works](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if tinnitus is regular or constant, is getting worse, affects only one ear or is disturbing your sleep, concentration or mood. Pulsatile tinnitus needs an urgent appointment.[^3]

Get emergency care for tinnitus that follows a head injury or comes with sudden hearing loss, weakness in the face or a spinning sensation.[^3] If tinnitus is making you think about harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away.[^7]
