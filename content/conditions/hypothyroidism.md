## What it is

Your thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland at the front of your neck. Its hormones set the pace at which your body uses energy, which affects your heart rate, temperature, digestion and mood. In hypothyroidism the gland makes too little hormone, and those processes slow down.[^1]

Nearly 5 in 100 Americans aged 12 and older have it, although most cases are mild. It is much more common in women and in people over 60.[^1]

## Symptoms

Symptoms build slowly, often over years, and are easy to put down to aging or a busy life. They include tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, thinning hair, low mood, muscle aches, a slow heart rate and heavy or irregular periods.[^1][^3]

None of these is specific to the thyroid, and plenty of people with normal thyroid tests have them too. Symptoms can raise the question, but only a blood test answers it.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

The most common cause in countries with enough iodine in the diet is Hashimoto's disease, in which the immune system attacks the thyroid.[^1] Too little iodine also causes hypothyroidism, which is why many countries add iodine to salt.[^2]

Other causes include thyroid surgery, radioactive iodine treatment, radiation to the neck, inflammation of the thyroid (thyroiditis, which can follow pregnancy) and some medicines, such as lithium and amiodarone. A few babies are born with an underactive thyroid, which newborn screening picks up.[^1][^3]

Your risk is higher if you have a family history of thyroid disease or another autoimmune condition, such as type 1 diabetes or celiac disease.[^1]

## Diagnosis

The key test measures thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), the signal your pituitary gland sends to tell the thyroid to work harder. A TSH above the laboratory's reference range with a low level of the thyroid hormone thyroxine (free T4) confirms hypothyroidism.[^2]

A raised TSH with a normal T4 is called subclinical hypothyroidism. It is milder, sometimes settles by itself and does not always need treatment. In the UK, NICE advises considering treatment when TSH is 10 mIU/L or higher on two tests three months apart.[^4]

## Treatment

The standard treatment is levothyroxine, a synthetic version of T4, taken as a daily tablet. The dose is adjusted using a TSH test about 6 to 8 weeks after you start or change it, then checked about once a year when stable.[^1] Most people need treatment for life.[^3]

Levothyroxine is absorbed best on an empty stomach, taken the same way each day. Calcium and iron supplements can block its absorption if taken within a few hours.[^5] The dose often needs to rise in pregnancy, so tell your doctor early if you are pregnant or planning to be.[^1][^2]

The American Thyroid Association does not recommend routinely adding the hormone T3 or using thyroid extracts from animal glands, because good evidence that they work better than levothyroxine alone is lacking.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

Hashimoto's disease cannot be prevented. Getting enough iodine, from iodized salt, dairy foods or seafood, prevents the iodine-deficiency form, but more is not better: high-dose iodine and kelp supplements can trigger or worsen thyroid problems.[^1][^2]

If you take levothyroxine, keep taking it even when you feel well and keep your blood test appointments. Untreated hypothyroidism can raise cholesterol and affect the heart and fertility.[^1] [Thyroid 101](/articles/thyroid-101) explains the tests and common myths.

## When to see a doctor

Ask for a thyroid blood test if you have persistent tiredness, feel cold, gain weight without explanation, feel low or notice a swelling in your neck.[^3]

Severe, long-untreated hypothyroidism can rarely lead to myxedema coma, with extreme drowsiness, confusion, low body temperature and slow breathing. This is an emergency: call your local emergency number.[^1]
