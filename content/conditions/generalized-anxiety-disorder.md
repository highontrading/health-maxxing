## What it is

Everyone worries. In generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), the worry is excessive, hard to switch off, and jumps between everyday topics such as health, money, work and family, even when there is little reason for concern.[^2] To count as GAD, it is present on most days for at least six months and causes distress or gets in the way of daily life.[^3]

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions in the world, affecting more than 300 million people. Yet WHO estimates that only about 1 in 4 of those who need treatment receive any.[^1]

## Symptoms

Along with constant worry, GAD causes physical and mental symptoms: feeling restless or on edge, tiring easily, trouble concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, headaches or unexplained aches, and difficulty falling or staying asleep.[^2] Many people first see a doctor about the physical symptoms, not the worry itself.[^3]

GAD often occurs alongside depression or another anxiety disorder.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

GAD develops from a combination of factors, not a single cause.[^1] Risk is higher if anxiety runs in your family, if you were shy or fearful as a child, or if you have lived through adversity, trauma or prolonged stress.[^2] Women are affected more often than men.[^1]

Some physical conditions and substances can cause or worsen anxiety symptoms, including thyroid problems, heart rhythm problems, caffeine and certain medicines.[^2]

## Diagnosis

There is no lab test for GAD. A clinician asks about your symptoms and their effect on your life, often with a short questionnaire such as the GAD-7, and may examine you or order blood tests to rule out physical causes.[^2][^4]

In the United States, the Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults under 65 for anxiety disorders, including during and after pregnancy.[^4]

## Treatment

NICE recommends a stepped approach that starts with the least intensive option likely to help:[^3]

1. Education about GAD and active monitoring.
2. Low-intensity psychological support, such as guided self-help or group courses based on cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).
3. Either a full course of CBT or applied relaxation, or medication, depending on what you prefer.

The first-choice medicines are SSRI antidepressants, with SNRIs and pregabalin as alternatives.[^3] They take several weeks to reach their full effect.[^2] Benzodiazepines (sedatives) are not recommended for GAD except as a short-term measure in a crisis, because of the risk of dependence.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Habits that help alongside treatment include regular physical activity, steady sleep and meal times, cutting back on alcohol, and learning relaxation techniques such as slow breathing or mindfulness.[^1] Cutting down on caffeine is worth trying too.[^2] These steps support professional care. They do not replace it.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or mental health professional if worry is hard to control, is present most days, or is affecting your sleep, work or relationships. Highly effective treatments exist, and too few people get them.[^1]

Chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting should be checked urgently, not assumed to be anxiety. If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^5]
