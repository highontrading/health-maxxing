## What it is

**A heart attack is a medical emergency. If you think you or someone near you is having one, call your local emergency number now.**

A heart attack (myocardial infarction) happens when blood flow to part of the heart muscle is suddenly blocked. Usually a fatty plaque in a coronary artery ruptures and a clot forms on it. Starved of oxygen, heart muscle begins to die, and the damage grows the longer the artery stays blocked.[^2]

A heart attack is not the same as cardiac arrest, in which the heart stops pumping and the person collapses, although a heart attack can trigger one.[^3]

## Symptoms

Symptoms can come on suddenly or build over hours. The American Heart Association lists these warning signs:[^1]

- Chest discomfort: pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain in the center of the chest that lasts more than a few minutes, or goes away and comes back.
- Discomfort in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw or stomach.
- Shortness of breath, with or without chest discomfort.
- A cold sweat, nausea or lightheadedness.

Chest pain is the most common symptom in both men and women, but women are somewhat more likely to have breathlessness, nausea or vomiting, and back or jaw pain.[^1] Some heart attacks cause only mild discomfort that feels like indigestion, or no chest pain at all, especially in women, older people and people with diabetes.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Most heart attacks are caused by [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease). Less often the cause is a severe spasm or a tear in the wall of a coronary artery.[^2] The main risk factors are smoking, high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, diabetes, excess weight, physical inactivity, older age and a family history of early heart disease.[^2][^3]

## Diagnosis

Speed matters, so testing starts at once. Guidelines call for an electrocardiogram (ECG) within 10 minutes of first medical contact.[^4] It can show a complete blockage, known as a STEMI, that needs the artery opened immediately. Blood tests for troponin, a protein released by damaged heart muscle, confirm other heart attacks, and coronary angiography shows where the blockage is.[^2][^4]

## Treatment

The priority is restoring blood flow. For a STEMI, the preferred treatment is emergency angioplasty: a thin tube is threaded to the blocked artery, a balloon opens it and a stent holds it open. Where that cannot be done quickly, clot-dissolving medicine is given instead.[^4] Some people need bypass surgery.[^2]

Hospital teams also give antiplatelet medicines, including aspirin, and anticoagulants. Afterward, most people take long-term medicines such as a statin and antiplatelets, often with a beta blocker or ACE inhibitor. They are also referred to cardiac rehabilitation, a supervised program of exercise and education that supports recovery.[^2][^4]

## Prevention and self-care

Not smoking, staying active, eating well and keeping blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar under control lower your risk of a first heart attack and of another one.[^2] After a heart attack, keep taking your prescribed medicines and complete cardiac rehab. [Know Your Risk](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors) explains what drives heart disease.

## When to see a doctor

**Call your local emergency number immediately if you have chest pain or pressure that lasts more than a few minutes, or any of the warning signs above. Do not wait to see if it passes, and do not drive yourself.** Ambulance crews can start treatment on the way.[^1]

While you wait, sit down and rest. The emergency operator may tell you to chew an aspirin if you are not allergic to it.[^3] If the person collapses and is not breathing normally, start CPR and send someone for a defibrillator (AED).[^3]
