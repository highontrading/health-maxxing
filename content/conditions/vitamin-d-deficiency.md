## What it is

Vitamin D helps your gut absorb calcium and phosphate, the minerals that harden bone, and it supports muscle function. Your skin makes it when exposed to sunlight. Food supplies smaller amounts, mainly from oily fish, egg yolks and fortified foods.[^1]

Deficiency means there is too little to keep bones healthy. In children it causes rickets, in which growing bones become soft and misshapen. In adults it causes osteomalacia, with bone pain and muscle weakness, and over time it contributes to osteoporosis.[^1][^5]

## Symptoms

Mild deficiency usually causes no symptoms. More severe deficiency causes bone pain, muscle weakness and aches. Children may have bowed legs, swollen wrists or knees, slow growth and late walking.[^5] If blood calcium drops as well, cramps, tingling in the hands and feet and, rarely, seizures can follow.[^5]

## Causes and risk factors

The main cause is too little sun on the skin: living far from the equator, spending most of the day indoors or wearing clothing that covers most of the body. In the UK, for example, sunlight is too weak to make vitamin D from October to early March.[^2]

Other risk factors are darker skin (which makes less vitamin D from the same sunlight), older age, obesity, conditions that reduce fat absorption such as celiac disease and Crohn's disease, and weight-loss surgery. Breastfed babies who are not given a supplement are also at risk.[^1]

## Diagnosis

The test is a blood level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Labs report it in nmol/L or ng/mL (1 ng/mL equals 2.5 nmol/L), and expert bodies draw the lines differently:

| Body | Deficient | Adequate for most people |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US National Academies | Below 30 nmol/L (12 ng/mL) | 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) or more |
| UK advisory committee (SACN) | Below 25 nmol/L (10 ng/mL) | Not defined |

Values between the two US figures may be inadequate for some people.[^1][^6]

Testing everyone is not recommended. The US Preventive Services Task Force (2021) found too little evidence to judge screening in adults without symptoms, and the Endocrine Society (2024) advises against routine testing of healthy people.[^4][^3] Testing makes sense if you have symptoms or a condition that puts you at risk.

## Treatment

Confirmed deficiency is treated with vitamin D supplements at a dose and for a period set by a clinician, sometimes with calcium, along with treatment of any underlying cause.[^5] Rickets and osteomalacia usually respond well.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

Recommended intakes are modest. The US recommended dietary allowance is 15 mcg (600 IU) a day from age 1 to 70 and 20 mcg (800 IU) after 70.[^1] The NHS advises everyone to consider 10 mcg (400 IU) a day in autumn and winter, and all year for people who get little sun or have darker skin. It also advises a daily supplement for breastfed babies from birth.[^2]

More is not better. The safe upper limit for adults is 100 mcg (4,000 IU) a day, and overdoing supplements raises blood calcium, which can cause nausea, vomiting, thirst, confusion and kidney damage.[^1][^2] Large trials in generally healthy adults have not shown that supplements prevent heart disease or cancer.[^1] The Endocrine Society suggests routine supplements only for certain groups, including children, people who are pregnant and adults aged 75 and over.[^3]

See [Vitamins and Supplements: Who Actually Needs Them?](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them) and [Osteoporosis and Bone Health](/articles/osteoporosis-and-bone-health).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor for persistent bone pain or muscle weakness, or if a child has bowed legs, slow growth or is late to walk.[^5] Ask about testing if you have a bowel, liver or kidney condition or have had weight-loss surgery.

Call your local emergency number if a baby or child has a seizure. Get urgent advice for vomiting, confusion and extreme thirst after taking high-dose vitamin D.[^1]
