## What it is

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is an ongoing difficulty getting or keeping an erection firm enough for sex. The occasional off night is normal. ED means it happens regularly.[^1]

It is common. ED affects about 30 million men in the United States, and in a well-known Massachusetts study about half of men aged 40 to 70 reported some degree of it.[^1][^5] It becomes more frequent with age, but it is not an inevitable part of aging and it can usually be treated.[^1]

## Symptoms

- Getting an erection sometimes, but not every time you want sex.
- Getting an erection that does not last long enough for sex.
- Being unable to get an erection at all.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

An erection depends on blood flow, nerves, hormones and state of mind, and a problem with any of them can cause ED. Often more than one is involved.[^1]

- **Physical causes** include narrowed blood vessels, high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, smoking, chronic kidney disease, [low testosterone](/conditions/low-testosterone) and nerve damage from surgery or injury.[^1][^4]
- **Medicines and substances** include some blood pressure medicines and antidepressants, and heavy use of alcohol or drugs.[^1]
- **Psychological causes** include stress, anxiety about performance, depression and relationship difficulties.[^1][^3]

ED shares its causes with heart disease and can appear before any heart symptoms. The American Urological Association (AUA) calls it a risk marker for cardiovascular disease, which is why checking your heart risk is part of the assessment.[^2]

## Diagnosis

There is no single test. A clinician will ask about your sexual, medical and mental health and your medicines, examine you and check your blood pressure. Blood tests usually include blood sugar, cholesterol and a morning testosterone level.[^2] Specialized tests, such as an ultrasound scan of blood flow in the penis, are sometimes used.[^1]

## Treatment

Treatment is a shared decision, and the AUA says any of these can be a reasonable place to start:[^2]

- **Lifestyle changes**: more physical activity, losing excess weight, stopping smoking and drinking less.[^1][^3]
- **A review of your medicines.** Do not stop a prescribed medicine without advice.[^1]
- **Counseling or sex therapy**, alone or alongside other treatment, when stress, anxiety or relationship issues play a part.[^2]
- **PDE5 inhibitors**, such as sildenafil and tadalafil, which increase blood flow to the penis in response to sexual stimulation. They must never be combined with nitrate medicines for chest pain, because blood pressure can fall dangerously.[^1][^2]
- **Other options**: vacuum devices, medicine injected into the penis or placed in the urethra, and surgically implanted devices.[^1][^4]
- **Testosterone**, only when blood tests confirm that it is low.[^2]

The AUA regards shockwave therapy and platelet-rich plasma injections as unproven.[^2] Be wary of "natural" ED supplements: they are not tested as medicines are and can interact with prescriptions.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

What protects your heart protects your erections: staying active, eating well, keeping a healthy weight, not smoking, limiting alcohol and keeping blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar under control.[^1][^3] [Erectile Dysfunction: Causes, the Heart Link and What Works](/articles/erectile-dysfunction-causes-and-treatments) goes into the evidence.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if erection problems keep happening. Apart from the effect on your sex life, ED can be the first sign of diabetes or heart disease.[^3]

**Get emergency care** for an erection that lasts more than four hours, or for sudden loss of vision or hearing after taking an ED medicine.[^2] If you have chest pain, call your local emergency number and tell the crew if you have taken an ED medicine in the past two days, because it affects which heart medicines are safe to give.[^2]
