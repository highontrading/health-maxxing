## What it is

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a long-term condition in which the immune system attacks myelin, the fatty coating that insulates nerve fibers in the brain, spinal cord and optic nerves. The resulting inflammation and scarring slow or block nerve signals.[^2]

WHO estimates that more than 1.8 million people worldwide have MS.[^1] It is most often diagnosed in people in their 20s, 30s and 40s and is two to three times more common in women than in men.[^3]

There are three main patterns.[^3] Most people, roughly 8 to 9 in 10, start with relapsing-remitting MS: attacks of symptoms (relapses) followed by partial or full recovery. Over time this can turn into secondary progressive MS, with steady worsening. About 1 in 10 have primary progressive MS, which worsens gradually from the start.

## Symptoms

Symptoms depend on which nerves are affected, so they vary widely from person to person. Common ones include blurred vision or pain on moving one eye, numbness or tingling, fatigue, leg weakness, poor balance, muscle stiffness, bladder problems and trouble with memory or concentration.[^1][^3]

A relapse is a new or worsening symptom that lasts more than 24 hours and is not explained by an infection or another cause.[^5]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not fully known. MS is not directly inherited, although having a close relative with it raises your risk.[^3]

Infection with Epstein-Barr virus appears to be a key trigger. In a study of more than 10 million US military personnel, the risk of MS rose about 32-fold after infection with the virus.[^4] Nearly everyone catches it at some point, though, and very few go on to develop MS. Other risk factors include smoking, low vitamin D and little sunlight, and obesity in the teenage years.[^3]

## Diagnosis

A neurologist makes the diagnosis by showing that damage has occurred in different parts of the central nervous system at different times, and by ruling out other explanations.[^6] MRI scans of the brain and spinal cord are central. A lumbar puncture to test spinal fluid and blood tests to exclude conditions that mimic MS are often added.[^3][^6]

## Treatment

There is no cure, but disease-modifying therapies reduce relapses and slow the build-up of damage. They come as injections, tablets and infusions of varying strength, and they work best in relapsing forms of MS.[^2] Fewer options exist for progressive MS. Ocrelizumab was the first medicine approved for the primary progressive form.[^2]

A relapse that interferes with daily life may be treated with a short course of high-dose steroids. This speeds recovery but does not change the long-term course.[^3][^5]

Rehabilitation and symptom treatments matter just as much: physiotherapy, and help with fatigue, muscle stiffness, bladder problems, pain and mood.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no proven way to prevent MS. If you have it, not smoking is the clearest step you can take, because smoking may speed up disability.[^5] Our guide to [quitting smoking](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) can help.

Regular exercise is safe and may improve strength, mobility and fatigue.[^5] NICE does not recommend taking vitamin D purely as a treatment for MS.[^5]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor about unexplained symptoms such as vision changes, numbness or weakness that last more than a day. If you already have MS, contact your MS team about a suspected relapse.

Depression and anxiety are common in MS and can be treated, so mention low mood too.[^1] If you ever think about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away.

Seek emergency care for sudden vision loss, for sudden weakness or trouble speaking (which could be a stroke), for new loss of bladder or bowel control, or for a fever while taking immune-suppressing treatment.
