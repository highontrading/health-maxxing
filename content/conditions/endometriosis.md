## What it is

Endometriosis is a long-term condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside it. It is found most often on the ovaries, the fallopian tubes and the lining of the pelvis, and sometimes on the bowel or bladder.[^1][^4] The tissue causes inflammation and can form scar tissue and cysts.

It affects roughly 1 in 10 women and girls of reproductive age, about 190 million people worldwide.[^1] Even so, diagnosis often takes years, partly because severe period pain is so often treated as normal.[^1]

## Symptoms

Symptoms vary widely, and how bad they are does not reliably reflect how much endometriosis is present. Some people have none.[^4] The common ones are:[^1][^5]

- period pain severe enough to stop normal activities
- ongoing pain in the pelvis or lower abdomen
- pain during or after sex
- pain when urinating or opening the bowels, mainly during periods
- heavy periods
- bloating, nausea and tiredness
- difficulty getting pregnant

Living with long-term pain can also lead to anxiety and depression.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not known.[^1] Leading explanations include menstrual blood flowing backward through the fallopian tubes into the pelvis, cells outside the uterus changing into lining-like cells, and the influence of the hormone estrogen, which fuels the condition.[^1]

The risk is higher if your mother, sister or daughter has endometriosis.[^4] Nothing you did caused it, and there is currently no known way to prevent it.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis starts with your symptoms and a pelvic examination, followed by an ultrasound scan, usually done through the vagina, or sometimes an MRI. Scans can show cysts on the ovaries and deeper disease.[^2][^3] A normal examination or scan does not rule endometriosis out.[^3]

Laparoscopy, keyhole surgery that lets a surgeon see the tissue directly and take a sample, used to be considered essential. The 2022 European guideline no longer treats it as the gold standard and reserves it mainly for people whose scans are negative or whose treatment has not worked.[^2] Doctors can start treatment on the basis of symptoms without waiting for surgical confirmation.[^3]

## Treatment

There is no cure, so treatment aims to control pain, improve quality of life and support fertility. The plan depends on your symptoms and whether you want to become pregnant.[^1] Options include:[^2][^3]

- **Pain relief** with anti-inflammatory painkillers.
- **Hormonal treatments** that suppress ovulation and periods: combined contraceptive pills, progestogens (as tablets, injections, an implant or a hormonal IUD), and medicines that lower estrogen, called GnRH agonists and antagonists. These treatments also prevent pregnancy, so they do not suit people who are trying to conceive.
- **Surgery,** usually keyhole, to remove or destroy patches of endometriosis and cysts. Symptoms can return afterward.
- **Fertility treatment,** which can include surgery or assisted reproduction such as IVF.

Hysterectomy is a last resort, and it is not a guaranteed cure.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Endometriosis cannot be prevented.[^1] A pain and symptom diary helps you and your doctor see patterns and judge whether a treatment is working.[^3] Evidence for diet, exercise and complementary therapies is limited, though some people find that heat, gentle movement and support groups help them cope.[^2]

[Period Pain and Heavy Periods](/articles/period-pain-and-heavy-periods) explains what is typical and what is not.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if period pain stops you doing normal activities, if you have pain during sex or when using the toilet around your period, or if your symptoms are affecting your work, relationships or mood.[^5] Seek advice too if you have been trying to conceive for a year without success, or for six months if you are 35 or older.

If your symptoms are dismissed and treatment is not helping, it is reasonable to ask for a referral to a gynecologist.[^3]

Get urgent care for sudden, severe pelvic or abdominal pain, especially with fever, vomiting, fainting or a possible pregnancy.
