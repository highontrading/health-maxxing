## What it is

Sciatica is a pattern of symptoms, not a diagnosis in itself: pain that travels along the path of the sciatic nerve, from the lower back or buttock down the back of the leg.[^6] It happens when one of the nerve roots that leave the lower spine and join to form the sciatic nerve is irritated or squeezed. It usually affects one leg.[^3]

## Symptoms

The leg pain is typically sharp, burning or like an electric shock, and it usually travels below the knee into the calf or foot.[^3] Your back may hurt too, but generally less than your buttock, leg or foot.[^1] You may also have tingling, numbness or weakness in the leg or foot, and symptoms can be worse when you move, cough or sneeze.[^1]

If you only have back pain, it is probably not sciatica.[^1] See [low back pain](/conditions/low-back-pain) instead.

## Causes and risk factors

The most common cause is a slipped (herniated) disc: the soft cushion between two spinal bones bulges out and presses on a nerve root.[^1][^3] Other causes include spinal stenosis (a narrowing of the canal that carries the nerves), a spinal bone slipping out of position and, less often, a back injury.[^1]

Sciatica is most common between the ages of 30 and 50.[^3]

## Diagnosis

A clinician can usually diagnose sciatica from your symptoms and an examination of leg strength, reflexes and sensation, including raising your straightened leg to see whether that brings on the pain.[^3]

Scans are not routinely needed.[^2] An MRI is generally reserved for people with warning signs or worsening weakness, and for those being considered for an injection or surgery because symptoms have not settled.[^2][^3]

## Treatment

Sciatica usually gets better within 4 to 6 weeks, although it can last longer.[^1] Most people recover without surgery.[^3]

Early treatment is about staying mobile while the nerve settles:[^1]

- Carry on with normal activities as far as you can, and avoid long periods in bed.
- Start gentle exercise and stretches as soon as you can, and use heat packs on the painful area.
- Ask a pharmacist about pain relief. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) on its own is unlikely to help, and it is not clear how much anti-inflammatory painkillers help with sciatica.

NICE advises against several medicines that were once commonly prescribed for sciatica, including gabapentin, pregabalin, oral steroids and benzodiazepines. It also advises against opioids for chronic sciatica.[^2]

If severe pain persists, options include an epidural injection of steroid and local anesthetic, and surgery to take pressure off the nerve.[^2] In a randomized trial, early surgery relieved leg pain faster than continued non-surgical care, but results at one year were similar in the two groups.[^5] So when there are no emergency signs, surgery is a choice, not a necessity.

## Prevention and self-care

To lower the chance of another episode, stay active with regular exercise, lift heavy objects with care, sit and stand with good posture, and lose weight if you are overweight.[^1] See also [what works for low back pain](/articles/low-back-pain-what-works).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if the pain has not improved after a few weeks of self-care, is getting worse, or stops you doing normal activities.[^1]

Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department straight away if you have:[^1]

- sciatica on both sides
- weakness or numbness in both legs that is severe or getting worse
- numbness around or under your genitals, or around your anus
- difficulty starting to urinate, being unable to urinate, or loss of bladder control
- loss of bowel control, or not noticing when you need to go

These are signs of cauda equina syndrome, in which the bundle of nerves at the base of the spine is compressed. It needs emergency surgery to prevent permanent damage.[^4] A foot that suddenly drags, or leg weakness that is quickly getting worse, also needs same-day assessment.
