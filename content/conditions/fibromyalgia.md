## What it is

Fibromyalgia is a long-term condition that causes pain all over the body, along with fatigue, poor sleep and difficulty thinking clearly.[^1] Researchers believe it involves a change in how the nervous system processes pain signals, so that people become more sensitive to pain.[^1][^3]

It is real and it can be disabling, but it is not an autoimmune or inflammatory disease, and it does not cause deformity or shorten life.[^5] It affects an estimated 2 to 4% of people, women more often than men.[^5]

## Symptoms

The defining symptom is widespread pain lasting three months or more, often with heightened sensitivity to touch and pressure.[^1][^6] Most people also have:[^1][^3]

- fatigue that rest does not fix
- sleep that leaves them unrefreshed
- problems with memory and concentration, often called "fibro fog"
- stiffness, headaches, irritable bowel symptoms, or tingling in the hands and feet

Symptoms come and go, and can be worse with stress, changes in the weather or changes in how active you are.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not fully understood. Fibromyalgia tends to run in families, and it often starts after a physically or emotionally stressful event such as an injury, an infection, an operation or a bereavement. Sometimes there is no obvious trigger.[^1][^3] It is more common in people who have rheumatoid arthritis, lupus or osteoarthritis.[^1]

## Diagnosis

There is no blood test or scan for fibromyalgia. Doctors diagnose it from the pattern of symptoms: pain in several regions of the body for at least three months, together with fatigue, unrefreshing sleep and problems with thinking.[^6] Counting "tender points" on the body is no longer required.[^5]

Blood tests are used to check for other conditions that can cause similar symptoms, such as thyroid disease, anemia and inflammatory arthritis.[^3] Fibromyalgia can also exist alongside them: having another diagnosis does not rule it out.[^6]

## Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be reduced and daily functioning improved. European guidelines recommend starting with non-drug treatment, and the approach with the strongest evidence is exercise, both aerobic and strengthening.[^2] Start low and build up gradually.

Other options with supporting evidence include cognitive behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindful movement such as tai chi or yoga, and acupuncture.[^2][^4]

Medicines help some people and are generally add-ons. Options include certain antidepressants, such as low-dose amitriptyline or duloxetine, and in some countries pregabalin.[^2] Guidance differs here: NICE advises against starting pregabalin or gabapentin for this kind of chronic pain.[^4]

Strong opioids and steroids are not recommended, and ordinary anti-inflammatory painkillers do little for fibromyalgia pain.[^2][^4]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no known way to prevent fibromyalgia. Day to day, what helps most is regular gentle activity, pacing (spreading tasks out and resting before you are exhausted), relaxation and a consistent sleep routine.[^3] [Sleep hygiene habits](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) and [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) are good starting points.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have had widespread pain for three months or more, especially with fatigue and poor sleep. Go back if new symptoms appear that do not fit your usual pattern, such as swollen joints, fever, unexplained weight loss or muscle weakness. Do not assume that everything is fibromyalgia.

Long-term pain and low mood often go together. If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^7]
