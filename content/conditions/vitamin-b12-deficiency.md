## What it is

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is needed to make red blood cells and DNA and to keep nerves working. It occurs naturally only in animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy, and is added to some fortified foods.[^1]

Absorbing it takes two steps. Stomach acid frees B12 from food, then a stomach protein called intrinsic factor carries it across the gut wall. A problem with either step, or a diet without B12, can cause deficiency. Because the body stores a large reserve, symptoms can take several years to appear.[^1]

Deficiency causes two main problems: anemia with unusually large red blood cells, and nerve damage. Nerve symptoms can occur without anemia and may become permanent if treatment is delayed.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

The anemia causes tiredness, weakness, breathlessness, palpitations and pale or slightly yellow skin.[^4] A sore, red tongue and mouth ulcers are common.[^2]

Nerve-related symptoms include pins and needles or numbness in the hands and feet, unsteady walking, memory problems, low mood, confusion and changes in vision.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

- **Pernicious anemia**, an autoimmune condition that stops the stomach making intrinsic factor. It is the most common cause in the UK.[^2]
- **Older age.** Many older adults make less stomach acid and absorb B12 from food poorly.[^1]
- **Stomach or bowel surgery**, including weight-loss surgery, and conditions such as celiac disease and Crohn's disease.[^1]
- **Medicines** taken long term, notably metformin and acid-suppressing drugs.[^1]
- **Vegan or near-vegan diets** without fortified foods or supplements. Breastfed babies of mothers who are low in B12 are also at risk.[^1]
- **Recreational nitrous oxide** (laughing gas), which NICE lists as a risk factor.[^3]

## Diagnosis

Doctors start with a blood count and a B12 level. Most laboratories treat a result below about 200 to 250 pg/mL (148 to 185 pmol/L) as low, although cut-offs vary.[^1] A borderline result can be clarified by measuring methylmalonic acid, which rises when B12 is lacking. Antibodies to intrinsic factor point to pernicious anemia.[^1][^3]

Folic acid can improve the anemia while nerve damage quietly continues, so B12 is checked before a folate deficiency is treated.[^2]

## Treatment

Treatment replaces the vitamin and deals with the cause where possible. B12 injections are widely used, especially when absorption is the problem or nerves are affected. High-dose tablets can also work, because a small fraction of a large dose is absorbed without intrinsic factor.[^1]

When the cause cannot be reversed, as in pernicious anemia or after stomach surgery, treatment continues for life.[^2] The anemia usually responds well. Nerve symptoms improve more slowly and sometimes incompletely, which is the argument for treating early.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Adults need 2.4 mcg of B12 a day.[^1] If you eat little or no animal food, use fortified foods or a B12 supplement. US guidance is that people over 50 should get most of their B12 from fortified foods or supplements, since these forms are absorbed more reliably.[^5]

If you take metformin or acid-suppressing medicine long term, ask whether your B12 should be checked.[^1] [Vitamins and Supplements: Who Actually Needs Them?](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them) covers who benefits from which supplement.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor for persistent tiredness, pins and needles, a sore tongue, or changes in memory, mood or balance, and mention any supplements or medicines you take.[^2] [Understanding Your Blood Tests](/articles/understanding-blood-tests) explains the blood count.

Severe anemia strains the heart.[^4] Call your local emergency number for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, sudden loss of vision, or rapidly worsening weakness or confusion.
