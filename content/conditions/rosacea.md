## What it is

Rosacea is a long-term inflammatory skin condition that mainly affects the center of the face: the cheeks, nose, chin and forehead. It causes flushing, lasting redness, visible blood vessels and, in many people, pimple-like bumps.[^1] Symptoms tend to flare for a while and then settle.

It usually appears after age 30 and is diagnosed most often in women and in people with fair skin. It occurs in every skin tone, though, and is easy to miss on darker skin, where redness is less obvious.[^1][^3] The National Rosacea Society estimates that it affects about 16 million people in the United States.[^4] It is not contagious.

## Symptoms

Experts now describe rosacea by its features, which can occur in any combination, rather than by rigid subtypes:[^5]

- lasting redness of the central face
- flushing
- bumps and pimples, without the blackheads seen in acne
- small visible blood vessels
- irritated eyes: dry, gritty, red or watery
- thickened, bumpy skin, usually on the nose, which is more common in men

Skin often stings or burns and reacts badly to products.[^1] On brown and Black skin, look for a dusky or darker discoloration, bumps that acne treatment does not clear, and stinging rather than obvious redness.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not fully understood. Research points to an overreactive immune and blood vessel response in facial skin, an inherited tendency, and possibly a reaction to microscopic mites that live on everyone's skin.[^1][^5]

Flares are often set off by triggers, which vary from person to person. Common ones include sun exposure, hot or cold weather, hot drinks, spicy food, alcohol, stress and strenuous exercise.[^2][^4] Steroid creams used on the face can also bring on or worsen rosacea.[^6]

## Diagnosis

There is no test for rosacea. A doctor or dermatologist diagnoses it from the appearance of your skin and your symptoms, and may check that the redness is not caused by something else, such as acne, eczema or lupus.[^1] If your eyes are affected, you may be referred to an eye specialist.

## Treatment

Rosacea cannot be cured, but treatment controls it. What doctors offer depends on which features you have:[^1][^2][^3]

- **Bumps and pimples:** creams or gels containing metronidazole, azelaic acid or ivermectin, and for more stubborn cases a course of antibiotic tablets such as doxycycline.
- **Lasting redness:** prescription gels or creams, such as brimonidine, that temporarily narrow the blood vessels.
- **Visible blood vessels:** laser or intense pulsed light treatment.
- **Thickened skin:** surgical or laser reshaping.
- **Eye symptoms:** eyelid cleaning, lubricating drops and sometimes antibiotics.

## Prevention and self-care

Rosacea cannot be prevented, but daily habits make flares less frequent:

- Keep a diary to find your triggers, then avoid the ones you can.[^4]
- Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher every day.[^2][^3] Our [sunscreen guide](/articles/sunscreen-guide) explains how to choose one.
- Wash with a mild cleanser, moisturize daily, and avoid scrubs, astringents and anything that stings.[^3] See our [simple skincare routine](/articles/simple-skincare-routine).
- Do not use steroid creams on your face unless a doctor has prescribed them for you.[^6]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have facial redness, flushing or bumps that do not go away. A diagnosis opens the door to prescription treatments, and rosacea tends to be easier to control when treated early.[^3]

Get same-day medical care if you have rosacea and develop eye pain, sensitivity to light or blurred vision. Rosacea can inflame the clear front surface of the eye (the cornea), which can damage sight if it is not treated quickly.[^2]
