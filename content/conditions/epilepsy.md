## What it is

Epilepsy is a long-term brain condition in which a person has a lasting tendency to have seizures: sudden bursts of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. One seizure does not mean epilepsy. The usual definition is two or more unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a high chance of another.[^3]

Around 50 million people worldwide have epilepsy, which makes it one of the most common neurological conditions. With proper diagnosis and treatment, up to 70% of them could live seizure-free.[^1]

## Symptoms

Seizures look different depending on where in the brain they start and how far they spread.[^2]

- **Focal seizures** begin in one area. You may stay aware and notice a strange smell or taste, a rising feeling in the stomach or twitching in one limb. Or awareness may be affected, with staring and repeated movements such as lip smacking.
- **Generalized seizures** involve both sides of the brain from the start. Tonic-clonic seizures cause stiffening, loss of consciousness and rhythmic jerking. Absence seizures cause brief blank spells, mostly in children.

Afterward, many people are confused, sleepy or sore for minutes to hours.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

In about half of cases worldwide, no cause is found. Known causes include genetic conditions, brain damage around the time of birth, serious head injury, stroke, brain infections such as meningitis, and brain tumors.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis depends heavily on a detailed description of what happened, ideally from a witness or a phone video. NICE recommends that anyone with a suspected first seizure is seen by a specialist urgently, within two weeks.[^5]

The specialist may arrange an electroencephalogram (EEG), which records the brain's electrical activity, and an MRI scan to look for a structural cause.[^2] A normal EEG does not rule epilepsy out.[^5]

## Treatment

Anti-seizure medicines are the main treatment. The choice depends on seizure type, age, sex, other conditions and other medicines.[^5] Sodium valproate, for example, can seriously harm an unborn baby, so its use is tightly restricted in women and girls who could become pregnant.[^5]

Do not stop medication suddenly. Any change should be planned with your doctor, because withdrawal can bring seizures back.[^2]

If seizures continue despite medicines, specialist options include epilepsy surgery, nerve stimulation devices such as vagus nerve stimulation, and a medically supervised ketogenic diet, used mainly in children.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

WHO estimates that about a quarter of epilepsy cases are preventable, by preventing head injuries, improving care around birth, and preventing strokes and brain infections.[^1]

If you have epilepsy, taking medication on schedule, getting enough sleep and limiting alcohol reduce the risk of seizures.[^2] Sensible precautions help too: shower rather than bathe, and do not swim alone.[^6] Driving rules vary by country.

Seizure first aid is worth knowing. Stay with the person and time the seizure, move them only if they are in danger, cushion their head, and roll them onto their side once the jerking stops. Do not put anything in their mouth.[^4] [First Aid Basics](/articles/first-aid-basics) covers more.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor promptly after any suspected seizure or unexplained blackout, and if seizures change or become more frequent on treatment.

Anxiety and depression are more common in people with epilepsy, so mention low mood too.[^1] If you ever think about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away.

Call your local emergency number if:[^4]

- a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes
- it is the person's first seizure
- one seizure follows another without the person regaining awareness
- the person is badly injured, has trouble breathing or does not fully come round afterward
