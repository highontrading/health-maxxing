## What it is

Hives are raised, itchy welts on the skin. They appear when immune cells in the skin called mast cells release histamine and other chemicals, which makes small blood vessels leak fluid.[^4] Each welt usually fades within 24 hours and leaves no mark, although new ones can keep appearing elsewhere.[^4]

Hives are very common: about 1 in 5 people have an episode at some point in their lives.[^4] Doctors call hives acute when they clear within six weeks, as most do, and chronic when they keep coming back for longer.[^4] They are not contagious.

Deeper swelling, called angioedema, can occur with hives or on its own. It mostly affects the lips, eyelids, face, hands, feet or genitals, tends to feel tight or painful more than itchy, and can last up to three days.[^4]

## Symptoms

Welts range from pinhead-sized spots to patches as large as a dinner plate, and they can merge. They often sting or burn as well as itch. On lighter skin they look pink or red. On brown and Black skin they may match your skin tone or be slightly lighter or darker, so the raised outline is the best clue.[^1][^2]

## Causes and risk factors

Short-lived hives are often set off by:[^1][^2][^3]

- infections such as colds and other viral illnesses
- foods, for example nuts, shellfish, eggs and milk
- medicines, including some antibiotics, aspirin and ibuprofen
- insect bites and stings
- contact with latex, plants or animals
- physical triggers such as pressure, scratching, cold, heat, sweat, sunlight or exercise

In many cases no trigger is ever found. That is especially true of chronic hives, which are rarely an allergy to something external. In many people the immune system itself keeps the mast cells firing.[^4]

## Diagnosis

Doctors diagnose hives from the look of the rash and your account of it. Photos help, because welts may be gone by the time of your appointment. A single short episode usually needs no tests, and allergy testing is useful only when your history points to a specific trigger.[^4] For chronic hives, international guidelines recommend a small set of blood tests, not broad allergy panels.[^4]

## Treatment

Mild hives often clear on their own within a few days.[^2] When treatment is needed, the options are:[^2][^4]

- **Non-sedating antihistamines** such as cetirizine, loratadine or fexofenadine, used as directed on the label. They are the first-line treatment in international guidelines, and for chronic hives doctors may prescribe higher doses.
- **A short course of steroid tablets** for a severe flare.
- **Omalizumab,** an injected antibody medicine, for chronic hives that antihistamines do not control, with cyclosporine as a later option.

Chronic hives usually go away on their own eventually, but that can take months or years. The aim of treatment is to keep you free of symptoms in the meantime.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

If you know your trigger, avoid it. A diary of foods, medicines, activities and stresses can reveal patterns. During an outbreak:[^1]

- cool the skin with a fan or a cool compress, unless cold is one of your triggers
- wear loose cotton clothing
- try not to scratch

Aspirin and anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen can make hives worse in some people.[^4] If foods seem to be involved, [Food Intolerance vs. Food Allergy](/articles/food-intolerance-vs-food-allergy) explains how true allergies are identified.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if hives have not improved after two days, keep coming back, are spreading, or come with a fever, feeling unwell or swelling under the skin.[^2] Welts that hurt, last longer than a day or leave bruise-like marks also need a check, because they can point to a different condition.[^4]

Call your local emergency number if hives come with difficulty breathing, wheezing, swelling of the tongue or throat, trouble swallowing, repeated vomiting, dizziness or fainting. These are signs of [anaphylaxis](/conditions/anaphylaxis). If you have been prescribed an epinephrine (adrenaline) auto-injector, use it straight away.[^2]
