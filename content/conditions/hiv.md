## What it is

HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) attacks the immune system, gradually destroying the CD4 cells that help the body fight infection. Without treatment it can lead to AIDS, the most advanced stage, when the body can no longer fend off serious illnesses.[^1][^3]

About 40 million people worldwide are living with HIV.[^1] There is no cure yet, but with early diagnosis and effective treatment, most people with HIV never develop AIDS-related illness and can expect a near-normal lifespan.[^5]

## Symptoms

Within two to four weeks of acquiring HIV, many people have a short flu-like illness with fever, sore throat, rash, swollen glands, muscle aches and tiredness. Others notice nothing.[^2]

HIV may then cause no symptoms for years while it damages the immune system. Without treatment, weight loss, night sweats, long-lasting diarrhea and repeated or unusual infections eventually follow.[^5]

Testing is the only way to know your status.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

HIV is passed on through certain body fluids of a person who has a detectable amount of the virus: blood, semen, rectal and vaginal fluids, and breast milk.[^2] Most transmission happens through vaginal or anal sex without a condom or preventive medicine, through shared needles or injecting equipment, or from mother to baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding.[^1][^2]

HIV is not passed on by hugging, shaking hands, kissing, or sharing food, dishes or toilets, nor by insects or through the air.[^1][^2]

## Diagnosis

HIV is diagnosed with a test on blood or oral fluid: a rapid test or self-test, which gives a result in 30 minutes or less, or a laboratory test.[^6] No test detects HIV immediately. Depending on the type, tests pick it up from about 10 to 90 days after exposure, so a negative result soon after a possible exposure may need repeating.[^6]

The CDC recommends at least one test for everyone aged 13 to 64 as part of routine care, and yearly testing for people with ongoing risk.[^6]

## Treatment

HIV is treated with antiretroviral therapy (ART), a combination of medicines taken as daily pills, often a single tablet, or for some people as long-acting injections. It is recommended for everyone with HIV, starting as soon as possible after diagnosis.[^1][^2]

ART stops the virus from multiplying. Within about six months, most people reach an undetectable viral load: too little virus in the blood for a standard test to measure.[^2][^4] Treatment is lifelong, with regular blood tests to check it is working.

A person with HIV who takes treatment and keeps an undetectable viral load does not pass the virus on through sex. This is known as undetectable = untransmittable, or U=U.[^1][^4]

## Prevention and self-care

- **Condoms,** used correctly every time you have sex.[^7]
- **PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis):** pills or long-acting injections for people who do not have HIV. Taken as prescribed, PrEP reduces the risk of getting HIV from sex by about 99%.[^7]
- **PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis):** a 28-day course of medicine started within 72 hours of a possible exposure.[^7]
- **Sterile injecting equipment,** never shared.[^7]
- **Treatment in pregnancy:** with HIV medicine during pregnancy and birth, and for the baby afterward, the chance of passing HIV on can fall below 1%.[^2]

If you are living with HIV, taking treatment as prescribed and keeping clinic appointments matters most. HIV also raises the risk of [tuberculosis](/conditions/tuberculosis), so ask about screening.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

Get tested if you have never had an HIV test, if you may have been exposed, or if a flu-like illness follows a possible exposure.

If you think you were exposed in the past 72 hours, go to an emergency department or sexual health clinic now and ask about PEP. The sooner it starts, the better it works.[^7]

If you have HIV and are not on effective treatment, seek urgent care for shortness of breath, a persistent fever, severe headache, confusion or changes in vision.
