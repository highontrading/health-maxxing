## What it is

Gum disease is an infection of the tissues that hold your teeth in place. It starts with plaque, a sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth every day. Plaque that is not brushed away hardens into tartar, which only a dental professional can remove.[^1]

There are two stages. Gingivitis is inflammation of the gums alone and can be reversed. Periodontitis is the advanced stage, in which the gums pull away from the teeth and the bone that supports them breaks down. That damage is permanent, but treatment can stop it going further.[^1]

It is very common. Severe gum disease affects about 19% of adults worldwide, more than 1 billion people.[^2] In a national US survey, 42% of adults aged 30 and older who had teeth had periodontitis.[^4]

## Symptoms

Gum disease is often painless at first. Early signs are red, swollen gums that bleed when you brush, floss or bite into hard food.[^3] As it progresses you may notice bad breath that does not go away, gums that have pulled back so teeth look longer, sensitive or loose teeth and pain when chewing.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Plaque build-up is the cause. Several things make gum disease more likely or more severe:[^1]

- **Smoking**, the most significant risk factor. It also makes treatment less successful.
- **Diabetes.**
- **Hormonal changes** in girls and women, including pregnancy.
- **Medicines that reduce saliva**, since a dry mouth protects teeth and gums less well.
- **Genes** and conditions that weaken the immune system.

## Diagnosis

A dentist or hygienist examines your gums and uses a thin probe to measure the pockets between gum and tooth. In a healthy mouth these are 1 to 3 mm deep. Deeper pockets suggest periodontitis, and X-rays show whether bone has been lost.[^1]

## Treatment

Gingivitis usually clears with a professional cleaning followed by thorough brushing and cleaning between the teeth at home.[^1]

Periodontitis needs more. The first step is a deep cleaning called scaling and root planing, which removes tartar from above and below the gum line and smooths the rough spots on the roots where bacteria collect. Dentists sometimes add an antimicrobial mouth rinse, a gel placed in the pockets or a short course of antibiotics. If deep pockets remain, a gum specialist (periodontist) may advise surgery to clean the roots or rebuild lost bone and gum.[^1]

No treatment holds without daily cleaning at home and regular maintenance visits. Stopping smoking improves the results.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

- Brush twice a day for about two minutes with fluoride toothpaste.[^3]
- Clean between your teeth every day with floss or interdental brushes.[^3]
- See a dentist or hygienist regularly, particularly if you are pregnant or have diabetes.[^3]
- Do not smoke.[^1]

People with gum disease have higher rates of heart disease, but that does not prove one causes the other. An American Heart Association review found an association in observational studies and no evidence that treating gum disease prevents heart attacks or strokes.[^5] Healthy gums are worth having for their own sake. ## When to see a doctor

See a dentist if your gums bleed, are swollen or sore, or have started to recede, or if you have bad breath that will not shift. Bleeding gums are not normal, even if they do not hurt.[^3]

Ask for an urgent dental appointment if your gums are very sore and swollen, you have mouth ulcers or teeth are becoming loose.[^3] Go to an emergency department if swelling spreads to your face, eye or neck, or you have trouble breathing, swallowing or opening your mouth. These are signs of a spreading infection.[^6]
