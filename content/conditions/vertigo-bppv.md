## What it is

Vertigo is the false feeling that you or the room is spinning.[^5] Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is its most common cause.[^1] The name describes it well: benign (not dangerous in itself), paroxysmal (sudden, brief spells) and positional (set off by changes in head position).

It starts in the inner ear. Tiny calcium crystals that normally sit in one part of the balance organ come loose and drift into one of the fluid-filled semicircular canals. There they send false signals about movement whenever you move your head.[^2]

## Symptoms

Spinning spells start a moment after you move your head and usually last less than a minute.[^1] Classic triggers are rolling over in bed, lying down, sitting up, looking up at a high shelf and bending down. Nausea is common, and you may feel off balance between spells.[^1][^3]

BPPV does not cause hearing loss, fainting, weakness or trouble speaking. Those point to something else.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Often no cause is found. BPPV becomes more common with age, and it can follow a head injury.[^2] Roughly 2 in every 100 people have it at some point in their lives.[^1]

## Diagnosis

A clinician can usually diagnose BPPV at the bedside with the Dix-Hallpike test. You are moved quickly from sitting to lying with your head turned and tilted back, while the examiner watches for vertigo and a characteristic flickering of the eyes called nystagmus. A related test, the supine roll test, checks a different canal.[^1]

When the history and these tests fit, the US guideline recommends against brain scans and balance-function testing.[^1]

## Treatment

The main treatment is a repositioning procedure, most often the Epley maneuver: a sequence of slow head and body positions that guides the loose crystals back to where they belong.[^1] A Cochrane review of 11 trials found it to be a safe and effective treatment for the most common form of BPPV.[^4] It often works within one to three sessions, and you may be taught a version to do at home.[^1]

The guideline advises against routinely treating BPPV with dizziness medicines such as antihistamines or benzodiazepines, which do not fix the cause. It also says there is no need to restrict your head position or sleeping posture after the maneuver.[^1]

Left alone, BPPV often settles over a few weeks, so watchful waiting is a reasonable option. Balance retraining exercises, known as vestibular rehabilitation, are another.[^1]

BPPV can come back. If it does, the same treatment usually works again.[^1][^4]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no reliable way to prevent BPPV. During a bout, lower your risk of falling: get out of bed slowly, sit down as soon as you feel dizzy, turn on the lights if you get up at night, and move your head carefully during daily activities.[^3] The guideline highlights the risk of falls, particularly for older adults.[^1]

[Dizziness and Vertigo](/articles/dizziness-and-vertigo) explains other reasons for spinning and lightheadedness.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if dizziness keeps coming back or does not settle, so that the cause can be confirmed and treated. Ask for an urgent appointment if vertigo comes with a severe headache, repeated vomiting or a high temperature.[^3]

Call your local emergency number if vertigo comes with double vision or loss of vision, hearing loss, trouble speaking, or weakness, numbness or tingling in an arm or leg.[^3] These can be signs of a stroke, which clinicians are told to rule out before settling on BPPV.[^1] A sudden severe headache, being unable to stand or walk, chest pain and fainting are emergencies too.

Continuous vertigo that lasts for hours is not BPPV and needs prompt assessment.
