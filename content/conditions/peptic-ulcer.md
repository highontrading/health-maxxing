## What it is

A peptic ulcer is an open sore in the lining of the stomach (a gastric ulcer) or of the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine (a duodenal ulcer). It forms when the protective layer of mucus breaks down and stomach acid damages the tissue underneath.[^2]

Most ulcers heal within a month or two of treatment.[^2] Left alone, they can bleed, or burn through the wall of the stomach or intestine.[^1]

## Symptoms

The typical symptom is a dull or burning pain anywhere between your navel and breastbone. It often comes on when your stomach is empty, between meals or during the night, eases briefly when you eat or take an antacid, and comes and goes over days or weeks.[^1] Bloating, belching, nausea and poor appetite are less common.[^1]

Some people, particularly older adults taking painkillers, have no warning symptoms and first learn of an ulcer when it bleeds.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Two causes account for most ulcers.[^1]

- **Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)**, a bacterium that lives in the stomach lining. It is a very common infection that is usually picked up in childhood, and most people who carry it never develop an ulcer.[^4]
- **Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**, such as aspirin, ibuprofen and naproxen, which weaken the stomach's protective lining. The risk is higher if you are 70 or older, take them regularly or at high doses, have had an ulcer before, or also take steroids.[^1]

Smoking raises the risk and may make treatment less effective.[^2] Stress and spicy food do not cause ulcers, although they can make symptoms worse.[^1]

## Diagnosis

H. pylori can be detected with a breath test, a stool test or a tissue sample. Acid-suppressing medicines and recent antibiotics can cause false-negative results, so you may be asked to stop them beforehand.[^3]

An upper endoscopy, in which a thin camera is passed through the mouth, shows the ulcer directly and allows samples to be taken. It is usually advised for older adults and for anyone with warning signs such as bleeding, weight loss or trouble swallowing.[^1][^2]

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. Acid-lowering medicine, usually a proton pump inhibitor, lets the ulcer heal.[^1]

If H. pylori is present, it is treated with a combination of acid-lowering medicine and antibiotics, sometimes with bismuth, typically for 14 days. Because antibiotic resistance is common, the American College of Gastroenterology recommends a test at least four weeks after treatment to confirm the infection has gone.[^3]

If an NSAID is the cause, your doctor will advise stopping it or switching if possible, or adding a protective medicine if you need to continue.[^1] Bleeding ulcers are usually treated through an endoscope. Surgery is now rarely needed.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Use NSAIDs at the lowest dose and for the shortest time that controls your symptoms, as directed on the label. If you need them long term and are at higher risk, ask whether you should take a protective medicine too.[^1] Do not stop prescribed low-dose aspirin without medical advice.

Not smoking lowers your risk and may help treatment work.[^2] If you are treated for H. pylori, finish the whole course. [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help) explains why that matters.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have persistent upper belly pain, or indigestion that keeps coming back. Unexplained weight loss, trouble swallowing or repeated vomiting need prompt assessment.[^2]

Call your local emergency number if you vomit blood or material that looks like coffee grounds, pass black or bloody stools, have sudden sharp belly pain that does not go away, or feel faint. These are signs of a bleeding or perforated ulcer.[^1]
