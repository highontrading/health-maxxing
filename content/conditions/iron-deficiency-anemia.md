## What it is

Iron-deficiency anemia develops when your body does not have enough iron to make hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. With less hemoglobin, your tissues get less oxygen and you tire easily. It is the most common type of anemia.[^2]

The World Health Organization estimates that anemia affects about 30% of women aged 15 to 49 and 37% of pregnant women worldwide, and iron deficiency is its most common nutritional cause.[^1]

## Symptoms

Mild anemia may cause no symptoms. As it worsens, the most common are:[^3]

- Tiredness and lack of energy.
- Shortness of breath.
- Noticeable heartbeats (palpitations).
- Paler skin than usual.
- Headaches.

Less common signs include a sore tongue, sores at the corners of the mouth, hair loss, spoon-shaped nails, restless legs and cravings for ice or non-food items such as paper.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Iron deficiency is a finding, not a final diagnosis. Something is causing it:[^2][^4]

- **Blood loss.** Heavy periods are the most common cause in women who menstruate. In men, and in women after menopause, the usual cause is slow bleeding from the stomach or bowel, for example from ulcers, anti-inflammatory pain relievers or, less often, cancer.[^3][^4]
- **Higher needs** during pregnancy and growth.
- **Low intake or poor absorption**, including diets low in iron, [celiac disease](/conditions/celiac-disease) and stomach or bowel surgery.

## Diagnosis

A complete blood count shows low hemoglobin and small, pale red cells. The WHO defines anemia as hemoglobin below 13 g/dL in men and 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women.[^4] Ferritin, a measure of stored iron, confirms that iron is the cause, although inflammation can raise ferritin and mask a deficiency.[^4] [Understanding Your Blood Tests](/articles/understanding-blood-tests) explains these results.

Because bleeding in the gut can be silent, the British Society of Gastroenterology (2021) recommends that men and postmenopausal women with newly diagnosed iron-deficiency anemia are tested for celiac disease and usually offered camera tests of the stomach and bowel.[^4]

## Treatment

Two things happen in parallel: finding and treating the cause, and replacing the iron.[^4]

- **Iron tablets** are the usual first step. They are taken for several months and continued after hemoglobin recovers, so that iron stores are rebuilt. Constipation, nausea and dark stools are common side effects.[^3][^4]
- **Intravenous iron** is used when tablets are not tolerated or do not work.[^4]
- **Blood transfusion** is reserved for severe cases.[^4]

Do not start iron on your own just because you feel tired. Too much iron can be harmful, and self-treatment can hide a problem that needs investigating. Keep supplements out of children's reach, because an iron overdose can be fatal for a child.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

Red meat, poultry and fish contain the form of iron your body absorbs best. Beans, lentils, tofu, spinach, nuts and fortified cereals provide iron too, and eating them with vitamin C-rich foods improves absorption.[^5] Large amounts of tea, coffee and dairy make iron harder to absorb.[^3]

US recommended intakes are 8 mg a day for men and for women over 50, 18 mg for women aged 19 to 50, and 27 mg in pregnancy.[^5] [Vitamins and Supplements: Who Actually Needs Them?](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them) covers when a supplement makes sense.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have ongoing tiredness, breathlessness or paleness, or [heavy periods](/articles/period-pain-and-heavy-periods). A simple blood test will show whether iron is the problem.[^3] Get checked promptly if you notice blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss or a lasting change in bowel habits.

**Seek emergency care** for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, vomiting blood, or black, tarry stools with dizziness or weakness.
