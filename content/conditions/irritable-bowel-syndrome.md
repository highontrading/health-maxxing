## What it is

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a group of symptoms that occur together: repeated belly pain along with changes in your bowel movements, without any visible damage or disease in the digestive tract.[^1]

Doctors class it as a disorder of gut-brain interaction. The gut and brain communicate differently, which makes the gut more sensitive and changes how its muscles contract.[^1] The symptoms are real and can be disruptive, but IBS does not damage the bowel.[^1]

It is common. Estimates for the US range from about 4% to 12% of people, depending on how strictly it is defined.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

The core symptom is belly pain that is usually related to bowel movements, together with a change in how often you go or how your stools look. Bloating, a feeling of not having finished and whitish mucus in the stool are also common.[^1]

IBS is grouped by the main bowel pattern: constipation (IBS-C), diarrhea (IBS-D) or mixed (IBS-M).[^2] Symptoms come and go, and many women find they are worse around their periods.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The cause is not fully understood. IBS is more common in women, in people under 50 and in those with a family member who has it.[^1] It is also more likely after a bowel infection, in people who went through stressful or difficult events early in life, and alongside anxiety or depression. Food intolerances and changes in gut bacteria may play a part.[^1]

## Diagnosis

IBS is diagnosed from the pattern of symptoms. Under the Rome IV criteria it means belly pain on average at least one day a week over the past three months, with at least two of these: the pain is related to bowel movements, you go more or less often, or your stools look different. Symptoms must have begun at least six months earlier.[^2]

The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends a positive diagnosis based on symptoms and a few targeted tests, not exhaustive testing. These are a blood test for celiac disease and, when diarrhea is the main symptom, blood and stool tests for inflammation to rule out inflammatory bowel disease.[^2] UK guidance similarly advises a blood count, an inflammation marker and celiac antibodies.[^4] A colonoscopy is not routinely needed unless you have warning signs or are due for bowel cancer screening.[^2]

## Treatment

Treatment is matched to your main symptoms. The ACG guideline supports:[^2]

- **Soluble fiber**, such as psyllium, for overall symptoms.
- **A limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet**, which temporarily cuts down certain fermentable carbohydrates.
- **Peppermint oil** for overall symptoms.
- **Prescription medicines** aimed at constipation or diarrhea, and low doses of tricyclic antidepressants, which act on gut nerve signals.
- **Gut-directed psychological therapy**, such as cognitive behavioral therapy or hypnotherapy.

The ACG suggests against probiotics for overall IBS symptoms, because the evidence is weak and inconsistent.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no known way to prevent IBS, but everyday habits can ease it. The NHS suggests eating regular meals without rushing, keeping a diary of what you eat and how you feel, staying active, finding ways to relax, and cutting down on fatty, spicy and processed foods, alcohol, fizzy drinks and caffeine.[^3]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor before deciding you have IBS, because [celiac disease](/conditions/celiac-disease) and [inflammatory bowel disease](/conditions/inflammatory-bowel-disease) can look similar.

Ask for an urgent appointment if you have blood in your stool or bleeding from your rectum, unexplained weight loss, a hard lump or swelling in your belly, or breathlessness with pale skin, which can be a sign of anemia.[^3] Symptoms that start after 50, diarrhea that wakes you at night, a fever, or a family history of bowel cancer or inflammatory bowel disease also need checking.[^1][^2]
