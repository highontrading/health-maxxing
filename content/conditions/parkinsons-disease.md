## What it is

Parkinson's disease is a progressive condition of the brain. Nerve cells in a region called the substantia nigra, which make the chemical messenger dopamine, are gradually lost, and movement becomes slow, stiff and shaky as a result.[^2]

WHO reports that disability and death from Parkinson's are rising faster than for any other neurological disorder. Its prevalence has doubled in 25 years, and more than 8.5 million people were living with it in 2019.[^1] It mainly affects older adults and is more common in men than in women, though younger people can develop it too.[^1]

## Symptoms

The three main movement symptoms are slowness of movement, a tremor that appears at rest, and muscle stiffness.[^5] They usually start on one side of the body.[^2] Handwriting may shrink, the voice may soften, the face may become less expressive and steps may shorten into a shuffle. Balance problems tend to come later.[^2]

Parkinson's is more than a movement disorder. Loss of smell, constipation, acting out dreams during sleep, depression, anxiety and fatigue are common and can appear years before any tremor.[^2] Problems with thinking and memory may develop in later stages.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

In most people the cause is unknown and is thought to be a mix of genes and environment. Age is the biggest risk factor. A minority of cases are linked to specific gene changes, and exposure to pesticides, industrial solvents and air pollution may raise risk.[^1][^2]

## Diagnosis

No blood test or routine scan confirms Parkinson's. A specialist makes the diagnosis from your history and an examination, looking for slowness of movement together with rest tremor, stiffness or both.[^3] NICE advises that people with suspected Parkinson's are referred to a specialist quickly, before any treatment is started.[^4]

A clear improvement on levodopa supports the diagnosis.[^3] A specialized brain scan that images dopamine nerve endings is sometimes used when it is hard to tell Parkinson's from other causes of tremor.[^4]

## Treatment

No treatment has yet been shown to slow the disease, but symptoms can be treated effectively.[^2] Levodopa, given with a companion drug such as carbidopa, is the main medicine and the most effective for movement symptoms.[^2][^4] Other options include dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors and COMT inhibitors, chosen according to your symptoms, age and daily life.[^4]

After some years, doses may wear off sooner and involuntary movements (dyskinesia) can appear.[^2] Dopamine agonists in particular can cause impulse control problems such as compulsive gambling, shopping or eating. Tell your doctor if you notice them.[^4]

When tablets no longer control symptoms, specialists may offer deep brain stimulation or medicine given by continuous infusion.[^4] Physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy are part of standard care.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no proven way to prevent Parkinson's. If you have it, staying physically active matters: strength, walking and balance training can improve function and quality of life.[^1] [Balance and fall prevention](/articles/balance-and-fall-prevention) has practical drills.

Never stop Parkinson's medicines abruptly. Sudden withdrawal can cause a dangerous reaction with severe stiffness and fever. If illness or surgery means you cannot take or absorb them, get medical advice the same day.[^4]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor about a persistent tremor, or about movements that have become slow or stiff. Loss of smell together with acting out dreams is also worth mentioning. If you have Parkinson's, tell your team about low mood or anxiety, which are part of the condition and can be treated.[^2]

Seek emergency care for high fever with severe stiffness and confusion, for being unable to swallow or take your medication, for a fall with a head injury or possible broken bone, or for sudden severe confusion or hallucinations.
