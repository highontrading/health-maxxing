## What it is

Your sinuses are air-filled spaces behind your cheeks, forehead and nose. Sinusitis is inflammation of their lining. The swelling blocks the narrow drainage channels, mucus builds up and pressure follows.[^1]

Doctors divide it by duration: acute sinusitis lasts up to four weeks, and chronic sinusitis lasts 12 weeks or longer.[^2] Most acute cases are caused by the same viruses as the common cold. Bacteria complicate only a small share, up to about 2 in 100 cases.[^2][^4]

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms are a blocked nose, thick yellow or green mucus from the nose or down the back of the throat, and pain or pressure around the cheeks, eyes or forehead. A reduced sense of smell, headache, toothache, bad breath, cough and fever are also common.[^3][^1]

The color of the mucus does not show whether the cause is a virus or bacteria.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

Sinusitis usually follows a cold. It is more likely if you have seasonal allergies, smoke or breathe secondhand smoke, have a structural problem in the nose such as polyps, or have a weakened immune system.[^1]

Chronic sinusitis is driven more by long-lasting inflammation than by infection, and often occurs alongside allergies, asthma or nasal polyps.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Sinusitis is diagnosed from your symptoms and an examination. Doctors suspect a bacterial infection when symptoms last 10 days or more without improving, or when they start to improve and then get worse again.[^2]

Scans are not needed for straightforward acute sinusitis. For chronic symptoms, a specialist may look inside the nose with a thin endoscope or arrange a CT scan to confirm inflammation and look for polyps.[^2]

## Treatment

Acute sinusitis usually lasts two to three weeks and gets better by itself.[^4] Treatment is about comfort:

- Pain relievers such as acetaminophen (paracetamol) or ibuprofen, taken as directed on the label.[^3]
- Saltwater (saline) nasal sprays or rinses, and a warm compress over the nose and forehead.[^1]
- A steroid nasal spray, which may help some people.[^2]
- Decongestant sprays for a few days at most. US specialist guidance advises no more than three to five days in a row, because longer use causes rebound congestion.[^2]

Antibiotics are not needed for most sinus infections. They do not work against viruses and can cause side effects.[^1] Even when a bacterial infection is suspected in an otherwise healthy adult, US specialist guidance supports either watchful waiting or an antibiotic, usually amoxicillin with or without clavulanate.[^2] UK guidance advises no antibiotic for symptoms lasting 10 days or less.[^4]

For chronic sinusitis, the mainstays are daily saline rinses and steroid nasal sprays. Surgery to open the drainage channels is an option when these are not enough.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Wash your hands, keep up with recommended vaccines such as the flu vaccine, avoid tobacco smoke and treat allergies.[^1] Rest and drink plenty of fluids while you recover.[^3]

If you rinse your sinuses, use only distilled, sterile or previously boiled and cooled water, never water straight from the tap, and clean the device after each use. Tap water can carry organisms that are harmless to swallow but dangerous in the nose.[^5]

See [Cold, Flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid) to compare symptoms, and [Antibiotics: When They Help and When They Hurt](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have severe facial pain or headache, symptoms that get worse after starting to improve, symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement, a fever for more than three to four days, or several sinus infections in a year.[^1]

Get emergency care for swelling or redness around an eye, double vision or other changes in vision, a severe headache with a stiff neck, swelling of the forehead or confusion. These can mean the infection is spreading toward the eye or brain.[^2]
