## What it is

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition marked by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that interferes with daily functioning or development. It begins in childhood and often continues into adult life.[^1]

There are three presentations: mainly inattentive, mainly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined.[^1] ADHD is common. In the United States, about 1 in 9 children aged 3 to 17 have been diagnosed with it at some point.[^2]

## Symptoms

**Inattention** shows up as trouble staying focused, careless mistakes, not following through, disorganization, losing things, forgetfulness and being easily distracted. **Hyperactivity and impulsivity** show up as fidgeting, restlessness, trouble staying seated, talking a lot, interrupting and finding it hard to wait.[^1]

Everyone does these things sometimes. In ADHD they are frequent, long-lasting, present in more than one setting (such as home and school or work) and cause real problems.[^1] In adults, hyperactivity often turns into restlessness, and disorganization and poor time management become the main difficulties.[^1] NICE notes that ADHD is under-recognized in girls and women.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

ADHD runs strongly in families, and genes are the largest known contributor.[^2] Other factors linked with higher risk include premature birth, low birth weight, exposure to alcohol or tobacco during pregnancy, lead exposure in early childhood, and brain injury.[^2]

Research does not support the popular ideas that ADHD is caused by eating too much sugar, watching too much television or poor parenting.[^2]

## Diagnosis

No blood test or brain scan can diagnose ADHD. Diagnosis requires a full assessment by a trained clinician, including a detailed history, rating scales and information from parents, teachers, partners or others who know the person well.[^1][^3]

The criteria require several symptoms to have been present before age 12, in two or more settings, with clear evidence that they interfere with functioning.[^1] The clinician also checks for conditions that can mimic or accompany ADHD, such as anxiety, depression, sleep problems and learning disorders.[^1]

## Treatment

Treatment is tailored to age and to how much symptoms affect daily life.

- **Young children.** For children under 6, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends parent training in behavior management first, before medication.[^4]
- **School-age children and teenagers.** US guidelines recommend medication together with behavioral support at home and at school.[^4] NICE in the UK reserves medication for children whose symptoms still cause significant problems after changes to their environment.[^3]
- **Adults.** NICE recommends medication when symptoms still cause significant impairment after environmental changes, with psychological approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy as an addition or an alternative.[^3]

Stimulants (methylphenidate and amphetamine-based medicines) are the most widely used medicines. Non-stimulant options include atomoxetine and guanfacine.[^1][^3] Clinicians monitor heart rate and blood pressure during treatment, and height and weight in children.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no known way to prevent ADHD, but daily structure makes it easier to live with: routines, written lists and reminders, a fixed place for keys and other essentials, and large tasks broken into smaller steps.[^1] Regular physical activity and enough sleep help too.[^2] These habits support treatment. They do not replace it. [ADHD in Adults](/articles/adhd-in-adults) covers adult diagnosis and day-to-day strategies.

## When to see a doctor

Ask for an assessment if inattention, restlessness or impulsivity have been present since childhood and are causing problems at school, at work or in relationships. Online quizzes can prompt the conversation, but they cannot diagnose ADHD.

Get urgent medical advice for chest pain, fainting or severe agitation while taking ADHD medication. If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^5]
