## What it is

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common lung disease that restricts airflow and makes breathing difficult. It covers two overlapping problems: chronic bronchitis, in which the airways are inflamed, narrowed and clogged with mucus, and emphysema, in which the tiny air sacs are damaged.[^1][^3]

COPD develops slowly and usually shows itself from midlife. It is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, responsible for more than 3 million deaths a year.[^1] It cannot be cured, but it can be prevented and treated, and removing the cause slows the damage.[^1]

## Symptoms

- Breathlessness, at first only with exertion.
- A persistent cough, often with phlegm.
- Wheezing and chest tightness.
- Frequent lung infections.
- Tiredness.[^1][^4]

Symptoms usually worsen gradually, with flare-ups (exacerbations) that last days or weeks and are often set off by infections.[^1] Many people put early symptoms down to a "smoker's cough" or aging, which delays diagnosis.[^4]

## Causes and risk factors

COPD is caused by long-term exposure to things that irritate and damage the lungs:[^1]

- **Tobacco smoke**, including secondhand smoke. Smoking accounts for more than 70% of cases in high-income countries.
- **Household air pollution** from burning wood, crop waste or coal for cooking and heating. It is a major cause in low- and middle-income countries, where smoking accounts for 30 to 40% of cases.
- **Dusts, fumes and chemicals** at work, and outdoor air pollution.
- **Early-life factors**, such as poor lung growth, prematurity and frequent childhood lung infections.
- **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency**, a rare inherited condition that can cause COPD at a young age.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires spirometry, a breathing test that measures how much air you can blow out and how fast. COPD is confirmed when the ratio of two of its measurements (FEV1/FVC) stays below 0.7 after a dose of bronchodilator.[^2] Chest imaging, oxygen levels and a blood test for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency may follow.[^2][^3]

## Treatment

- **Stopping smoking** is the most effective way to slow the disease.[^2][^4] [How to Quit Smoking (and Vaping)](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) ranks the methods by evidence.
- **Inhaled bronchodilators**, usually long-acting ones, relax the airways and are the mainstay of medication. An inhaled corticosteroid is added for some people who have frequent flare-ups.[^2]
- **Pulmonary rehabilitation**, a program of supervised exercise and education, improves breathlessness, fitness and quality of life.[^2][^4]
- **Vaccines** against flu, pneumococcal disease, COVID-19 and RSV reduce the infections that drive flare-ups.[^2]
- **Long-term oxygen** helps people whose blood oxygen is persistently very low. Surgery or valve procedures suit a small number of people.[^2][^3]

Flare-ups are treated with extra inhaled medicine, short courses of steroid tablets and sometimes antibiotics.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Not smoking, and avoiding smoke, dust and fumes at home and at work, are the main ways to prevent COPD.[^1] If you have it, stay as active as you can, keep vaccinations up to date (see [Vaccines for Adults](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide)), learn correct inhaler technique, and agree on a plan with your care team for what to do when symptoms flare.[^4][^5]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have a cough that will not go away, breathlessness that is getting worse or frequent lung infections, particularly if you are over 35 and smoke or used to smoke.[^4]

**Call your local emergency number** if you are struggling to breathe or too breathless to talk, your lips or fingernails turn blue or gray, you become confused or very drowsy, or you have chest pain.[^3][^5]
