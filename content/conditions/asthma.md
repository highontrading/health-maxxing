## What it is

Asthma is a long-term condition in which the airways are inflamed and extra-sensitive. When something irritates them, the muscles around them tighten, the lining swells and mucus builds up, so less air gets through. Symptoms come and go, and can flare into an asthma attack.[^3]

Asthma affected an estimated 262 million people worldwide in 2019 and is the most common chronic disease among children.[^1] It cannot be cured, but with good treatment most people can lead a normal, active life.[^1]

## Symptoms

- Wheezing, a whistling sound when you breathe.
- Shortness of breath.
- A tight chest.
- Coughing.[^3][^4]

Symptoms are often worse at night and early in the morning, vary from day to day and tend to follow a trigger.[^2][^4]

## Causes and risk factors

Why some people develop asthma is not fully understood. It is more likely if you have a family history of asthma or have allergic conditions such as [eczema](/conditions/eczema) or [hay fever](/conditions/allergic-rhinitis).[^1][^4]

Common triggers include colds and flu, pollen, dust mites, animal fur, mold, tobacco smoke, air pollution, cold air and exercise. Some people react to anti-inflammatory pain relievers such as aspirin and ibuprofen.[^4]

## Diagnosis

There is no single test. Doctors combine your history with breathing tests that show airflow changing over time:[^2][^4]

- **Spirometry**, which measures how much air you can blow out and how fast, before and after a reliever medicine.
- **Peak flow** readings recorded at home over a couple of weeks.
- **FeNO**, a breath test that detects inflammation in the airways.

Diagnosis is harder in young children, who cannot do these tests reliably.[^4]

## Treatment

Most treatment is inhaled, and inhalers do two jobs:[^2][^3]

- **Preventers (controllers)** contain an inhaled corticosteroid that calms airway inflammation and lowers the risk of attacks.
- **Relievers** open the airways quickly when symptoms occur.

The Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) no longer recommends treating adults and adolescents with a short-acting reliever alone, because that leaves the inflammation untreated and raises the risk of severe attacks. Its preferred approach is a single combination inhaler, containing a low-dose corticosteroid and formoterol, used as needed for relief and, if required, as daily treatment too.[^2]

If asthma is still poorly controlled, doctors check inhaler technique and whether doses are being missed before stepping up to higher doses, add-on medicines or, for severe asthma, biologic injections.[^2] Everyone with asthma should have a written action plan that sets out daily treatment and what to do when symptoms worsen.[^3][^4]

## Prevention and self-care

- Use your preventer as prescribed, even when you feel well.
- Ask a nurse or pharmacist to check your inhaler technique.
- Avoid your triggers where practical, do not smoke and get a flu vaccine each year.[^4][^5] If pollen sets you off, our guide to [seasonal allergy relief](/articles/seasonal-allergies-relief) can help.
- Stay active. Well-controlled asthma should not stop you from exercising.[^5]

Waking at night with symptoms, or needing your reliever more than usual, means your asthma is not well controlled and your treatment needs reviewing.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have symptoms that could be asthma, or if you have asthma and your symptoms are getting worse or more frequent.[^4]

**An asthma attack can be life-threatening. Call your local emergency number if:**[^3][^4]

- Your reliever is not helping, or its effect does not last.
- You are too breathless to speak, eat or sleep.
- Your breathing is getting faster and you cannot catch your breath.
- Your lips or fingertips turn blue or gray, or you feel drowsy, confused or exhausted.

While you wait, sit upright, try to stay calm and follow the emergency steps in your action plan.[^4]
