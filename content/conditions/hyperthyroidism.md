## What it is

Your thyroid, a small gland at the front of your neck, makes hormones that set the pace of your metabolism. In hyperthyroidism it makes more than you need, and the body runs fast: the heart, gut and nervous system all speed up.[^1]

About 1 in 100 Americans aged 12 and older have it. It is more common in women and in people over 60.[^1]

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms are weight loss despite a normal or bigger appetite, a fast or irregular heartbeat, nervousness, irritability, shaky hands, sweating, trouble tolerating heat, more frequent bowel movements, tiredness, muscle weakness and poor sleep. The thyroid may enlarge into a visible swelling called a goiter.[^1][^2]

Graves' disease can also affect the eyes, making them bulge or feel gritty, red and sore.[^5] Older adults often have fewer classic signs: loss of appetite, low mood or a new irregular heartbeat may be the only clues.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

The most common cause is Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition in which antibodies push the thyroid to overproduce.[^5] Other causes are overactive lumps in the gland (nodules), inflammation that makes the thyroid leak stored hormone (thyroiditis, usually temporary), too much iodine from certain medicines or supplements, and too high a dose of thyroid hormone medicine.[^1]

Your risk is higher if thyroid disease runs in your family, if you have another autoimmune condition such as type 1 diabetes or pernicious anemia, or if you were recently pregnant. Smoking raises the risk of Graves' disease, and especially of its eye problems.[^1][^5]

## Diagnosis

Blood tests show a low level of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) together with high levels of the thyroid hormones T4, T3 or both.[^2] To find the cause, doctors may test for thyroid antibodies, measure how much radioactive iodine the gland takes up, or use an ultrasound scan.[^4]

## Treatment

There are three main treatments, and the American Thyroid Association regards all three as reasonable first choices for Graves' disease.[^4]

- **Antithyroid medicine** (methimazole, or carbimazole in some countries) blocks hormone production. A course is typically 12 to 18 months and leads to lasting remission in some people.[^4]
- **Radioactive iodine**, taken by mouth, gradually destroys overactive thyroid tissue. It is not used in pregnancy.[^4]
- **Surgery** removes most or all of the gland.[^4]

Radioactive iodine and surgery are more definitive, but they usually leave the thyroid underactive, which means taking levothyroxine for life.[^1] Beta blockers do not lower hormone levels, but they ease the racing heart and tremor while other treatments take effect.[^1] The options change in pregnancy, so tell your doctor if you are pregnant or planning to be.[^4]

Antithyroid medicines rarely cause a dangerous drop in white blood cells or liver injury. A fever, a severe sore throat or yellowing skin while taking them needs an urgent blood test.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no proven way to prevent hyperthyroidism. If you have Graves' disease, not smoking protects your eyes, and it is wise to avoid high-iodine supplements such as kelp.[^5]

Left untreated, hyperthyroidism can cause an irregular heart rhythm ([atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation)), heart failure and thinning bones, so keep up with follow-up blood tests even when you feel better.[^1] [Thyroid 101](/articles/thyroid-101) explains the tests.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have unexplained weight loss, a persistently fast or irregular heartbeat, tremor, heat intolerance or a swelling in your neck.[^3]

A thyroid storm is a rare, sudden worsening with high fever, a very fast heartbeat, agitation or confusion, often with vomiting or diarrhea. It is life-threatening: call your local emergency number.[^4]
