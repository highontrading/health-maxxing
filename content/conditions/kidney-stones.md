## What it is

Kidney stones are hard, pebble-like pieces that form in the kidneys when minerals and salts in the urine become concentrated enough to crystallize. They can be as small as a grain of sand or, rarely, as large as a golf ball.[^1]

Most are calcium stones. Others are made of uric acid, struvite (which is linked to urinary infections) or, rarely, cystine.[^1] Stones are common: about 11% of men and 6% of women in the United States have one at least once in their lives.[^1]

## Symptoms

A stone sitting in the kidney may cause no symptoms. Pain starts when it moves into the ureter, the narrow tube that leads to the bladder:[^1][^2]

- Sharp, cramping pain in the back or side that comes in waves and may spread to the lower abdomen or groin.
- Pink, red or brown urine.
- Nausea and vomiting.
- A constant need to urinate, or pain when you do.
- Cloudy or bad-smelling urine.

## Causes and risk factors

Not drinking enough is the most common contributor. The less urine you make, the more concentrated it is.[^3] The risk is also higher with:[^1]

- A personal or family history of stones.
- A diet high in salt, sugar or animal protein.
- Obesity.
- Conditions such as [gout](/conditions/gout), an overactive parathyroid gland, inflammatory bowel disease and repeated urinary infections.
- Some medicines, including certain diuretics and calcium-based antacids.

## Diagnosis

A CT scan is the most accurate way to find a stone. Ultrasound, which avoids radiation, is an alternative.[^2][^3] Urine and blood tests check for infection, kidney function and substances that promote stones.[^1]

If you pass a stone, try to catch it so it can be analyzed: knowing the type guides prevention. People with repeated stones may be asked to collect their urine for 24 hours for the same reason.[^1][^4]

## Treatment

- **Small stones** usually pass on their own within days to a few weeks. Treatment is pain relief, often an anti-inflammatory medicine, plenty of fluids and sometimes an alpha blocker, which relaxes the ureter.[^2][^3]
- **Larger or stuck stones** may need a procedure. The options are shock wave lithotripsy, which breaks the stone from outside the body; ureteroscopy, in which a thin scope is passed up to the stone and a laser breaks it; and minimally invasive surgery through a small cut in the back for very large stones.[^1][^2]
- **A blocked kidney with infection** is an emergency that needs urgent drainage and antibiotics.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

Stones often come back, so prevention matters:[^4]

- **Drink enough to keep your urine pale.** The American Urological Association advises drinking enough to produce at least 2.5 liters of urine a day. The NHS suggests up to 3 liters (about 3 quarts) of fluid a day for people who have had a stone.[^2][^4]
- **Cut back on salt** and avoid large portions of meat.[^1][^4]
- **Keep eating calcium-rich foods.** Calcium from food binds oxalate in the gut, so cutting out dairy can backfire. For people whose calcium stones are linked to high calcium in the urine, the AUA recommends 1,000 to 1,200 mg of calcium a day from food.[^4]
- Depending on the stone type, your doctor may also suggest limiting high-oxalate foods such as spinach, rhubarb and nuts, or prescribe a preventive medicine.[^1][^4]

Our [water intake calculator](/tools/water) gives a general starting point, and [How Much Water Do You Really Need?](/articles/how-much-water-do-you-need) explains the basics. If you form stones, go by your doctor's target.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have pain in your side or back that does not go away, or blood in your urine.[^1]

**Get emergency care** if the pain is so severe that you cannot sit still, or if it comes with fever and chills, vomiting that stops you from keeping fluids down, or being unable to pass urine.[^1][^2] Fever with a kidney stone can mean an infected, blocked kidney, which needs urgent treatment.[^3]
