## What it is

The lens sits behind the colored part of your eye and focuses light onto the retina. A cataract is a cloudy area in that lens. It forms when the proteins in the lens break down and clump together, a process that starts around age 40 and usually takes years to affect sight.[^1]

Cataracts are very common. More than half of Americans aged 80 or older either have cataracts or have had surgery for them.[^1] Worldwide, cataract is a leading cause of vision impairment: an estimated 94 million people have sight loss from cataracts that have not been treated.[^2]

## Symptoms

Cataracts are painless and develop slowly. You may notice cloudy or blurry vision, faded colors, poor night vision, glare from lamps and headlights, halos around lights, double vision in one eye or a glasses prescription that keeps changing.[^1] They usually affect both eyes, though not always equally.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Most cataracts are a result of normal aging. They are more likely, or come earlier, if you have diabetes, smoke, drink heavily, have spent a lot of time in the sun, have taken steroid medicines for a long time, have had an eye injury or eye surgery, or have a family history of cataracts.[^1] Some babies are born with cataracts or develop them in childhood, which needs specialist care.[^4]

## Diagnosis

An eye care professional diagnoses cataracts with a dilated eye exam. Drops widen your pupils so the lens and the back of the eye can be examined, alongside a standard vision test.[^1]

## Treatment

Early on, stronger glasses, brighter reading lights, magnifiers and anti-glare sunglasses may be enough.[^1] No eye drop or supplement clears a cataract. Surgery is the only way to remove one.[^1]

Surgery is recommended when cataracts get in the way of everyday activities such as reading, driving or watching TV.[^1] There is no need to wait for a cataract to become severe. NICE advises that access to surgery should not be restricted on the basis of an eye-chart score alone.[^6]

The surgeon removes the cloudy lens through a tiny cut and replaces it with a clear artificial one. The operation usually takes 30 to 45 minutes under local anesthetic, and you go home the same day. If both eyes need treatment, they are usually done separately.[^5]

Results are good. About 9 in 10 people see better afterward.[^1] Serious complications are uncommon. The NHS puts the risk of permanent sight loss from the operation at around 1 in 1,000.[^5] Months or years later, the capsule that holds the new lens can turn cloudy and blur vision again. A quick laser treatment fixes this.[^1]

## Prevention and self-care

You cannot stop the lens aging, but you can slow the damage. Wear sunglasses that block ultraviolet light and a hat with a brim, do not smoke, and keep diabetes well controlled.[^1] If smoking is the habit to tackle, see [How to Quit Smoking (and Vaping)](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping).

Regular eye exams catch cataracts along with other age-related conditions such as [glaucoma](/conditions/glaucoma). The US National Eye Institute recommends a dilated eye exam at least every two years from age 60.[^1]

## When to see a doctor

Book an eye exam if your vision is becoming cloudy, glare is making night driving hard or your prescription keeps changing.[^3]

Cataracts do not cause sudden changes. Sudden loss of vision, a burst of new floaters or flashes, a curtain across your sight, or a painful red eye is an emergency. After cataract surgery, increasing pain, redness or worsening vision also needs same-day attention.[^5]
