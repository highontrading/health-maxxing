## What it is

COVID-19 is an infectious disease caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, first identified in late 2019. The global health emergency was declared over in 2023, but the virus still circulates and keeps evolving into new variants.[^1]

Most people have a mild to moderate illness and recover without special treatment. Some become seriously ill and need hospital care, particularly older adults and people with underlying conditions.[^1]

## Symptoms

Symptoms appear 2 to 14 days after exposure and range from none at all to severe.[^3] Common ones are fever or chills, cough, sore throat, a runny or stuffy nose, tiredness, muscle aches and headache. Some people have shortness of breath, nausea, vomiting or diarrhea, or a new loss of taste or smell.[^3]

These overlap heavily with colds and flu, so symptoms alone cannot tell you which you have. See [Cold, Flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid).

A minority of people develop long COVID, also called post COVID-19 condition: symptoms such as fatigue, breathlessness and trouble thinking clearly that continue or appear three months after the infection and last at least two months. The WHO estimates that it affects about 6 in every 100 people who have COVID-19.[^6]

## Causes and risk factors

The virus spreads mainly through droplets and smaller airborne particles breathed out by a person who has it, especially indoors in crowded, poorly ventilated spaces. People can pass it on before their symptoms start, or without ever having any.[^1][^2]

The risk of severe illness rises with age. It is also higher in people with conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, chronic lung disease or cancer, in people with a weakened immune system, and during pregnancy.[^1][^2]

## Diagnosis

Rapid antigen tests, which you can do at home, give a result in minutes. Laboratory tests such as PCR are more sensitive.[^4] A negative home test early in the illness does not rule COVID-19 out. If you have symptoms, test again 48 hours later.[^4]

## Treatment

Most people recover at home with rest, fluids and over-the-counter fever or pain relievers used as directed on the label.[^8]

For people at higher risk of severe illness, doctors can prescribe antiviral medicines, such as nirmatrelvir with ritonavir, or remdesivir. Treatment has to start within five to seven days of the first symptoms, so do not wait to see whether you get worse.[^5] Which medicines are available, and who qualifies, differs by country.[^8] People who are admitted to hospital may need oxygen and other treatments.[^1] Antibiotics do not work against the virus.

## Prevention and self-care

Vaccines lower the risk of severe illness, hospitalization and death.[^1] Advice on who should have further doses, and how often, varies by country and has changed over time, with priority generally given to older adults and people at higher risk. Check your national schedule; our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) explains how.

Everyday measures help too: fresh air indoors, covering coughs and sneezes, washing your hands, and staying home when you are sick.[^7]

On going back out, the US CDC advises waiting until your symptoms have been improving overall for at least 24 hours and any fever has gone without medication, then taking extra care around others for the next five days.[^7] Rules differ elsewhere, so follow local advice.

## When to see a doctor

Contact a doctor promptly if you have symptoms or a positive test and you are older, pregnant, or have a long-term condition or a weakened immune system. Antiviral treatment only works if it starts early.[^5] Also seek advice if you are getting worse, or if symptoms are still troubling you after several weeks.

Call your local emergency number for trouble breathing, persistent pain or pressure in the chest, new confusion, inability to wake or stay awake, or pale, gray or blue skin, lips or nail beds.[^3]
