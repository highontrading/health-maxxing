## What it is

Urinary incontinence is any unintentional leaking of urine. It is a symptom with several possible causes, and it comes in different types:[^1][^2]

- **Stress incontinence**: leaks when pressure on the bladder rises, as when you cough, sneeze, laugh, lift or exercise.
- **Urge incontinence**: a sudden, intense need to urinate, followed by a leak. It is often part of an overactive bladder.
- **Mixed incontinence**: both of the above.
- **Overflow incontinence**: the bladder does not empty properly, so it dribbles.
- **Functional incontinence**: a physical or mental limitation, such as arthritis or dementia, stops you from reaching the toilet in time.

Roughly a quarter to a third of adults in the United States have some incontinence.[^4] It becomes more common with age, but it is not an inevitable part of aging, and it can be treated.[^1]

## Symptoms

Leaks range from a few drops to enough to soak through clothes. Depending on the type, you may also need to urinate often, wake at night to go, feel sudden urges, or have a weak stream and a feeling that the bladder never empties.[^1][^2]

## Causes and risk factors

- **Stress incontinence** results from weakness in the pelvic floor muscles or the valve that keeps the bladder closed. Pregnancy and vaginal birth, menopause, obesity, long-term coughing and constipation all contribute. In men, the usual cause is prostate surgery.[^1][^2]
- **Urge incontinence** results from an overactive bladder muscle. Often no cause is found, but urinary infections, caffeine, alcohol and neurological conditions such as stroke, multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease can be responsible.[^1][^2]
- **Overflow incontinence** follows a blockage, such as an [enlarged prostate](/conditions/benign-prostatic-hyperplasia) or severe constipation, or nerve damage that weakens the bladder.[^1][^2]

Some medicines, including diuretics, can make leaking worse.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Expect questions about when you leak and how much, a urine test to rule out infection, and a physical examination. You may be asked to keep a bladder diary for a few days, noting drinks, toilet visits and leaks. A scan can show whether your bladder empties fully. Specialized bladder pressure tests are kept for unclear cases or before surgery.[^1][^2]

## Treatment

Non-surgical treatments come first:[^1][^2]

- **Pelvic floor muscle training.** In a Cochrane review, women with stress incontinence who did pelvic floor training were about eight times more likely to report being cured than women who had no treatment or a sham treatment (56% versus 6%).[^3] Programs usually run for at least three months.[^2] [Your Pelvic Floor: A User's Guide](/articles/pelvic-floor-health) shows how to do the exercises correctly.
- **Bladder training** for urge incontinence, which gradually lengthens the time between toilet visits.[^2]
- **Lifestyle changes**: losing excess weight, cutting down on caffeine and alcohol, and treating constipation.[^2]
- **Medicines** that calm an overactive bladder.[^1][^2]

If these are not enough, options for urge incontinence include botulinum toxin (Botox) injections into the bladder and nerve stimulation. Options for stress incontinence include vaginal devices and surgery such as a sling. Men with leakage after prostate surgery may be offered a sling or an artificial sphincter.[^1][^4]

Pads and similar products help you stay active while treatment takes effect.

## Prevention and self-care

- Do pelvic floor exercises regularly, including during pregnancy and after birth.[^2]
- Keep a healthy weight and stay active.[^1][^2]
- Avoid constipation, and limit caffeine and alcohol.[^1][^2]
- Keep drinking normally. Restricting fluids too much can make incontinence worse.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor about any leaking that bothers you or limits what you do. It is common and treatable, and there is no need to feel embarrassed.[^2] Get checked promptly if you also have pain, blood in your urine or repeated infections.

**Seek emergency care** if you suddenly lose control of your bladder or bowels together with back pain, leg weakness or numbness around the genitals or buttocks, which can mean pressure on the nerves at the base of the spine. Do the same if you cannot pass urine at all and your lower abdomen is painful and swollen.
