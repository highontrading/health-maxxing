## What it is

Depression is more than feeling sad. It is a health condition in which low mood, or a loss of interest and pleasure, lasts most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks and gets in the way of work, relationships and daily life.[^1][^2]

It is common. WHO estimates that roughly 1 in 20 adults worldwide is living with depression, and that it is about 1.5 times as common in women as in men.[^1] It is not a weakness or a character flaw, and effective treatments exist.[^1]

## Symptoms

Alongside low mood or loss of interest, symptoms include tiredness, changes in sleep and appetite, poor concentration, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, hopelessness, moving or speaking more slowly (or feeling agitated), and thoughts of death or suicide.[^2]

Depression does not look the same in everyone. Some people mainly notice irritability, anger, physical aches or a wish to withdraw from others.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

There is no single cause. Depression arises from a mix of biological, psychological and social factors.[^1] Risk is higher with a family history of depression, after major stresses such as bereavement, job loss or trauma, with long-term physical illness, and around pregnancy and childbirth.[^1][^2] Some medicines can contribute.[^2]

## Diagnosis

A clinician diagnoses depression by asking about your symptoms, how long they have lasted and how they affect you, often with a short questionnaire such as the PHQ-9.[^4] They may check for physical conditions that cause similar symptoms, and they will ask about past periods of unusually high mood or energy, because [bipolar disorder](/conditions/bipolar-disorder) is treated differently.[^2]

In the United States, the Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all adults for depression, including during and after pregnancy.[^4]

## Treatment

The right treatment depends on how severe the depression is and on what you prefer.[^3]

- **Talking therapies** such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), behavioral activation and interpersonal therapy.
- **Antidepressant medicines**, most often SSRIs. They usually take several weeks to work fully.[^2]
- **Both together**, which NICE lists as a leading option for more severe depression.[^3]

For less severe depression, NICE suggests starting with options such as guided self-help, CBT, behavioral activation or group exercise. It does not recommend antidepressants as the routine first choice unless that is your preference.[^3]

Do not stop antidepressants abruptly. Doses are reduced in stages with your prescriber to limit withdrawal symptoms.[^3] People under 25 are watched closely when starting an antidepressant, because suicidal thoughts can increase in the first weeks.[^2]

When depression has not responded, specialists can offer other medicines, esketamine, or brain stimulation treatments such as electroconvulsive therapy.[^2]

## Prevention and self-care

Self-care supports professional treatment. It does not replace it. Physical activity has the best evidence: a 2024 analysis of randomized trials found that walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all reduced depressive symptoms, although many of the trials were small and of low quality.[^6] Regular sleep, staying in touch with people and cutting back on alcohol also help.[^1]

For more, see [Depression: Signs, Causes and Treatments That Work](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment) and [exercise for mental health](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or mental health professional if low mood or loss of interest has lasted most days for two weeks or more, or is affecting your work, relationships or daily life. You do not need to wait until things are unbearable.

If you are thinking about suicide or harming yourself, or you are worried about someone else, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^5]
