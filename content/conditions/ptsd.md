## What it is

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a mental health condition that can develop after you experience or witness an event involving death, serious injury or sexual violence, or the threat of them. Being shaken after a trauma is normal. PTSD is diagnosed when symptoms last longer than a month and disrupt daily life.[^2]

Trauma is common, and PTSD is the exception, not the rule. WHO estimates that about 70% of people experience a potentially traumatic event during their lives, but only a minority, about 5.6%, go on to develop PTSD.[^1] It can happen to anyone, and it is not a sign of weakness.[^3]

## Symptoms

Symptoms fall into four groups:[^2][^3]

- **Reliving the event:** flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive memories, strong reactions to reminders
- **Avoidance:** staying away from thoughts, places, people or activities connected to the trauma
- **Negative thoughts and feelings:** guilt, shame, blame, numbness, loss of interest, feeling cut off from others
- **Feeling on edge:** being easily startled, constantly on guard or irritable, sleeping badly, struggling to concentrate

Symptoms usually begin soon after the event, but they can first appear months or years later.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Whether PTSD develops depends on the trauma and on what surrounds it. Risk is especially high after sexual violence and after exposure to war or violent conflict.[^1] It is also higher with earlier trauma, particularly in childhood, with a history of mental health conditions, and with little social support afterward. Women are more likely to develop PTSD than men.[^2]

Support from family, friends and community after the event is protective.[^2]

## Diagnosis

A mental health professional makes the diagnosis after a detailed conversation about your symptoms and how they affect you, often with the help of a structured questionnaire. To meet the criteria, an adult must have symptoms from all four groups for at least a month.[^2]

## Treatment

Trauma-focused talking therapies have the strongest evidence and are the first-line treatment.[^3][^4] They include:

- **Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy**, such as prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy
- **Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR)**

These therapies help you process the traumatic memory safely so that it loses its grip. A typical course runs for about 8 to 12 sessions.[^4]

Medicines can help, particularly certain antidepressants (an SSRI such as sertraline, or venlafaxine), when therapy is not available, is not wanted or is not enough on its own.[^3][^4]

Two things are not recommended: single-session psychological "debriefing" straight after a traumatic event, and benzodiazepines (sedatives) to prevent PTSD.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

After a trauma, leaning on people you trust, keeping to your routines and giving yourself time all support natural recovery.[^2]

If you have PTSD, regular physical activity and steady sleep habits can help alongside treatment, while alcohol and drugs tend to make symptoms worse in the long run.[^1] Self-help supports professional treatment. It does not replace it. See [exercise for mental health](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health) and [stress and your body](/articles/stress-and-your-body).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor or mental health professional if symptoms have lasted longer than a month, are severe, or are interfering with work, relationships or daily life. Treatment can help even when the trauma happened years ago.[^3]

If you are thinking about harming yourself or ending your life, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^5] If you are in danger from another person, call your local emergency number.
