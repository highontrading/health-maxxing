## What it is

Food poisoning is illness caused by eating or drinking something contaminated with bacteria, viruses or parasites, or with poisons that some bacteria produce. The WHO estimates that 600 million people, almost 1 in 10 worldwide, fall ill from contaminated food each year and 420,000 die.[^1] In the United States alone, the CDC estimates 48 million illnesses a year.[^2]

Most cases are unpleasant but short. A few are dangerous, mainly through dehydration.

## Symptoms

The usual symptoms are nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, stomach cramps and sometimes a fever.[^3] They can start anywhere from 30 minutes to a week or more after the contaminated meal, depending on the germ, so the last thing you ate is not always the culprit.[^3] Most people get better within a week without treatment.[^6]

## Causes and risk factors

Common causes include norovirus, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Clostridium perfringens, Staphylococcus aureus and certain strains of E. coli.[^2] Higher-risk foods include raw or undercooked meat, poultry, eggs and seafood, unpasteurized milk, unwashed fruit and vegetables, and cooked food left too long at room temperature.[^2][^6]

Some people are more likely to become seriously ill: adults aged 65 and older, children under 5, pregnant women and people with a weakened immune system.[^2] Listeria is a particular concern in pregnancy; [Pregnancy Basics](/articles/pregnancy-nutrition-and-exercise) lists the foods to avoid.

## Diagnosis

Doctors usually diagnose food poisoning from your symptoms and what you have eaten. Stool or blood tests are reserved for illness that is severe, bloody or long-lasting, and for suspected outbreaks.[^5]

## Treatment

The main treatment is replacing lost fluids and salts.[^5] Take small, frequent sips of water, broth or diluted juice. Oral rehydration solution is the best choice for children, older adults and anyone losing a lot of fluid, which makes it a sensible item for a [home first-aid kit](/articles/home-first-aid-kit). Eat when you feel able to.

Medicines play a smaller part:[^5]

- Over-the-counter medicines such as loperamide, used as directed on the label, can ease watery diarrhea in adults. Do not use them if you have bloody diarrhea or a fever, or give them to young children without medical advice.
- Antibiotics are needed only for certain bacterial and parasitic infections, not for most cases.
- Severe dehydration is treated in hospital with fluids given into a vein.

## Prevention and self-care

The CDC boils kitchen safety down to four steps:[^4]

1. **Clean.** Wash your hands for 20 seconds and wash utensils and surfaces often. Rinse fresh produce under running water.
2. **Separate.** Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs away from ready-to-eat food.
3. **Cook.** Use a food thermometer. Poultry and leftovers should reach 74 °C (165 °F), ground beef and pork 71 °C (160 °F), and fish and whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb 63 °C (145 °F).
4. **Chill.** Refrigerate perishable food within two hours, or one hour in hot weather, and keep the refrigerator at 4 °C (40 °F) or below.

While you are ill, wash your hands often and avoid preparing food for other people. The NHS advises staying off work or school until two days after vomiting and diarrhea have stopped.[^6]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have:[^3]

- bloody diarrhea
- diarrhea that lasts more than three days
- a fever above 38.9 °C (102 °F)
- vomiting so often that you cannot keep liquids down
- signs of dehydration: passing little urine, a very dry mouth and throat, or dizziness when you stand up

Seek advice early if you are over 65 or have a weakened immune system, or if the person who is ill is a baby or young child.[^5] If you are pregnant and develop a fever with flu-like symptoms, see a doctor straight away.[^3]

Get emergency care for blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing or muscle weakness, which can be signs of botulism, and for confusion or fainting.[^3]
