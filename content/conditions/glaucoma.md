## What it is

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases that damage the optic nerve, the cable that carries signals from the eye to the brain.[^1] The damage is usually linked to pressure inside the eye, which rises when the fluid that fills the front of the eye drains too slowly.[^2]

Vision lost to glaucoma cannot be restored, but early treatment can often stop further damage.[^1] The WHO estimates that 7.7 million people worldwide have significant sight loss from glaucoma.[^4]

There are several types:[^1][^2]

- **Open-angle glaucoma** is the most common. Fluid drains too slowly, pressure builds gradually and there is no pain.
- **Angle-closure glaucoma** happens when the iris blocks the drainage angle. If the block is sudden, it is a medical emergency.
- **Normal-tension glaucoma** damages the nerve even though pressure is in the normal range.

## Symptoms

Open-angle glaucoma has no early symptoms. Vision is lost from the edges first, so slowly that most people do not notice until the damage is advanced. About half of people with glaucoma do not know they have it.[^1]

A sudden angle-closure attack is very different: severe eye pain, a red eye, blurred vision, halos around lights, headache and nausea or vomiting. Without rapid treatment it can cause blindness.[^2]

## Causes and risk factors

Anyone can develop glaucoma, but the risk is higher if you are over 60, are of African or Hispanic heritage, or have a close relative with it.[^1] Other risk factors include high eye pressure, thin corneas, being very nearsighted or farsighted, diabetes, a past eye injury and long-term use of steroid medicines.[^2]

## Diagnosis

Glaucoma is found through a comprehensive eye exam, not a pressure check alone. The exam measures eye pressure, inspects the drainage angle, examines and images the optic nerve, tests side vision and measures the thickness of the cornea.[^2]

No single pressure reading defines glaucoma. Some people have nerve damage at normal pressure, and others have raised pressure with a healthy nerve, which is called ocular hypertension and is monitored or treated to lower the risk.[^2][^5]

## Treatment

All treatments work by lowering eye pressure to protect the optic nerve. They cannot bring back lost vision.[^1]

- **Eye drops** reduce the amount of fluid the eye makes or help it drain. They work only if used every day, long term.[^1]
- **Laser treatment** improves drainage. In the UK, NICE recommends offering a laser procedure called selective laser trabeculoplasty as the first treatment for newly diagnosed open-angle glaucoma.[^5] For angle closure, a laser makes a tiny opening in the iris.[^2]
- **Surgery** creates a new drainage channel or places a small drainage tube when drops and laser are not enough.[^2]

Glaucoma needs lifelong follow-up to check that the pressure is low enough and vision is stable.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

You cannot prevent glaucoma, but finding it early can protect your sight. The American Academy of Ophthalmology recommends a comprehensive eye exam at age 40 if you have not had one, then every few years, and every one to two years from 65.[^6] The US National Eye Institute advises a dilated eye exam every one to two years if you are at higher risk.[^1] [Health Screenings by Age](/articles/health-screenings-by-age) lists other checks worth scheduling.

If you have glaucoma, use your drops exactly as prescribed, even though you feel nothing wrong. Tell your brothers, sisters and children, because the condition runs in families and they should be examined too.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

Have regular eye exams from 40, sooner if glaucoma runs in your family. See an eye care professional if you notice any change in your side vision.[^3]

Go to an emergency department immediately for sudden severe eye pain, a red eye with blurred vision, halos around lights or eye pain with nausea. These are signs of an angle-closure attack, and treatment within hours protects your sight.[^1][^2]
