## What it is

Migraine is a neurological condition, not just a bad headache. It causes repeated attacks of moderate to severe head pain, usually throbbing and often on one side, that last 4 to 72 hours if untreated and come with nausea or with sensitivity to light and sound.[^3]

Migraine affects more than 1 in 10 people worldwide and is two to three times as common in women as in men.[^1][^2]

## Symptoms

An attack can move through up to four phases: early warning signs such as yawning, food cravings, mood changes or a stiff neck; aura; the headache itself; and a washed-out recovery phase. Not everyone gets every phase.[^2]

Some people with migraine get aura before or during the headache: zigzag lines, flashing lights or blind spots, or tingling that creeps across one side of the body. Migraine without aura is the more common type.[^5] Each aura symptom builds gradually over at least 5 minutes and usually lasts 5 to 60 minutes.[^3]

During the headache, ordinary activity such as climbing stairs makes the pain worse, which is why most people want to lie still in a dark, quiet room.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Migraine runs in families. Researchers think attacks arise from changes in brain activity that affect pain pathways around the head.[^2] A signaling molecule called CGRP is involved, which is why several newer medicines target it.[^4]

Commonly reported triggers include hormonal changes around periods, stress, too little or too much sleep, skipped meals, alcohol, bright lights and strong smells.[^2][^5] Triggers vary from person to person, so a diary tells you more than a list of banned foods.

## Diagnosis

No blood test or scan diagnoses migraine. Doctors go by your description of the attacks and a neurological examination, using criteria based on how long attacks last, what the pain is like and what comes with it.[^3] Brain imaging is reserved for headaches with warning features. A headache diary kept for a few weeks makes the pattern much easier to see.[^5]

Chronic migraine means headache on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days.[^3]

## Treatment

Treatment has two arms.[^4]

- **Acute treatment** stops an attack. For milder attacks doctors suggest anti-inflammatory painkillers or other simple pain relief. For moderate or severe attacks they use migraine-specific medicines such as triptans or the newer gepants. Anti-nausea medicine can be added. All work best when taken early.
- **Preventive treatment** is considered when attacks are frequent, around four or more headache days a month, or disabling. Options include some blood pressure medicines, anti-seizure medicines and antidepressants, medicines that block CGRP, and botulinum toxin injections for chronic migraine.

Taking acute medicines too often can itself cause more frequent headaches, known as medication-overuse headache. US guidance suggests limiting them to about two days a week on average and avoiding opioids for migraine.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

Steady habits make attacks less likely for many people: regular sleep and meal times, enough fluids, regular exercise and ways of managing stress.[^5] [Headaches and Migraine](/articles/headaches-and-migraine) covers triggers and treatment choices in more depth.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if headaches are frequent, severe or getting worse, or if over-the-counter medicine taken as directed on the label is not controlling them. You do not have to just put up with it.

Call your local emergency number for a sudden, agonizing headache unlike any you have had before, for a headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, seizures or double vision, or for weakness in an arm or one side of the face or slurred speech.[^5] These can signal a stroke, bleeding or infection rather than migraine. A headache after a head injury, or a first-ever or unusually long aura, also needs urgent assessment.
