## What it is

Psoriasis is a long-term condition in which an overactive immune system makes skin cells multiply far faster than normal. Skin cells are usually replaced every three to four weeks. In psoriasis the cycle takes about three to seven days, so cells pile up as raised, scaly patches called plaques.[^2]

It affects about 2 in 100 people in the UK and more than 8 million people in the United States.[^2][^3] It can start at any age, tends to flare and settle, and is not contagious.[^1]

Plaque psoriasis accounts for 80% to 90% of cases.[^2] Less common forms include guttate psoriasis (small, drop-shaped spots, often after a [strep throat](/conditions/strep-throat)), inverse psoriasis in skin folds, and pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis, which can be severe.[^1]

## Symptoms

Plaques most often appear on the elbows, knees, scalp and lower back, but can occur anywhere. On lighter skin they look pink or red with silvery-white scale. On brown and Black skin they tend to be purple or dark brown with gray scale.[^2] They can itch or feel sore, and nails may become pitted, thickened or discolored.[^1]

About 1 in 3 people with psoriasis also develop psoriatic arthritis, which causes joint pain, stiffness and swelling.[^3]

## Causes and risk factors

Psoriasis comes from a mix of genes and immune activity. It often runs in families, and immune cells that normally fight infection trigger inflammation in the skin instead.[^1] Flares are commonly set off by:[^1][^2][^4]

- infections, especially strep throat
- injury to the skin, such as a cut, scrape or sunburn
- stress
- smoking and heavy drinking
- some medicines, including lithium, certain antimalarials and beta blockers
- cold, dry weather

Psoriasis is also linked with higher rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity and depression, so it deserves attention as more than a skin problem.[^1]

## Diagnosis

A doctor can usually diagnose psoriasis by examining your skin, scalp and nails. Occasionally a small skin sample (biopsy) is taken to rule out look-alikes.[^1] Expect questions about your joints too: UK guidance recommends checking for psoriatic arthritis regularly, because early treatment helps prevent joint damage.[^5]

## Treatment

There is no cure, but treatment can clear plaques or shrink them substantially. Doctors usually work up through three tiers:[^2][^5]

- **Topical treatments:** steroid creams and ointments, vitamin D-based preparations such as calcipotriene (calcipotriol), and sometimes coal tar or dithranol.
- **Phototherapy:** controlled doses of ultraviolet light, given in a clinic.
- **Systemic treatments** for moderate to severe psoriasis: tablets such as methotrexate, cyclosporine and acitretin, and injected biologic medicines that block specific immune signals.

The choice depends on how much skin is affected, where, and how much it disrupts your life.[^5]

## Prevention and self-care

Psoriasis cannot be prevented, but you can reduce flares. Moisturize regularly, use treatments as prescribed, and note what sets your skin off. Protect your skin from injury and sunburn, both of which can trigger new plaques.[^2]

Not smoking, keeping alcohol low, staying active and eating well also look after your heart, which deserves extra attention when you have psoriasis.[^2] [How to Quit Smoking and Vaping](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) can help with the first.

Psoriasis can weigh on mood and confidence. Tell your doctor if it does, because treatment should address that too.[^5]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you think you have psoriasis, if treatment is not controlling it, or if it is affecting your daily life or mood. Mention any joint pain, swelling or morning stiffness promptly.

Seek emergency care if redness and shedding spread over most of your body, or pus-filled blisters appear over large areas, especially with fever, chills or feeling very unwell. Erythrodermic and generalized pustular psoriasis are rare but can be life-threatening.[^1][^3]
