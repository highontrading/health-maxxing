## What it is

Your kidneys filter waste and extra fluid from the blood, help control blood pressure and keep minerals in balance. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) means they have been damaged or working below normal for at least three months.[^2] The damage usually progresses slowly, over years.

CKD is common and mostly silent. In the United States, about 1 in 7 adults are estimated to have it, and as many as 9 in 10 of them do not know.[^4] Most people with CKD never reach kidney failure, but the condition raises the risk of heart disease and stroke, which is one reason finding it early matters.[^3]

## Symptoms

Early CKD causes no symptoms, and the kidneys can lose a lot of function before you feel anything.[^1] In later stages you may notice:[^1][^3]

- Tiredness.
- Swollen ankles, feet or hands.
- Shortness of breath.
- Nausea and loss of appetite.
- Blood in your urine, or foamy urine.
- Urinating more often, particularly at night.
- Itchy skin and muscle cramps.

## Causes and risk factors

Diabetes and high blood pressure are the two leading causes.[^1] Others include inflammation of the kidney's filters (glomerulonephritis), polycystic kidney disease (an inherited condition), long-term blockage from an enlarged prostate or [kidney stones](/conditions/kidney-stones), repeated kidney infections, and long-term regular use of certain medicines, including anti-inflammatory pain relievers.[^3]

Your risk is also higher if you have heart disease or a family history of kidney failure.[^1]

## Diagnosis

Two simple tests find CKD, and both are needed:[^1][^2]

- **A blood test** for creatinine, used to calculate your estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), a measure of how well the kidneys filter.
- **A urine test** for albumin, a protein that leaks through damaged filters. It is reported as the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR).

CKD is diagnosed when eGFR stays below 60, or uACR stays at 30 mg/g (3 mg/mmol) or higher, for more than three months.[^2] It is staged by eGFR:[^2]

| Stage | eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) |
| --- | --- |
| G1 and G2 | 60 or higher, with other signs of kidney damage |
| G3a | 45 to 59 |
| G3b | 30 to 44 |
| G4 | 15 to 29 |
| G5 | Below 15 (kidney failure) |

[Understanding Your Blood Tests](/articles/understanding-blood-tests) explains creatinine and eGFR.

## Treatment

There is no cure, so treatment aims to slow the damage and protect the heart:[^2]

- Blood pressure control, usually with an ACE inhibitor or ARB when there is protein in the urine.
- SGLT2 inhibitors, which slow kidney decline in many people with CKD, with or without diabetes.
- For people with diabetes, good glucose control and, for some, additional kidney-protective medicines.
- A statin to lower cardiovascular risk in most adults aged 50 or over.
- Treatment of complications such as anemia and bone and mineral problems.

If the kidneys fail, the options are dialysis, a kidney transplant or supportive care without dialysis.[^1][^3]

## Prevention and self-care

- Keep [blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure) and blood sugar in your target range.[^1]
- Eat less salt, stay active, keep a healthy weight and do not smoke.[^1][^3]
- Take care with over-the-counter anti-inflammatory pain relievers such as ibuprofen, which can harm the kidneys. Ask a pharmacist first.[^1][^3]
- Check before following a high-protein diet. Guidelines advise people with moderate to advanced CKD to avoid high protein intakes, so targets from general tools such as our [protein calculator](/tools/protein) may not suit you.[^2]

## When to see a doctor

Ask for kidney tests if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease or a family history of kidney failure, even if you feel well. Testing is the only way to catch CKD early.[^1] See a doctor about persistent swelling, foamy or bloody urine, or a change in how often you urinate.[^3]

**Seek emergency care** for severe shortness of breath, chest pain, passing little or no urine, confusion, or severe muscle weakness with an irregular heartbeat.
