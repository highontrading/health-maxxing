## What it is

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition. The immune system destroys the cells in the pancreas that make insulin, the hormone that lets glucose (sugar) move from your blood into your cells. With little or no insulin, glucose builds up in the blood while the body runs short of fuel.[^1]

People with type 1 diabetes need insulin every day to stay alive.[^1] It accounts for about 5% to 10% of all diabetes and can begin at any age, although it often starts in childhood or early adulthood.[^3]

## Symptoms

Symptoms usually appear over days or weeks, especially in children: intense thirst, passing a lot of urine (a child who was dry at night may start wetting the bed), weight loss without trying, tiredness and blurred vision.[^1][^2] In adults the onset can be slower, and type 1 is sometimes mistaken for type 2.[^3]

Without insulin the body burns fat for fuel and acids called ketones build up in the blood. This is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Signs include nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, fruity-smelling breath, deep or fast breathing, drowsiness and confusion. DKA is life-threatening and needs emergency treatment.[^4]

## Causes and risk factors

Type 1 diabetes is not caused by diet or lifestyle. Researchers think genes and environmental triggers, possibly viruses, set off the immune attack.[^1] Having a parent or sibling with type 1 raises your risk.[^1] People with type 1 are also more prone to other autoimmune conditions, including thyroid disease and [celiac disease](/conditions/celiac-disease).[^3]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis uses the same blood tests as other types of diabetes, for example a random glucose of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher with classic symptoms. When symptoms come on fast, glucose is a more reliable test than HbA1c.[^3] Blood tests for autoantibodies, and sometimes C-peptide (a marker of how much insulin you still make), help tell type 1 from type 2.[^3]

Antibody testing can also detect type 1 diabetes at an early stage, before glucose rises or symptoms start.[^3]

## Treatment

Insulin is the treatment. It is given as several injections a day or through a pump, with doses matched to food, activity and glucose levels.[^5] Glucose is tracked with finger-prick tests or a continuous glucose monitor, a small sensor worn on the skin. Some systems link a monitor to a pump and adjust insulin automatically.[^5]

Too much insulin for the moment can push glucose too low, below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). This causes shakiness, sweating, hunger and confusion, and is treated with 15 to 20 g of fast-acting carbohydrate, such as glucose tablets or juice, with a recheck after 15 minutes. Severe episodes need glucagon and emergency help.[^6]

For people found at an early stage, the ADA says the medicine teplizumab can be considered from age 8 to delay the onset of symptomatic type 1 diabetes.[^7]

## Prevention and self-care

There is currently no way to prevent type 1 diabetes. Day to day, the essentials are these: never stop insulin when you are ill, even if you are not eating, and check for ketones when glucose runs high.[^4] Carry fast-acting sugar, and make sure the people around you know what a low looks like.[^6]

Go to your regular checks of HbA1c, eyes, kidneys, feet and blood pressure.[^2] [Blood Sugar Basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explains the numbers.

## When to see a doctor

Thirst, frequent urination, tiredness and weight loss need an urgent medical assessment, ideally the same day, particularly in a child.[^2] Do not wait for a routine appointment.

Call your local emergency number for signs of DKA (vomiting, stomach pain, fast breathing, drowsiness) or for a low that causes a seizure or loss of consciousness.[^4][^6]
