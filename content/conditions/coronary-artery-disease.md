## What it is

Coronary artery disease (CAD), also called coronary heart disease, develops when fatty plaque builds up inside the arteries that supply your heart muscle. The process takes decades. Narrowed arteries deliver less oxygen-rich blood, and if a plaque ruptures, a clot can block the artery completely and cause a [heart attack](/conditions/heart-attack).[^1]

CAD is the most common type of heart disease.[^4] Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, and most of those deaths are from heart attacks and strokes.[^2]

## Symptoms

The classic symptom is angina: pressure, tightness or pain in the chest that comes on with exertion or stress and eases within a few minutes of rest. It can spread to the arms, neck, jaw, back or stomach.[^1][^3] Some people notice breathlessness, nausea or unusual tiredness instead of chest pain.[^1]

Many people have no symptoms at all until an artery is badly narrowed or a heart attack happens.[^1]

## Causes and risk factors

Plaque builds faster with high LDL cholesterol, [high blood pressure](/conditions/high-blood-pressure), smoking, diabetes, excess weight, physical inactivity and an unhealthy diet.[^1][^3] Risk also rises with age and with a family history of early heart disease.[^1] [Know Your Risk](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors) explains how these add up.

## Diagnosis

Your doctor will ask about symptoms and risk factors, then choose tests according to how likely CAD seems:[^1][^3]

- An electrocardiogram (ECG), which records the heart's electrical activity.
- Blood tests for cholesterol and blood sugar.
- A stress test, which monitors the heart while you exercise.
- A CT coronary angiogram or calcium scan, which images the arteries from outside.
- Coronary angiography, in which dye is injected through a thin tube (catheter) to show blockages.

## Treatment

Treatment aims to relieve symptoms and prevent heart attacks. The 2023 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guideline puts lifestyle change and medicines first for most people with stable disease.[^5]

- **Medicines** usually include a statin to lower LDL and an antiplatelet such as low-dose aspirin to prevent clots. Beta blockers, calcium channel blockers and nitrates ease angina, and blood pressure and diabetes are treated too.[^5]
- **Procedures** restore blood flow when symptoms persist or the disease is extensive: angioplasty with a stent (a mesh tube that props the artery open) or bypass surgery.[^1][^5]
- **Cardiac rehabilitation**, a supervised program of exercise and education, is recommended after a heart attack or procedure.[^5]

Aspirin is for people whose doctor has advised it. The US Preventive Services Task Force (2022) recommends against starting it to prevent a first heart attack or stroke in adults aged 60 or older, because the risk of bleeding offsets the benefit.[^6]

## Prevention and self-care

The same steps prevent CAD and slow it once you have it: do not smoke, stay physically active, eat plenty of vegetables, fruit and whole grains, keep a healthy weight, and keep blood pressure, [cholesterol](/conditions/high-cholesterol) and blood sugar in range.[^1][^2] Take prescribed medicines consistently, even when you feel well.

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment if you get chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness with exertion, even if it settles with rest.[^3]

**Call your local emergency number immediately** if chest pain or pressure lasts more than a few minutes, does not ease with rest, comes on at rest, or comes with sweating, nausea, breathlessness or lightheadedness. These are [warning signs of a heart attack](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs).[^1] Do not drive yourself to the hospital.
