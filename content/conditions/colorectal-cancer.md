## What it is

Colorectal cancer starts in the colon or the rectum, which together make up the large intestine. Most cases begin as a polyp, a small growth on the inner lining. Most polyps never become cancer, but some do, a change that usually takes 10 to 15 years.[^4]

That slow timeline is why screening works. Removing polyps prevents cancer, and finding cancer early makes it far more treatable.[^2]

It is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer death, with more than 1.9 million new cases in 2020.[^1] Most cases occur after 50, but rates in younger adults have been rising in the US and several other countries.[^4]

## Symptoms

Early colorectal cancer often causes no symptoms.[^3] When symptoms appear, they include a lasting change in bowel habit (looser stools, constipation or narrower stools), blood in or on the stool, belly pain or cramps that do not go away, a feeling that the bowel has not emptied, unexplained weight loss and tiredness.[^3] Slow bleeding from a tumor can cause iron-deficiency anemia.[^7]

These symptoms are far more often caused by something benign, such as [hemorrhoids](/conditions/hemorrhoids), but they should not be assumed to be.

## Causes and risk factors

Risk rises with age. It is also higher if a close relative has had colorectal cancer or polyps, if you have had polyps yourself, or if you have long-standing [inflammatory bowel disease](/conditions/inflammatory-bowel-disease) or an inherited condition such as Lynch syndrome.[^3]

Lifestyle factors include a diet high in processed meat and low in fruit and vegetables, alcohol, smoking, obesity and physical inactivity.[^1]

## Diagnosis

The main diagnostic test is a colonoscopy, in which a flexible camera examines the whole colon and takes tissue samples. If cancer is found, scans show whether it has spread, and the tumor is tested for genetic features that guide treatment.[^3]

Screening is for people without symptoms. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends it for adults aged 45 to 75, with an individual decision from 76 to 85. Options include:[^2]

| Test | How often |
| --- | --- |
| Stool test for hidden blood (FIT) | Every year |
| Stool DNA test with FIT | Every 1 to 3 years |
| Colonoscopy | Every 10 years |
| CT colonography | Every 5 years |
| Flexible sigmoidoscopy | Every 5 years |

An abnormal result on any test other than colonoscopy needs a follow-up colonoscopy.[^2] Programs differ by country. In England, for example, the NHS sends a home stool test every two years.[^5] People at higher risk are usually screened earlier and more often.[^4]

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the stage and location. Polyps and very early cancers can be removed during a colonoscopy. Surgery to remove the affected section of bowel is the main treatment for cancer that has not spread, often followed by chemotherapy if lymph nodes are involved. Radiation is used mainly for rectal cancer. Advanced cancers are treated with chemotherapy, targeted drugs and, for tumors with certain genetic features, immunotherapy.[^3]

Stage at diagnosis matters enormously. In US data, five-year relative survival (survival compared with similar people without the cancer) is about 90% when the cancer is still confined to the bowel and under 20% once it has spread to distant organs.[^6]

## Prevention and self-care

Taking part in screening is the most effective step. Beyond that, the modifiable risks are the ones listed above: limit processed and red meat and alcohol, do not smoke, stay active and eat plenty of fiber-rich foods.[^1][^4] [Cancer Screening, Explained](/articles/cancer-screening-explained) compares the tests.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor about any blood in your stool, and about a change in bowel habit or other symptoms above that last three weeks or more, even if a recent screening test was normal.[^5][^7]

Go to an emergency department for severe belly pain with swelling, vomiting and an inability to pass stool or gas, which suggests a blocked bowel, or for heavy rectal bleeding.[^3]
