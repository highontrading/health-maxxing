## What it is

Tension-type headache is the ordinary, everyday headache, and the most common headache disorder there is.[^1] It causes a dull, pressing ache, usually on both sides of the head, that lasts anywhere from 30 minutes to several days.[^2]

Most people get one now and then. When headaches occur on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, the pattern is called chronic tension-type headache.[^2] WHO estimates that it affects 1 to 3% of adults.[^1]

## Symptoms

The pain is mild to moderate, feels like pressure or a tight band rather than a pulse, and does not get worse with routine activity such as walking or climbing stairs.[^2] The scalp, neck and shoulder muscles may feel tender.[^4]

What is missing matters as much. There is no vomiting and usually no nausea, and you may be bothered by light or by noise, but not both.[^2] Throbbing, one-sided pain with nausea points to [migraine](/conditions/migraine) instead.

## Causes and risk factors

The exact mechanism is not fully understood. Tender muscles around the head and neck seem to play a part in occasional headaches, while changes in how the nervous system processes pain are thought to drive the chronic form.[^2]

Commonly reported triggers include stress, poor sleep, skipped meals, dehydration, caffeine, eye strain and holding one posture for a long time.[^4][^5]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on your description and a normal examination. For a typical pattern no scan or blood test is needed, and NICE advises against brain imaging done purely for reassurance.[^3]

A headache diary helps your clinician tell tension-type headache from migraine and spot overuse of painkillers. Note when headaches happen, how bad they are, possible triggers and every medicine you take for them.[^3]

## Treatment

Occasional headaches usually respond to simple painkillers such as paracetamol (acetaminophen), ibuprofen or aspirin, taken as directed on the label.[^3][^4] NICE advises against opioids for tension-type headache.[^3]

The catch is frequency. Taking simple painkillers on 15 or more days a month, or triptans, opioids or combination painkillers on 10 or more days a month, for three months or longer can cause medication-overuse headache, in which the treatment itself keeps the headaches going.[^3]

For frequent or chronic headaches, doctors therefore shift to prevention. NICE recommends considering a course of acupuncture for chronic tension-type headache,[^3] and a low dose of the antidepressant amitriptyline is sometimes prescribed to prevent attacks.[^4] Relaxation techniques, regular exercise and massage help some people.[^4]

## Prevention and self-care

Regular sleep, regular meals, plenty of fluids, physical activity and breaks from long spells at a screen all lower the odds of a headache.[^4] A heat or cold pack on a tight neck can ease one that has started.[^4]

If stress is your main trigger, see [stress and your body](/articles/stress-and-your-body). For telling headache types apart, see [Headaches and Migraine](/articles/headaches-and-migraine).

## When to see a doctor

Book an appointment if you get headaches several times a week, if they are severe, or if painkillers are not helping.[^4] Do the same if you need painkillers on more than a couple of days a week.

Call your local emergency number for a sudden, extremely painful headache, a headache after a serious blow to the head, or one that comes with fever and a stiff neck, confusion, drowsiness, a seizure, weakness, trouble speaking or loss of vision.[^4]

A persistent new headache in someone over 50, or any new headache in someone with a history of cancer or a weakened immune system, also needs prompt assessment.[^3][^5]
