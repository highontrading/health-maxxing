## What it is

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease: the immune system mistakenly attacks the lining of the joints and causes inflammation. Left untreated, that inflammation damages cartilage and bone and can deform joints. RA can also affect the lungs, heart, eyes and blood vessels.[^2]

About 18 million people worldwide were living with RA in 2019. Around 70% were women, and just over half were older than 55.[^1]

It is a different disease from [osteoarthritis](/conditions/osteoarthritis), which is driven by mechanical and structural changes in the joint. [Arthritis: Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid, Explained](/articles/arthritis-osteo-vs-rheumatoid) compares the two.

## Symptoms

RA typically causes pain, swelling and warmth in several joints. It usually starts in the small joints of the hands, wrists and feet and affects the same joints on both sides of the body.[^2] Stiffness is worst in the morning or after rest and tends to last longer than 30 minutes.[^2]

Because inflammation affects the whole body, fatigue, low-grade fever and loss of appetite are common.[^2] Symptoms often come in flares, with quieter periods in between.[^5]

## Causes and risk factors

The trigger is not known. Genes raise susceptibility, and women are affected more often than men.[^1][^2] Smoking is the best-established risk factor that you can change, and obesity also raises risk.[^2]

## Diagnosis

No single test confirms RA. Doctors combine:[^2][^5]

- the pattern of swollen, tender joints on examination
- blood tests for inflammation (ESR and CRP) and for antibodies (rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP)
- imaging such as X-rays or ultrasound

Antibody tests can be negative in people who do have RA, so a normal result does not rule it out.[^5]

Speed matters. NICE advises urgent referral to a specialist for anyone with persistent, unexplained joint swelling if the small joints of the hands or feet are involved, if more than one joint is affected, or if symptoms have already lasted three months or more.[^3]

## Treatment

The goal is remission or, failing that, low disease activity, with treatment adjusted until the target is reached.[^3][^4]

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) are the foundation, because they slow or stop joint damage and do more than ease pain. NICE recommends starting one ideally within three months of persistent symptoms beginning.[^3] Methotrexate is the usual first choice.[^4]

If that is not enough, other conventional DMARDs, biologic medicines (such as TNF inhibitors) or targeted tablets called JAK inhibitors can be added or substituted.[^3][^4] Short courses of steroids can calm a flare or bridge the gap while DMARDs take effect.[^3] Anti-inflammatory painkillers ease symptoms but do not change the course of the disease.[^5]

Because many of these medicines dampen the immune system, regular blood tests are part of care.[^5] Physiotherapy and occupational therapy help protect joints and keep you functioning.[^3]

## Prevention and self-care

There is no sure way to prevent RA, but not smoking lowers your risk.[^2] If you have RA, staying physically active is safe and helps strength, mobility and fatigue.[^5] RA also raises the risk of heart disease, so blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking deserve attention.[^5]

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor promptly if you have joint swelling, or morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, for more than a few weeks. Early treatment lowers the risk of joint damage that cannot be undone.[^5]

Seek urgent care for fever or signs of infection while on immune-suppressing treatment, for a single joint that suddenly becomes hot and very painful, or for chest pain or new breathlessness.
