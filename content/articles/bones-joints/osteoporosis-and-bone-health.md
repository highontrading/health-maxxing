Your skeleton looks permanent, but it is under constant renovation. Old bone is dissolved and new bone laid down throughout life, and for the first three decades the builders outpace the demolition crew. After that, the balance slowly tips.

Osteoporosis is what happens when it tips too far: bones become porous and fragile enough to break from a minor fall. It causes no symptoms on the way, which is why it is often discovered at the moment of a fracture.[^1] This guide covers how bone is built and lost, who should be tested, and what protects it.

## How bone is built and lost

Bone mass climbs through childhood and adolescence and reaches its lifetime peak by about age 30.[^1] The higher that peak, the more you have in reserve, which makes activity and nutrition in the teens and twenties a long-term investment.

From midlife, loss exceeds replacement. In women, falling estrogen at [menopause](/articles/perimenopause-and-menopause) speeds this up sharply, and bone is lost fastest in the first few years afterward.[^2] Men lose bone more gradually but are not exempt.

Risk rises with:[^1][^2]

- older age, being female, and early menopause (before 45)
- a parent who had a hip fracture
- low body weight, or a history of an eating disorder
- smoking and heavy drinking
- steroid tablets taken for three months or more
- conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, celiac disease and an overactive thyroid.

## Who should have a scan

Bone density is measured with a DXA scan, a low-dose X-ray of the hip and spine that takes about 10 to 20 minutes. The result is a T-score, which compares your bone with that of a healthy young adult:[^3][^4]

| T-score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| -1.0 or above | Normal |
| Between -1.0 and -2.5 | Low bone mass (osteopenia) |
| -2.5 or lower | Osteoporosis |

Who gets scanned depends on where you live. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all women aged 65 and over, and postmenopausal women under 65 who are at increased risk. It says the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against screening men.[^5] The US Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation goes further and suggests testing men from age 70.[^3] The UK has no screening program, and doctors there assess fracture risk in people with risk factors first.[^2]

A scan is only part of the picture. Doctors often combine it with FRAX, a calculator that estimates your chance of a major fracture over the next 10 years from age, sex, weight, smoking, family history and other factors.[^3]

## Calcium and vitamin D: food first

Calcium is the main mineral in bone, and vitamin D helps you absorb it. The targets differ between countries:[^2][^6][^7]

| Nutrient | US recommendation | UK recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium | 1,000 mg a day; 1,200 mg for women over 50 and men over 70 | 700 mg a day |
| Vitamin D | 600 IU (15 mcg) a day; 800 IU (20 mcg) over 70 | 10 mcg (400 IU) a day |

Food can cover the calcium. Typical amounts per serving:[^6]

| Food | Calcium |
| --- | --- |
| Plain yogurt, 225 g (8 oz) | About 400 mg |
| Canned sardines with bones, 85 g (3 oz) | About 325 mg |
| Milk or fortified soy drink, 240 mL (1 cup) | About 300 mg |
| Tofu set with calcium, half a cup | About 250 mg |
| Cooked kale, 1 cup | About 95 mg |

Supplements are for filling a gap, not for piling on. The body absorbs calcium best in amounts of 500 mg or less at a time, and supplements have been linked to a small increase in kidney stones.[^6]

Vitamin D is similar. Correcting a real shortage matters: see our page on [vitamin D deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-d-deficiency). But in a randomized trial of nearly 26,000 generally healthy midlife and older US adults, 2,000 IU of vitamin D a day for about five years did not reduce fractures compared with placebo.[^8] More is not better if you already have enough.

> [!NOTE]
> People who are being treated for osteoporosis, who are housebound, or who have low vitamin D levels are a different case. For them, adequate calcium and vitamin D are part of treatment, so follow your doctor's advice and not headlines about healthy volunteers.[^3]

## Exercise: bones respond to load

Bone is living tissue that strengthens when it is made to work. Two kinds of activity do that:[^9]

- **Weight-bearing exercise**, where you carry your own weight against gravity: brisk walking, jogging, stair climbing, dancing, tennis.
- **Resistance exercise**: lifting weights, using machines or bands, and bodyweight moves such as push-ups.

Swimming and cycling are excellent for the heart, but they are not the best way to exercise your bones.[^9]

How hard can you go? A trial called LIFTMOR enrolled 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass. Half did supervised heavy lifting and impact training for 30 minutes twice a week, and half did gentle home exercise. After eight months, spine bone density had risen by about 3% in the lifting group and fallen by about 1% in the comparison group, and the lifters were stronger and steadier.[^10]

That is one modest-sized trial with expert coaching, so treat it as proof of principle, not a license to load a barbell alone. Standard advice for people with low bone mass is still to avoid high-impact moves and exercises that bend or twist the spine forcefully unless a professional has cleared them.[^9] Our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) is a sensible on-ramp.

## Do not fall

Most hip and wrist fractures need two things: fragile bone and a fall. The WHO recommends that adults aged 65 and over do activities that challenge balance and build strength on three or more days a week to prevent falls.[^11] Check your home for loose rugs and poor lighting, have your eyesight tested, and ask for a review of any medicines that make you dizzy. See [balance and fall prevention](/articles/balance-and-fall-prevention).

## Medicines for people at high risk

Drug treatment is recommended for people who have already had a hip or spine fracture, who have a T-score of -2.5 or lower, or who have low bone mass plus a high 10-year fracture risk on FRAX.[^3]

The usual first choice is a bisphosphonate, a class of drug that slows bone breakdown. In trials these medicines cut spine fractures by around half or more and hip fractures by roughly 40%. Other options include denosumab injections and, for people at very high risk, bone-building drugs.[^3]

Two things are worth knowing. Rare side effects of long-term bisphosphonate use include damage to the jaw bone and unusual thigh fractures, which is why treatment is reviewed after three to five years. And denosumab should never be stopped or delayed without a plan, because bone loss rebounds quickly.[^3]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you break a bone after a minor fall from standing height or less, if you have lost 4 cm (1.5 inches) or more in height, or if you develop sudden back pain or a stooping posture. Ask about a bone assessment if you went through menopause before 45, have taken steroid tablets for more than three months, or have a parent who broke a hip.

## Men get it too

Osteoporosis is often framed as a women's disease, and men pay for that with late diagnosis. Estimates run from about one in five to one in four men over 50 having an osteoporosis-related fracture, and men are more likely than women to die in the year after a hip fracture.[^3] Long-term steroids, low testosterone, heavy drinking and smoking are common causes. A man who breaks a bone from a minor fall deserves the same work-up as a woman.

## The bottom line

Build bone while you are young, then defend it: eat enough calcium, keep your vitamin D adequate, do weight-bearing and resistance exercise every week, and do not smoke. Get a scan when guidelines say you are due or sooner if you have risk factors, and if you are offered treatment after a fracture, take the offer seriously. A broken hip is far harder to fix than a thin one is to protect.
