Low back pain is the single biggest cause of disability on the planet. The World Health Organization counted 619 million people living with it in 2020 and expects 843 million by 2050.[^1] If your back hurts right now, you are in very large company.

The encouraging part is how the evidence has shifted. Over the past two decades, trial after trial has pointed the same way: the things that help most are cheap and active, and several things long assumed to help, from bed rest to strong painkillers to routine scans, do not. This guide sets out what the major guidelines agree on.

## First, rule out the rare serious causes

About 90% of low back pain is what doctors call non-specific: the pain is real, but there is no fracture, infection, tumor or trapped nerve behind it.[^1] Muscles, joints, discs and the nervous system's sensitivity all play a part, and pinning it on one structure is usually not possible or useful.

A small minority of cases do have a specific cause that needs prompt treatment. Check this list before reading further.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Go to an emergency department, or call your local emergency number, if back pain comes with numbness or tingling around your genitals or buttocks, new difficulty passing urine, loss of bladder or bowel control, or weakness or numbness in both legs. These can be signs of cauda equina syndrome, in which the nerves at the base of the spine are compressed. It is a surgical emergency. Also get urgent help for back pain after a serious accident, or with chest pain.[^2] See a doctor soon if you have a fever, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, pain that is worse at night, or a leg that is getting weaker.

Pain that shoots below the knee, often with pins and needles, suggests an irritated nerve root. That is [sciatica](/conditions/sciatica), and most cases also settle with time.

## The first weeks: keep moving

Most new episodes improve substantially within a few weeks.[^2] What you do in that time matters.

**Stay active.** This is the most consistent advice in every guideline. Carry on with normal activities as far as you can, including work, and expect some discomfort. Hurt does not mean harm here. Bed rest delays recovery.[^2][^3]

**Use heat.** The American College of Physicians (ACP) recommends superficial heat, such as a heat wrap or hot water bottle, for new back pain, based on moderate-quality evidence.[^4]

**If you need a painkiller, think anti-inflammatory.** Both the ACP and NICE, which writes guidance for the NHS in England, list non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen as the first choice, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time.[^3][^4] Use them as directed on the label. They are not suitable for everyone, including people with stomach ulcers, kidney disease or heart disease, so check with a pharmacist.

**Hands-on treatment is an optional extra.** Massage or spinal manipulation can be part of the mix, but NICE recommends them only alongside exercise, not as stand-alone treatments.[^3]

Two common choices have not held up in trials:

- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen).** In a trial of more than 1,600 people with acute back pain, those taking paracetamol recovered no faster than those on placebo: a median of 17 days versus 16.[^5] NICE advises against paracetamol alone for back pain.[^3]
- **Opioids.** In a placebo-controlled trial of about 350 adults with acute back or neck pain, an opioid gave no better pain relief than placebo at six weeks.[^6] NICE says not to offer opioids for chronic low back pain.[^3]

> [!STAT] 17 vs 16 days
> Median time to recovery with paracetamol versus placebo in a randomized trial of acute low back pain. The drug made no difference.[^5]

## Why you probably do not need a scan

It feels logical to look inside. The problem is what scans find in people with no pain.

A review of 33 studies covering more than 3,000 pain-free adults found disc degeneration on imaging in 37% of 20-year-olds and 96% of 80-year-olds. Disc bulges were present in 30% of people in their twenties and 84% of those in their eighties.[^7] These changes are, for the most part, the spinal equivalent of gray hair.

So a scan often turns up a "finding" that is not the cause of the pain, which leads to worry and sometimes to procedures that do not help. NICE advises against routine imaging for low back pain, and suggests it only when the result is likely to change how you are treated.[^3] Red flags are the main exception.

## When pain lingers: what works

Pain that lasts beyond three months is called chronic. Here the evidence favors active treatment even more strongly.

**Exercise.** A Cochrane review of 249 trials concluded, with moderate certainty, that exercise therapy probably reduces chronic low back pain compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo. No single type was clearly superior.[^8] NICE suggests choosing by preference: stretching and strengthening, aerobic exercise, or mind-body approaches such as yoga and tai chi.[^3] Our [stretching and mobility](/articles/stretching-and-mobility) guide is a place to start.

**Psychological approaches.** Chronic pain is shaped by sleep, stress, mood and fear of movement as well as by tissues. The WHO's 2023 guideline on chronic low back pain includes psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy among its recommended options, alongside education, exercise programs, spinal manipulation, massage and NSAIDs.[^10] In one trial of 492 people with disabling chronic back pain, cognitive functional therapy, which combines coaching on movement with changing unhelpful beliefs about pain, produced large improvements that were still present a year later.[^11]

> [!TIP] Pick the exercise you will repeat
> Because no form of exercise clearly wins, consistency decides the result. A daily 30-minute walk that you actually do is worth more than a perfect program you abandon in week two.

## Stopping the next episode

Back pain tends to come back, so what you do after recovering matters too. In an Australian trial of 701 adults who had just got over an episode, a progressive walking program with coaching from a physiotherapist nearly doubled the time before pain returned: a median of 208 days, against 112 in the control group.[^9] The participants built up gradually toward walking most days of the week. It is hard to think of a cheaper intervention.

## What does not earn its place

Several popular treatments are advised against by the guidelines, or divide them:[^2][^3][^4][^10]

| Treatment | What guidelines say |
| --- | --- |
| Bed rest | Advised against |
| Belts, corsets and braces | Not recommended (NICE, WHO) |
| Traction, ultrasound, TENS | Not recommended (NICE, WHO) |
| Opioids for chronic pain | Not recommended (NICE, WHO) |
| Gabapentin and pregabalin | Not recommended for low back pain (NICE) |
| Spinal injections for non-specific pain | Not recommended (NICE) |
| Acupuncture | Guidelines disagree: NICE says no, the ACP and WHO include it |

Disagreement over acupuncture is a useful reminder that guideline panels read the same imperfect trials and sometimes land in different places. Where they all agree, on movement, exercise and avoiding opioids, you can be more confident.

Sitting itself is not the villain it is made out to be, but long unbroken stretches do not help. See [posture and tech neck](/articles/posture-and-tech-neck) and [sitting all day](/articles/sitting-all-day-what-to-do).

## The bottom line

Check the red flags, then keep moving. Use heat and, if it is safe for you, a short course of an anti-inflammatory. Skip the scan unless your doctor has a specific reason for it. If the pain is still there after a few weeks, put your effort into regular exercise you enjoy, and ask about physiotherapy or a psychological approach if it is wearing you down.
