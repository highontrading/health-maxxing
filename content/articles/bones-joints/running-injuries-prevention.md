Running is about the simplest exercise there is, and one of the best studied for health. It also has a reputation for breaking people. In a review of studies on distance runners, the share who picked up a leg injury ranged from about 19% to 79%, depending on how injury was defined and how long runners were followed.[^1]

Those numbers are less grim than they look. Running injuries are overwhelmingly overuse injuries, which means they build gradually, give warning, and are tied to things you control. This guide covers the common ones, what causes them, what prevents them, and the few you must not run through.

## The usual suspects

The knee is the most commonly injured site, and a handful of conditions account for most of the trouble.[^1][^2]

| Injury | Where it hurts | Typical clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Runner's knee (patellofemoral pain) | Around or behind the kneecap | Stairs, downhills, sitting with knees bent |
| Shin splints | Along the inner edge of the shin | Spread over several centimeters, eases as you warm up |
| Achilles tendinopathy | Back of the heel or lower calf | Stiff in the morning and at the start of a run |
| Plantar fasciitis | Under the heel | First steps out of bed |
| Iliotibial band syndrome | Outer side of the knee | Arrives at a predictable point in a run |
| Patellar tendinopathy | Just below the kneecap | Hills and jumping |
| Stress fracture | One spot on a bone: shin, foot or hip | Worsens as you run, lingers at rest |

Our guides to [knee pain](/articles/knee-pain-causes-and-exercises) and [plantar fasciitis](/conditions/plantar-fasciitis) go into the first-line treatment for two of these. The rest of this article is about not getting them.

## The main cause: too much, too soon

Tissue adapts to load, but on its own schedule. Heart and lungs improve within weeks, which lets you run further than your tendons and bones are ready for. That gap is where injuries live.

Two findings support this. First, beginners are the most vulnerable: a meta-analysis put the injury rate at about 17.8 per 1,000 hours of running in novices, against 7.7 in recreational runners.[^3] Second, in a study of novice runners, those who increased their weekly distance by more than 30% over two weeks had more injuries than those who increased it by less than 10%.[^4]

> [!STAT] 17.8 vs 7.7
> Injuries per 1,000 hours of running in novice runners compared with recreational runners.[^3]

That brings us to the famous 10% rule: never add more than 10% to your weekly distance. It is reasonable advice, but it is not a law of nature. When Dutch researchers tested it in a randomized trial of 532 beginners, a gradual 13-week program built on the rule produced almost exactly the same injury rate as a standard 8-week program: about 21% versus 20%.[^5]

More recent work suggests the single session may matter more than the weekly total. A study that tracked more than 5,000 runners through their GPS watches found that injury risk rose when one run was more than 10% longer than the longest run of the previous 30 days, and more than doubled when it was over twice as long.[^6] It is one observational study, so treat it as a strong hint.

Put together, the practical rules are:

- **Avoid heroic single runs.** Keep your longest run close to what you have done in the past month.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Distance, speed, hills or a new surface, not all at once.
- **Build in easy weeks.** Every third or fourth week, cut back. See [rest, recovery and overtraining](/articles/rest-recovery-and-overtraining).
- **Respect a previous injury.** Having been injured before is one of the most consistent risk factors for being injured again.[^1]

## Strength: a good bet with imperfect proof

A meta-analysis of 25 injury prevention trials across sports found that strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third and almost halved overuse injuries. Stretching, by contrast, showed no protective effect.[^7]

The honest caveat is that most of those trials involved team sports and military recruits, and direct evidence in runners is thinner. Still, stronger muscles and tendons tolerate more load, and strength work has other benefits. Two sessions a week covering calves, thighs, hips and trunk is a sound investment: calf raises, squats or step-ups, lunges, bridges and side planks. Our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) shows how to start.

## Shoes, cadence and stretching

**Shoes.** Runners are often told to buy shoes matched to their foot type. The evidence does not back that up. A Cochrane review found that prescribing shoes by foot shape probably makes little or no difference to injury rates, and that no category of shoe, whether cushioned, minimalist or motion-control, has been shown to prevent injuries. Most of the evidence was rated low or very low certainty.[^8] A group of biomechanics researchers has proposed a simpler filter: choose the shoe that feels most comfortable to you. It is a hypothesis more than a proven method, but nothing has beaten it.[^9]

**Cadence.** Taking slightly quicker, shorter steps changes the forces on your legs. In a laboratory study, raising step rate by 5% to 10% reduced loading at the knee and hip.[^10] That is a measurement of mechanics, not a trial showing fewer injuries, but it is a low-risk experiment if you have recurring knee pain.

**Stretching.** It has not been shown to prevent running injuries.[^7] Stretch if you enjoy it. Do not count on it for protection.

## Pain rules for running

Not every ache means stop. Sports physiotherapists commonly use a traffic-light approach:

- **Green.** Mild discomfort, about 3 out of 10 or less, that does not change your stride and is gone by the next morning. Carry on.
- **Amber.** Pain that builds during the run or is still there the next day. Cut the distance and intensity, and swap some runs for cycling or swimming.
- **Red.** Pain that makes you limp, sharp pain in one spot on a bone, swelling, or pain at rest or at night. Stop and get assessed.

When you return after a layoff, start with alternating walking and jogging on flat ground, every other day, and only add distance when a session is pain-free during and after.

## The ones you must not run through

Two problems deserve special attention because pushing on makes them much worse.

**Stress fractures.** These are small cracks in bone caused by repeated load. The pain comes on gradually, sits in one spot you can press on, worsens with activity and, later, persists at rest. Early X-rays can be normal.[^11] A stress fracture in the hip can become a complete break, so groin or hip pain that worsens with impact needs prompt assessment.

**Relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs).** When you consistently eat too little for the training you do, the body cuts back on other functions. The International Olympic Committee's consensus statement describes effects on bone, hormones, immunity, mood and performance, in athletes of any sex.[^12] Warning signs include missed periods, repeated stress fractures, constant fatigue, frequent illness and a loss of sex drive.

> [!WARNING]
> Periods that stop for three months or more in a woman who trains hard are not a sign of fitness. They can signal low energy availability, which weakens bone. See a doctor.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you cannot put weight on a leg, if pain is focused on one point on a bone, if pain wakes you at night, or if an injury has not improved after two to three weeks of reduced running. Get urgent help for a hot, swollen, painful calf, and call your local emergency number for chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness during a run.

## The bottom line

Increase your running gradually, avoid sudden long runs, and strengthen your legs twice a week. Buy shoes that feel comfortable and ignore the rest of the sales pitch. Run through mild aches that settle overnight, and stop for pain that is sharp, focused on a bone or still there at rest. Most running injuries are a message about load, and the fix is usually to listen early.
