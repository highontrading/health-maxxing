"Sit up straight" may be the most repeated health instruction in history. It has recently been joined by a scarier cousin: the warning that bending over your phone loads your neck with the weight of a small child and is deforming a generation of spines.

Both ideas are intuitive. Neither is well supported. Research over the past decade has undercut the notion that there is a correct posture at all, and pointed toward something more forgiving and more useful: bodies cope well with many positions, and badly with stillness.

## Where the 60-pound claim comes from

The number traces back to a 2014 paper by a spine surgeon who built a computer model of the neck. The model calculated that the force on the cervical spine rises as the head tilts forward, from about 5 kg (10 to 12 lb) when upright to about 27 kg (60 lb) at a 60-degree tilt.[^2]

The physics is not the issue. Tilting any weight forward increases the load on what holds it up. But the paper was a model. It did not examine a single person with neck pain, and it did not show that those forces injure anything. Necks are built to bend: you tilt your head just as far to read a book or chop an onion.

## What studies of real people show

When researchers look for a link between posture and pain in actual humans, it is surprisingly hard to find.

- **Phone posture.** A study of 150 adults aged 18 to 21 assessed their posture while texting and found no association between "text neck" and neck pain, or how often it occurred.[^3]
- **Posture over time.** A study that followed teenagers into adulthood found that sitting neck posture at age 17 was not a risk factor for persistent neck pain at 22.[^4]
- **Forward head posture.** A meta-analysis found that adults with neck pain tend to hold their heads slightly further forward than adults without it, but found no such link in adolescents. The studies were snapshots, so they cannot say whether posture causes pain or pain changes posture.[^5]

Reviewing this kind of evidence for the back, a group of physiotherapy researchers reached a blunt conclusion: there is no strong evidence that one optimal posture exists, or that avoiding "incorrect" postures prevents back pain. They added that comfortable postures are safe, that spines are robust, and that sitting is not dangerous in itself.[^1]

> [!NOTE]
> This does not mean posture never matters. During a flare of pain, some positions will feel better than others, and that is worth using. The point is narrower: the long-held belief that slouching damages a healthy spine has little evidence behind it.

## So why does my neck ache at my desk?

Because it is a real and common complaint. Neck pain affected an estimated 203 million people worldwide in 2020.[^6] The question is what drives it.

The most plausible answer is time. Muscles asked to hold any position for long enough will tire and ache, whether that position is a slump or a ramrod-straight sit. Hours of low-level, unbroken effort is a more likely explanation for desk-related neck and shoulder ache than the angle of your spine. General fitness, sleep and stress also influence how much a given load bothers you.

This is the logic behind a line ergonomists like to repeat: your best posture is your next posture. The goal is not to find the right position and hold it. It is to keep changing.

## Fixes with evidence behind them

**Break up stillness.** The UK Health and Safety Executive advises screen workers that short, frequent breaks beat longer, rarer ones: 5 to 10 minutes every hour is better than 20 minutes every two hours.[^7] A break means a change of activity, not scrolling your phone in the same chair. Stand up for calls, walk to refill your water, and set a timer if you forget. Our guide to [sitting all day](/articles/sitting-all-day-what-to-do) has more ideas.

**Consider a sit-stand desk, with modest expectations.** A Cochrane review found that sit-stand desks reduced sitting at work by roughly 100 minutes a day in the short term. Whether that translates into less pain or better health was unclear.[^8] Standing still all day is not an upgrade on sitting still all day. The benefit is in switching.

**Get stronger.** This is the fix with the best evidence for pain. A Cochrane review found that strengthening exercises for the neck, shoulders and shoulder blades probably reduce chronic neck pain, while stretching on its own showed no clear benefit.[^9] Two or three sessions a week of rows, band pull-aparts, shoulder shrugs and gentle resisted neck movements is a reasonable start. The [exercise library](/exercises) shows how.

**Set up your workstation sensibly.** Ergonomics will not cure pain alone, but it makes comfortable positions easier to find:[^7]

| Item | Setup |
| --- | --- |
| Screen | Top of the screen at about eye level, roughly an arm's length away |
| Chair | Lower back supported, feet flat on the floor or a footrest |
| Keyboard and mouse | Close to you, forearms roughly level, wrists straight |
| Laptop | For long sessions, raise it and add a separate keyboard and mouse |
| Phone | Lift it toward eye level, and use a headset for long calls |

> [!TIP] The 30-second reset
> Every half hour or so, stand, roll your shoulders back a few times, look at something far away and take three slow breaths. It is too short to break your concentration and long enough to change the load on your neck.

## What about posture correctors?

Braces and wearable gadgets that pull the shoulders back or buzz when you slouch are sold on the assumption that slouching is the problem. Given the evidence above, that assumption is shaky, and there is no good-quality research showing that these devices prevent or relieve pain.

A reminder to move is harmless. Relying on a strap to hold you up is not a plan, because it does nothing for the strength and endurance that make any position more comfortable. For back pain, NICE, which writes guidance for the NHS in England, advises against belts and corsets.[^10] Our guide to [low back pain](/articles/low-back-pain-what-works) covers what does help.

## When posture is not the problem

Most neck and upper-back ache is muscular and settles within days to weeks. A few patterns need a doctor.[^11]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly if neck pain comes with weakness in an arm or hand, numbness or tingling that spreads down the arm, clumsy hands, or difficulty walking or balancing. Get emergency help for neck pain after a fall or collision, or for a stiff neck with fever, severe headache and sensitivity to light. Also see a doctor if you notice a fixed, worsening curve in your upper back or a loss of height, which can indicate fractures from [osteoporosis](/conditions/osteoporosis).

## The bottom line

Stop policing your posture and start varying it. Change position often, take a short movement break at least once an hour, set up your screen so that looking at it is comfortable, and strengthen your neck, shoulders and upper back a couple of times a week. If a position feels fine, it probably is fine, as long as you do not stay in it all day.
