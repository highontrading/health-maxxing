Fiber has an image problem. It is the nutrient of bran cereal and awkward small talk, and nobody has ever built a following by posting about it. Yet when researchers pool decades of nutrition studies, few things on the plate look as consistently good for long-term health.

Most people eat far less of it than recommended. This explainer covers what fiber is, how much you need, what the evidence shows, and how to eat more without regretting it.

## What fiber actually is

Fiber is the part of plant foods that your small intestine cannot break down. Instead of being absorbed like sugar or starch, it travels on to the large intestine, where it holds water, adds bulk and feeds the bacteria that live there.[^6]

There are many kinds, and they behave differently. Three properties matter most:[^6]

| Property | What it does | Good sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soluble and gel-forming | Thickens in the gut, slows sugar absorption, lowers LDL cholesterol | Oats, barley, beans, lentils, psyllium, apples |
| Insoluble | Adds bulk and keeps things moving | Wheat bran, whole grains, nuts, vegetable skins |
| Fermentable | Feeds gut bacteria, which turn it into short-chain fatty acids | Beans, onions, garlic, oats, barley |

These categories overlap, and most plants contain a mixture. That is the practical argument for variety: oats, beans, vegetables, fruit, nuts and whole grains each bring a different blend.

## How much you need

The targets vary a little by country but point the same way:

- **World Health Organization (2023):** at least 25 g a day for adults, from fiber naturally present in food. For children, at least 15 g a day at ages 2 to 5, 21 g at ages 6 to 9 and 25 g from age 10.[^1]
- **United States:** 14 g per 1,000 calories, which works out to 25 g a day for women and 38 g for men up to age 50, then 21 g and 30 g.[^3]
- **United Kingdom:** 30 g a day for adults.[^4]

Few people get there. UK adults average about 20 g a day.[^4] In the US, more than 90% of women and 97% of men fall short of the recommended intake, according to the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans.[^5]

> [!STAT] 25 g
> The WHO's minimum daily fiber intake for adults, set in 2023. The UK target is 30 g.[^1][^4]

## What the evidence shows

The landmark analysis was commissioned by the WHO and published in The Lancet in 2019. It combined 185 prospective studies with 58 clinical trials. Compared with people who ate the least fiber, those who ate the most had a 15 to 30% lower risk of dying early from any cause or from cardiovascular disease, and of developing coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer.[^2]

The benefit was greatest at intakes of 25 to 29 g a day, and the dose-response curves suggested that more could be better still. In the clinical trials, higher fiber intakes also led to lower body weight, systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol.[^2]

How sure can we be? The long-term outcomes come from observational studies, which cannot fully separate fiber from the other habits of people who eat plenty of plants. The authors rated the certainty of the fiber findings as moderate, and the trial results on weight, blood pressure and cholesterol point in the same direction.[^2]

The mechanisms are plausible, too. Gel-forming fibers slow the absorption of sugar and carry some cholesterol out of the body. Bulky fibers hold water and speed transit. Fermentable fibers are converted by gut bacteria into short-chain fatty acids, which help fuel the cells lining the colon.[^6] For more on that last part, see our guide to the [gut microbiome](/articles/gut-microbiome-explained).

## Where to find it

Legumes, whole grains, seeds and fruit eaten with its skin do most of the heavy lifting. Approximate figures from US Department of Agriculture data:[^7]

| Food | Portion | Fiber |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lentils, cooked | 1 cup (198 g) | About 16 g |
| Chickpeas, cooked | 1 cup (164 g) | About 12.5 g |
| Chia seeds | 2 tbsp (28 g) | About 10 g |
| Raspberries | 1 cup (123 g) | About 8 g |
| Pear, with skin | 1 medium | About 5.5 g |
| Broccoli, cooked | 1 cup | About 5 g |
| Oats, dry | 40 g (half a cup) | About 4 g |
| Almonds | 28 g (1 oz) | About 3.5 g |
| Brown rice, cooked | 1 cup | About 3.5 g |
| Whole-wheat bread | 1 slice | About 2 g |
| White bread | 1 slice | Under 1 g |

A day does not need to be heroic. Oats with a cup of raspberries (about 12 g), a lentil salad with two slices of whole-wheat bread at lunch (about 12 g with half a cup of lentils) and broccoli with brown rice at dinner (about 8 g) already add up to roughly 32 g. A pear takes you past 37.

> [!TIP] The easiest upgrade
> Tip half a can of drained beans, chickpeas or lentils into whatever you are already cooking: soup, curry, pasta sauce, salad. That adds roughly 6 to 10 g of fiber for almost no effort.

## How to eat more without the bloating

The usual reason people give up on fiber is gas. Fermentation produces it, and a gut that is used to 15 g a day needs time to adjust to 30.[^6] A few habits make the transition easier:

- **Go slowly.** Add one change at a time and give yourself several days before the next. US digestive health guidance says to add fiber a little at a time so your body can get used to it.[^8]
- **Drink enough.** Fiber works by holding water, so fluid matters, especially with bran and supplements.[^8]
- **Spread it out.** Some at every meal is gentler than one enormous bean dinner.
- **Watch added fibers.** Bars, cereals and "high-fiber" snacks often rely on isolated fibers such as inulin (chicory root), which ferment quickly and cause a lot of gas in some people.[^6]
- **Swap rather than add.** Whole-grain bread, pasta and rice in place of white, whole fruit in place of juice, beans in place of some of the meat.

If you have irritable bowel syndrome, the type of fiber matters. US gastroenterology guidelines suggest soluble fiber, such as psyllium or oats, rather than insoluble bran, which can aggravate symptoms.[^11] Our guides to [bloating](/articles/bloating-causes-and-relief) and [the low-FODMAP diet](/articles/ibs-and-the-low-fodmap-diet) go deeper.

## Do supplements count?

Food comes first. The WHO target refers to fiber naturally present in foods, and most of the long-term evidence is for fiber eaten that way.[^1][^2] Supplements still have a place.

Psyllium husk, a gel-forming fiber, has the best track record. US gastroenterology societies suggest fiber supplements, psyllium in particular, as an early step for chronic constipation, while noting that the supporting trials are small.[^9] A meta-analysis of 28 randomized trials found that about 10 g of psyllium a day lowered LDL cholesterol by roughly 0.33 mmol/L (13 mg/dL), a modest but useful drop.[^10]

> [!WARNING]
> Take psyllium and other bulk-forming fibers with a full glass of water, as the label directs. With too little fluid they can swell and cause choking or a blockage. Fiber can also reduce the absorption of some medicines, so follow the label on spacing them apart.

For other approaches, see [constipation relief that works](/articles/constipation-relief-that-works).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if a change in your bowel habits lasts three weeks or more, or if you notice blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, or persistent abdominal pain or bloating. Check before raising your fiber intake if you have Crohn's disease, a history of bowel obstruction or narrowing, or have had bowel surgery.

## The bottom line

Aim for at least 25 to 30 g of fiber a day, mostly from food: legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Build up over a few weeks, drink enough, and consider psyllium if you need help with constipation or cholesterol. Few changes to your diet have evidence this consistent at a price this low.
