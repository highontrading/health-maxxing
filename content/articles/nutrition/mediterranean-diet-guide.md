Diets come and go. This one keeps being re-examined and keeps holding up. The Mediterranean diet has no brand owner, no forbidden foods and no phases, and it has something very few eating patterns can claim: large randomized trials showing fewer heart attacks and strokes, not just better blood tests.

This guide covers what the pattern includes, what the trials found (flaws included), and how to make it work in an ordinary kitchen a long way from the Mediterranean.

## What it is

The name describes how people traditionally ate in olive-growing regions such as Crete and southern Italy around the middle of the last century. It is a pattern, not a meal plan:[^8]

| How often | Foods |
| --- | --- |
| Every day | Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, olive oil as the main fat, nuts and seeds, herbs and spices, moderate yogurt or cheese |
| A few times a week | Fish and seafood, legumes, eggs, poultry |
| Rarely | Red and processed meat, sweets, sugary drinks, butter and cream |

It also covers habits that a nutrient table cannot capture: cooking at home, eating with other people and using seasonal food. That is part of why UNESCO lists it as intangible cultural heritage.[^8]

Wine with meals was part of the tradition, in low to moderate amounts.[^6] It is optional, and not a reason to start drinking.

## What the trials found

### PREDIMED: preventing a first heart attack or stroke

The key study enrolled 7,447 adults in Spain, aged 55 to 80, who had no cardiovascular disease but were at high risk of it. They were assigned to one of three groups: a Mediterranean diet with free extra-virgin olive oil (about a liter a week per household), a Mediterranean diet with free mixed nuts (30 g a day), or advice to follow a low-fat diet. Nobody was told to cut calories.[^1]

After a median of 4.8 years, the combined rate of heart attack, stroke and cardiovascular death was about 30% lower in both Mediterranean groups than in the control group.[^1]

> [!STAT] About 30%
> Fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths with a Mediterranean diet plus olive oil or nuts than with low-fat advice, among 7,447 high-risk adults.[^1]

In absolute terms that was roughly 3 fewer events for every 1,000 people each year: a real benefit, and a modest one for any single person.[^1]

The trial has a complicated history, and it is worth knowing. The original 2013 report was retracted and republished in 2018 after the investigators found that randomization had not been carried out properly for about a fifth of participants. Re-analysis without them gave almost identical results.[^1] The benefit came mostly from fewer strokes, and there was no significant difference in deaths from all causes.[^1]

Keep the population in mind, too. These were older Spaniards at high risk who already ate a fairly Mediterranean diet. Whether younger, lower-risk people elsewhere would see the same size of benefit has not been tested in a trial of this scale.

### CORDIOPREV: after heart disease

A second Spanish trial followed 1,002 people who already had coronary heart disease for seven years. Those assigned to a Mediterranean diet had roughly a quarter fewer major cardiovascular events than those assigned to a low-fat diet (87 events compared with 111).[^2]

### The cautious view

A 2019 Cochrane review of 30 trials was more guarded. Outside PREDIMED, most trials were small or short, and the reviewers rated the evidence on heart attacks and strokes as low to moderate in quality.[^3] Even so, European cardiology guidelines recommend a Mediterranean or similar diet to lower cardiovascular risk, and the American Heart Association counts it among the patterns that fit its dietary guidance.[^7][^12]

Why would it work? No single ingredient explains it. The pattern replaces saturated fat with unsaturated fat, supplies plenty of fiber, and leaves little room for processed meat, sweets and sugary drinks. Each of those changes is recommended for heart health in its own right.[^12]

## Beyond the heart

**Type 2 diabetes.** Among 3,541 PREDIMED participants who did not have diabetes at the start, those in the olive oil group were 40% less likely to develop it over about four years than the control group. The reduction in the nuts group was smaller and not statistically significant.[^4] This was a subgroup analysis, so treat it as encouraging and not as settled.

**Memory and thinking.** The picture is mixed. A PREDIMED sub-study of 447 volunteers found small improvements on cognitive tests in the Mediterranean groups.[^11] But a three-year trial of the related MIND diet in 604 older adults found no significant difference in cognition or brain scans compared with a control diet.[^5] We look at this more closely in [dementia prevention: what we know](/articles/dementia-prevention-what-we-know).

**Weight.** Despite all that oil and those nuts, PREDIMED participants did not gain weight relative to the control group.[^10]

## The trial's checklist

PREDIMED dietitians scored participants with a 14-point checklist.[^9] Its main targets make a practical to-do list:

| Eat more | Eat less |
| --- | --- |
| Olive oil as the main fat, 4 or more tablespoons a day | Red and processed meat: less than 1 serving a day |
| Vegetables: 2 or more servings a day | Butter and cream: less than 1 serving a day |
| Fruit: 3 or more pieces a day | Sugary drinks: less than 1 a day |
| Legumes: 3 or more servings a week | Commercial pastries and sweets: fewer than 3 a week |
| Fish or seafood: 3 or more servings a week | |
| Nuts: 3 or more servings a week | |

Two further points round it out: choosing poultry over red meat, and eating dishes made with sofrito (tomato, onion and garlic simmered in olive oil) at least twice a week.[^9]

## Making it work anywhere

You do not need to cook Greek or Italian food. The trials tested a Spanish version, but the principles are what guidelines endorse, and the American Heart Association stresses that a healthy pattern can be adapted to personal and cultural preferences.[^12] Those principles are:

- plants at the center of the plate
- an unsaturated oil as your main fat (see [fats, decoded](/articles/dietary-fats-decoded))
- fish and legumes in place of most red and processed meat
- whole grains in place of refined ones
- sweets and sugary drinks as occasional extras

Dal with vegetables and whole-wheat roti fits. So do black beans, avocado and corn tortillas, or tofu, fish and greens over brown rice. The [DASH diet](/articles/dash-diet-and-salt) is a close cousin with more emphasis on salt.

A sample day: plain yogurt with fruit, oats and walnuts for breakfast; a chickpea, tomato and cucumber salad with olive oil, lemon and whole-grain bread for lunch; fruit and a handful of nuts in the afternoon; sardines or lentil stew with roasted vegetables and bulgur or brown rice for dinner.

> [!TIP] Keeping the cost down
> Canned sardines, mackerel and salmon count as fish and cost far less than fresh. Dried or canned beans and lentils are among the cheapest protein foods anywhere. Frozen vegetables and fruit are fine. Peanuts counted as nuts in the trial's checklist. And eating less meat frees up money for olive oil.

> [!DOCTOR]
> A Mediterranean diet works alongside prescribed treatment for blood pressure, cholesterol or diabetes; it does not replace it. Check with your doctor before big changes if you take warfarin or have kidney disease. Chest pain or pressure, sudden weakness on one side, or trouble speaking are emergencies: see [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs).

## The bottom line

Build your meals around vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fruit, nuts and olive oil. Eat fish a few times a week, and keep red and processed meat and sweets for occasional use. The evidence for your heart is the best any diet has, and you can follow it in almost any cuisine and on almost any budget.
