You are in the cereal aisle holding two boxes of granola. One says "high in fiber", the other "no added sugar", and both have a sunrise on the front. The useful information is on the back, in small print, and you have about 30 seconds before someone needs to get past.

That is enough time if you know where to look. This guide gives you a five-step scan that works on US Nutrition Facts panels and on the per-100 g tables used in the UK, the EU and much of the world. Then it decodes the claims and symbols on the front.

## The 30-second scan

**1. Serving size (5 seconds).** Every number on the panel refers to one serving, so start here. On US labels the serving size reflects what people typically eat, not what you should eat, and a package that looks like a single portion may hold two or three. Some packages now show two columns, one per serving and one for the whole container.[^1]

**2. Calories (3 seconds).** Multiply by the number of servings you will actually eat. Percentages on US labels assume a 2,000-calorie day, a general reference and not a personal target.[^1]

**3. The limit list (10 seconds).** Look at saturated fat, sodium and added sugars. These are the three the FDA says most people should get less of.[^1]

**4. The get-enough list (5 seconds).** Check fiber. The FDA also flags vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium as nutrients many people fall short on.[^1] Protein is listed in grams, usually without a percentage; its Daily Value is 50 g.[^2]

**5. Ingredients (7 seconds).** Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight.[^5] If sugar under any name, or refined flour, is among the first three, that is most of what you are buying.

## The 5/20 rule

On a US label, % Daily Value (%DV) shows how much of a day's worth of a nutrient one serving provides. The FDA's rule of thumb is simple: 5% DV or less is low, and 20% DV or more is high.[^1]

| Nutrient | Daily Value | Low (5% or less) | High (20% or more) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saturated fat | 20 g | 1 g | 4 g |
| Sodium | 2,300 mg | 115 mg | 460 mg |
| Added sugars | 50 g | 2.5 g | 10 g |
| Dietary fiber | 28 g | 1.4 g | 5.6 g |

The Daily Values are the FDA's.[^2] You want the first three rows low and the last one high. A cereal with 12 g of added sugar per serving is at 24% DV, which counts as high before you have added anything else to your day.

## Sugar: total or added

US labels show "Total Sugars" and, indented beneath it, "Includes X g Added Sugars". Total sugars cover everything, including the natural sugar in milk and fruit. Added sugars are those put in during processing, along with syrups, honey and concentrated fruit juices, and they are the ones to limit.[^3]

UK and EU labels list only total sugars, so the ingredient list has to do the work. Sugar goes by many names: sucrose, glucose, fructose, dextrose, maltose, syrups, honey and fruit juice concentrate are the common ones. For why the distinction matters, see [carbs, explained](/articles/carbs-explained).

## Salt and sodium

> [!STAT] Over 70%
> The share of sodium in the US diet that comes from packaged and prepared foods, not the salt shaker. The label is where most of your salt intake is decided.[^4]

The US Daily Value for sodium is less than 2,300 mg a day.[^4] The World Health Organization is stricter: less than 5 g of salt a day, which is about 2,000 mg of sodium.[^7] US labels list sodium and European ones list salt; multiply sodium by 2.5 to convert. Bread, sauces, soups, cheese and cured meats are the usual surprises. Our [DASH diet and salt](/articles/dash-diet-and-salt) guide has more.

## Comparing two products: use per 100 g

Serving sizes differ between brands, which makes side-by-side comparison unreliable. One granola calls 30 g a serving, another 55 g. Wherever a label has a per-100 g column, as UK and EU labels must, use that.[^5]

The UK's traffic-light thresholds make a handy yardstick for any per-100 g table:[^5]

| Per 100 g of food | Low (green) | High (red) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fat | 3 g or less | More than 17.5 g |
| Saturated fat | 1.5 g or less | More than 5 g |
| Sugars | 5 g or less | More than 22.5 g |
| Salt | 0.3 g or less | More than 1.5 g |

On a US label with no per-100 g column, compare %DV only when the serving sizes are similar, or do the quick division yourself.

> [!TIP] The two-product test
> When you are choosing between similar items, compare just three lines per 100 g: sugars, salt and fiber. Pick the one that wins two out of three and move on.

## What the claims on the front really mean

Front-of-pack claims are regulated, but the definitions are narrower than the wording suggests.

In the US, claims are set per serving. "Low sodium" means 140 mg or less. "Reduced sodium" means at least 25% less than the regular version, and "light in sodium" at least 50% less. "No salt added" does not mean sodium-free.[^4]

In the EU and UK, most claims are set per 100 g of food:[^6]

- **Low fat:** 3 g or less per 100 g
- **Low sugars:** 5 g or less per 100 g
- **Source of fiber:** at least 3 g per 100 g; **high fiber:** at least 6 g
- **High protein:** at least 20% of the food's calories come from protein
- **Reduced:** at least 30% less than a similar product

Two traps are worth remembering. "Reduced" compares a product with its own regular version, not with a healthy benchmark, so reduced-salt soy sauce is still very salty. And one good number says nothing about the rest: a "high protein" bar can also be high in sugar. Words such as "natural", "wholesome" and "multigrain" tell you nothing about sugar, salt or fiber. The panel does.

## Front-of-pack symbols

Many countries add a summary to the front so you do not have to turn the pack over.

- **Traffic lights (UK):** red, amber and green for fat, saturated fat, sugars and salt, using the thresholds above.[^5]
- **Nutri-Score (France and several other European countries):** a single letter from A to E that weighs sugar, salt, saturated fat and calories against fiber, protein, and fruit and vegetable content. It works best for comparing similar products.[^10]
- **Warning labels (pioneered by Chile in 2016):** black "high in" symbols for sugar, salt, saturated fat and calories.[^9]

Do they work? Modestly. A meta-analysis of 60 studies found that food labeling reduced people's calorie intake by about 7% and total fat by about 11%, and increased vegetable intake by about 14%.[^8] A comparison of schemes found that color-coded labels and warning labels both nudged shoppers toward healthier choices.[^10] In Chile, purchases of sugary drinks carrying a warning fell by about 24% after the law took effect, although the law also restricted marketing and school sales, so the label cannot take all the credit.[^9]

Symbols are a shortcut. They compare products, they do not certify a food as healthy, and they are no substitute for the ingredient list when you are trying to spot [ultra-processed foods](/articles/ultra-processed-foods).

> [!DOCTOR]
> If you have a food allergy, read the ingredient list every time, even on products you buy often, because recipes change. US law requires nine major allergens to be declared: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soy and sesame. "May contain" warnings are voluntary, so their absence is not a guarantee.[^11] Trouble breathing, swelling of the throat or faintness after eating is an emergency: see [anaphylaxis](/conditions/anaphylaxis). If you have kidney disease, heart failure, diabetes or celiac disease, ask your care team which label lines matter most for you.

## The bottom line

Serving size, then the limit list, then fiber, then the first three ingredients. Use 5% and 20% as your low and high markers on US labels, and the per-100 g column to compare products everywhere else. Ignore the sunrise on the front.
