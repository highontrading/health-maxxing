The supplement aisle makes a simple promise: whatever your diet is missing, a capsule can supply. It is an appealing idea, and for a few groups of people it is true and important.

For most healthy adults who eat a reasonably varied diet, though, the research tells a narrower story. This guide separates the supplements with good evidence from the ones without, and covers what the label leaves out: upper limits, quality and interactions with medicines.

## Do multivitamins do anything?

In 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), an independent panel that grades preventive care, reviewed the trials of vitamin and mineral supplements in healthy, non-pregnant adults. Its conclusions:[^1]

- **Multivitamins:** the evidence is insufficient to say whether they prevent cardiovascular disease or cancer.
- **Most single vitamins and minerals:** the same verdict.
- **Beta-carotene and vitamin E:** do not take them for this purpose. Vitamin E showed no benefit, and beta-carotene raised the risk of lung cancer in people who smoke or have been exposed to asbestos.

Long-term data on survival point the same way. An analysis of nearly 400,000 generally healthy US adults followed for more than 20 years found that daily multivitamin use was not associated with a lower risk of death.[^10]

None of this means a standard multivitamin is dangerous. It means that if you already eat a varied diet, it is unlikely to add much.

> [!NOTE]
> Vitamins in food arrive with fiber, protein and thousands of other plant compounds that a pill does not contain. That is one likely reason diets rich in fruit and vegetables look better in studies than supplements do.

## Who actually benefits

| Who | Supplement | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anyone who could become pregnant | Folic acid, 400 to 800 mcg a day | Lowers the risk of neural tube defects |
| Vegans, many older adults, people on metformin or long-term acid-reducing medicines | Vitamin B12 | Found naturally only in animal foods; absorption can fall with age and some medicines |
| People who get little sun, and some age groups | Vitamin D | Skin makes it from sunlight; food supplies little |
| People with a diagnosed deficiency | Iron | Treats deficiency; not a general tonic |
| Pregnant or breastfeeding women; people who avoid dairy, seafood and iodized salt | Iodine | Needed to make thyroid hormone |

### Folic acid

The USPSTF gives its highest grade to this one: everyone who is planning a pregnancy or could become pregnant should take 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid a day, starting at least a month before conception and continuing through the first two to three months.[^2] The baby's neural tube closes very early, often before a pregnancy is recognized, which is why the advice covers anyone who could conceive. Some people need a higher prescribed dose. See our [pregnancy nutrition guide](/articles/pregnancy-nutrition-and-exercise).

### Vitamin B12

B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or a supplement. The adult requirement is small, 2.4 mcg a day, but absorbing B12 from food depends on stomach acid, and that becomes less reliable with age and with long-term use of metformin or acid-reducing medicines.[^3] Tiredness, numbness or tingling, and memory problems can be signs of [vitamin B12 deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-b12-deficiency) and deserve a blood test.

### Vitamin D

Advice here depends on where you live and who you are:

- The US recommended intake is 15 mcg (600 IU) a day up to age 70 and 20 mcg (800 IU) after that.[^4]
- The NHS advises everyone in the UK to consider a 10 mcg (400 IU) supplement in autumn and winter, and all year for people who are rarely outdoors, who cover their skin, or who have dark skin.[^5]
- The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline suggests routine supplements for children and teenagers, adults over 75, pregnant women and people with high-risk prediabetes. For other healthy adults under 75 it advises against going above the recommended intake and against routine blood tests.[^6]

The caution is based on trials. In the largest, about 26,000 US adults took 50 mcg (2,000 IU) a day or a placebo for five years, and the vitamin did not reduce cancer or cardiovascular events.[^4] If you think you may be low, see [vitamin D deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-d-deficiency).

### Iron

Iron is for people who need it: those with a deficiency confirmed by a blood test, and pregnant women when their doctor or midwife recommends it. Heavy periods, pregnancy, frequent blood donation and vegetarian diets all raise the risk.[^7] In men and in women after menopause, low iron needs an explanation and not just tablets, because hidden bleeding is a common cause. More on this in [iron deficiency anemia](/conditions/iron-deficiency-anemia).

### Iodine

Adults need 150 mcg a day, rising to 220 mcg in pregnancy and 290 mcg while breastfeeding. Dairy foods, seafood, eggs and iodized salt are the main sources, so people who avoid them can run short. The American Thyroid Association recommends a daily supplement containing 150 mcg for women who are planning a pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding, and not every prenatal vitamin includes it.[^8]

## More is not better

Each of these nutrients has an upper limit, and some supplements exceed it in a single tablet.

| Nutrient | US upper limit for adults | What too much can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin A (preformed) | 3,000 mcg a day | Liver damage; birth defects if taken in pregnancy |
| Vitamin D | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) a day | High blood calcium, kidney stones |
| Vitamin B6 | 100 mg a day | Nerve damage with numbness and tingling |
| Iron | 45 mg a day | Stomach upset; overdose can be fatal |
| Iodine | 1,100 mcg a day | Thyroid problems |

The limits are those listed by the US National Institutes of Health.[^12][^4][^11][^7][^8]

> [!WARNING]
> Keep iron tablets, and any multivitamin that contains iron, out of the reach of children. Accidental iron overdose is one of the most dangerous poisonings in young children.[^7]

## Quality and interactions

In the US, supplements are regulated as foods. The Food and Drug Administration does not review them for safety or effectiveness before they go on sale, and what is in the bottle is the manufacturer's responsibility.[^9] Seals from independent testers such as USP, NSF or ConsumerLab confirm that a product contains what the label says and is free of specified contaminants. They say nothing about whether it works.[^9]

Supplements can also interfere with medicines:[^9]

- **St John's wort** speeds up the breakdown of many drugs and can make antidepressants, birth control pills, heart medicines and transplant drugs less effective.
- **Vitamin K** can weaken the blood thinner warfarin.
- **Antioxidants** such as vitamins C and E may reduce the effect of some chemotherapy.

Iron tablets can also block the absorption of thyroid hormone tablets if the two are taken together.[^7]

> [!TIP] Bring the bottles
> At your next appointment or pharmacy visit, show everything you take, herbal products included. Tell your surgical team before any operation. For other products, see our [longevity supplements evidence check](/articles/longevity-supplements-evidence-check).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you have lasting tiredness, breathlessness, pins and needles or numbness: these call for a blood test, not guesswork with pills. If a child may have swallowed iron tablets, or anyone has taken a large overdose, contact your local emergency number or poison center straight away. Stop a new supplement and seek advice if you develop yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or a rash.

## The bottom line

Spend your money on food first. Take folic acid if you could become pregnant, B12 if you are vegan or your doctor finds you are low, and vitamin D if you get little sun or are in a group advised to take it. Use iron and iodine when there is a clear need. Stay under the upper limits, choose independently tested products, and tell your doctor and pharmacist what you take.
