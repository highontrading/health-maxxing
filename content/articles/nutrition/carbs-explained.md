Few nutrients have been blamed for as much as carbohydrates, or defended as fiercely. Part of the trouble is that the word covers too much ground. A bowl of lentils, a croissant and a can of cola are all "carbs", and lumping them together explains very little.

This explainer sorts them out: the three types, why the source matters, how much sugar is too much, what the glycemic index can and cannot tell you, and what the evidence says about going low-carb.

## Three kinds of carbohydrate

- **Sugars** are single or double units such as glucose, fructose, sucrose (table sugar) and lactose (milk sugar). They occur naturally in fruit and milk and are added to drinks, sweets, sauces and cereals.
- **Starches** are long chains of glucose, found in grains, potatoes and legumes. Digestion breaks them back down into glucose.
- **Fiber** is plant carbohydrate that you cannot digest. It has its own story, told in our [fiber explainer](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient).

Sugars and starches supply about 4 calories per gram, and most of that ends up as glucose in your blood, the brain's usual fuel. The US reference intake of 130 g a day for adults is based on the glucose the brain typically uses, and the acceptable range is wide: 45 to 65% of calories.[^5]

Chemically, sugar is sugar. What differs is the package. The sugar in an apple arrives with fiber and water and takes a while to chew. The sugar in apple juice arrives all at once.

## Why the source matters more than the amount

In 2023 the World Health Organization recommended that carbohydrates come mainly from whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses, with at least 400 g of vegetables and fruit and 25 g of fiber a day for adults.[^3]

A whole grain keeps all three parts of the kernel: the fiber-rich bran, the germ and the starchy middle. Refining removes the bran and germ, and most of the fiber goes with them. That difference shows up in health data. In a WHO-commissioned review of 185 prospective studies, people who ate the most fiber had a 15 to 30% lower risk of early death, coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer than those who ate the least, and the findings for whole grains were similar.[^4]

A separate meta-analysis in The BMJ put a number on it: each extra 90 g of whole grains a day, roughly three servings such as two slices of whole-grain bread and a bowl of oats, was linked with a 17% lower risk of death from any cause.[^12] These are observational studies, so they show association and not proof, but the pattern is consistent.

US guidance has moved the same way. The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans call for two to four servings of whole grains a day and far fewer refined carbohydrates.[^6]

> [!TIP] Swap, do not subtract
> Trade white bread, white rice and sugary cereal for whole-grain bread, brown rice, barley, bulgur or oats, and juice for whole fruit. You keep the foods you like and change the package they come in.

## How much sugar is too much?

Guidelines target the sugar you add or that manufacturers add for you, not the sugar inside whole fruit or plain milk. The WHO calls these "free sugars" and includes honey, syrups and fruit juice.[^1]

| Who says | Limit | In practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WHO (2015) | Free sugars under 10% of calories, ideally under 5% | 50 g, ideally 25 g, on 2,000 calories |
| American Heart Association | Added sugars up to about 100 calories a day for women, 150 for men | 25 g (6 tsp) and 36 g (9 tsp) |
| NHS (UK) | Free sugars up to 5% of calories | 30 g a day for adults |
| US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030 | No amount recommended | If eaten, at most 10 g per meal |

The figures come from each body's own guidance.[^1][^2][^11][^6] The WHO's 10% limit is a strong recommendation, based on evidence linking sugar with weight gain and tooth decay. The 5% target is labeled conditional because the supporting evidence, mostly on dental health, is weaker.[^1]

> [!STAT] About 35 g
> The sugar in a 330 ml can of regular cola. That is more than a full day's allowance under the stricter 25 to 30 g limits.[^1][^11]

Sweetened drinks are the easiest place to start: in the US they are the largest single source of added sugar.[^2] After that, check cereals, flavored yogurts and sauces. Our guide to [reading a nutrition label](/articles/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label) shows where the added sugar line sits.

## The glycemic index: useful, with limits

The glycemic index (GI) ranks foods by how much they raise blood glucose compared with pure glucose, on a scale to 100. Scores of 55 or less count as low and 70 or more as high. White bread and most potatoes score high; lentils, beans and most whole fruit score low. Glycemic load adjusts for portion size, which is why watermelon, high on GI but mostly water, has little effect in a normal serving.

It sounds precise. It is not. When researchers tested the same white bread repeatedly in 63 healthy adults, the measured GI varied by about 20% within the same person and 25% between people.[^10] GI is also measured on single foods eaten alone, and real meals contain protein, fat and fiber that change the response. And a low score does not make a food healthy: ice cream has a lower GI than a baked potato.

The WHO-commissioned review reached a similar verdict. Diets with a lower glycemic index showed smaller or no reductions in disease risk, and the certainty of that evidence was low to very low, well below the evidence for fiber and whole grains.[^4] If you track your glucose, our [blood sugar basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explains what the numbers mean.

## Do low-carb diets work?

Yes, about as well as the alternatives. A 2022 Cochrane review pooled 61 randomized trials in nearly 7,000 adults with excess weight, some with type 2 diabetes. Low-carbohydrate diets and balanced-carbohydrate diets led to very similar weight loss, differing by about 1 kg (2 lb), over periods of up to two years.[^7]

For people with type 2 diabetes, the American Diabetes Association's consensus report says that reducing overall carbohydrate intake has the most evidence for improving blood glucose, and that it can be done within many different eating patterns.[^8] The same report advises against very low-carb diets during pregnancy and for people with kidney disease or a history of disordered eating.[^8]

## Carbs for training

Carbohydrate is the main fuel for hard exercise, and sports nutrition bodies scale intake to training load:[^9]

| Training load | Daily carbohydrate |
| --- | --- |
| Light or skill-based | 3 to 5 g/kg |
| Moderate, about an hour a day | 5 to 7 g/kg |
| High, 1 to 3 hours a day | 6 to 10 g/kg |
| Very high, more than 4 hours a day | 8 to 12 g/kg |

For a 70 kg (154 lb) person training an hour a day, that is 350 to 490 g. During sessions lasting more than an hour, 30 to 60 g of carbohydrate an hour helps maintain performance.[^9] Our [macro calculator](/tools/macros) can turn these ranges into a daily plan.

> [!DOCTOR]
> If you have diabetes and take insulin, a sulfonylurea or an SGLT2 inhibitor, talk to your care team before cutting carbohydrates sharply: doses may need to change, and very low-carb eating raises the risk of ketoacidosis with SGLT2 inhibitors. See a doctor promptly for unusual thirst, frequent urination, blurred vision or unexplained weight loss, which can be signs of [type 2 diabetes](/conditions/type-2-diabetes).

## The bottom line

Stop counting carbs as one thing. Eat most of yours as whole grains, legumes, vegetables and whole fruit, keep added sugar under about 25 to 50 g a day, and treat glycemic index as a rough guide. Whether you eat more or fewer carbs overall matters less than whether you can keep eating that way.
