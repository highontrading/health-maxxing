For a generation, advice about dietary fat fit on a sticker: eat less of it. Then the pendulum swung, butter made a comeback, and now social media insists that the real danger is the bottle of sunflower oil in your cupboard.

The evidence is calmer than the argument. How much fat you eat matters less than researchers once thought. Which kind you eat matters a good deal. This explainer covers the main types, what health bodies recommend, and what the research says about omega-3, seed oils and cooking.

## The four families

| Type | Main sources | Effect on LDL cholesterol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Saturated | Butter, cheese, fatty and processed meat, coconut and palm oil | Raises it |
| Monounsaturated | Olive and canola (rapeseed) oil, avocado, most nuts | Lowers it when it replaces saturated fat |
| Polyunsaturated | Sunflower, soybean and corn oil, walnuts, seeds, oily fish | Lowers it when it replaces saturated fat |
| Trans | Partially hydrogenated oils in some fried and baked foods | Raises it |

LDL cholesterol is the kind that builds up in artery walls, which is why these effects matter.[^4] Every fat supplies 9 calories per gram, more than twice as much as protein or carbohydrate, so portions still count even when the type is right. Our guide to [cholesterol](/articles/cholesterol-explained) explains the blood test.

## Saturated fat: how much, and replaced with what

The main guidelines agree on direction and differ on strictness:

- **World Health Organization (2023):** less than 10% of daily calories.[^1]
- **American Heart Association:** less than 6% of calories, about 13 g a day on a 2,000-calorie diet.[^3]
- **US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030:** less than 10% of calories, the same cap as before, alongside new encouragement to eat full-fat dairy.[^10]

Ten percent of 2,000 calories is about 22 g. A tablespoon of butter has about 7 g, so the cap is easy to reach if butter, cheese and fatty meat appear at most meals.

The crucial question is what you eat instead. An American Heart Association review of the randomized trials concluded that replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated vegetable oil cut cardiovascular disease by about 30%, while replacing it with refined carbohydrates and sugar brought no benefit.[^4] A Cochrane review of 15 trials reached a more modest figure: cutting saturated fat for at least two years reduced cardiovascular events by about 17%, with little or no effect on overall mortality.[^5]

The WHO draws the same conclusion. It advises replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated or plant-based monounsaturated fat, or with carbohydrates from whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses.[^1]

> [!NOTE]
> "Low-fat" on a package does not mean healthier. If the fat has been replaced with sugar or refined starch, the trials suggest you have gained nothing.[^4]

## Trans fat: the one to avoid

Industrially produced trans fat is made by partially hydrogenating vegetable oil to make it solid and shelf-stable. It raises LDL cholesterol, lowers protective HDL cholesterol, and has no known benefit.[^2] The WHO attributes more than 278,000 deaths a year worldwide to it and advises keeping all trans fat below 1% of calories, which is about 2.2 g a day.[^2]

This is one nutrition problem that policy is solving. In 2018 the WHO launched a plan called REPLACE to remove industrial trans fat from the global food supply. Its recommended policies are a limit of 2 g of trans fat per 100 g of fat in all foods, or a ban on partially hydrogenated oils. Denmark led the way in 2003, the US, Canada and the European Union have since acted, and by 2024 best-practice policies covered 53 countries and nearly half the world's population.[^2]

> [!WARNING]
> Where trans fat is still allowed, labels can hide it. In the US, a food with less than 0.5 g per serving may list 0 g. Check the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated" oil.[^3]

## Omega-3: the fish fat

Omega-3 fats come in three main forms. ALA is found in flaxseed, chia seeds, walnuts and canola (rapeseed) oil. EPA and DHA come from fish, seafood and algae. ALA is essential, meaning your body cannot make it, and only a small share of it (less than 15%) is converted into EPA and DHA.[^7] The US adequate intake for ALA is 1.6 g a day for men and 1.1 g for women.[^7]

For EPA and DHA, the practical advice is to eat fish. The American Heart Association recommends two servings a week, particularly oily fish such as salmon, sardines, mackerel, herring and trout. A serving is about 85 g (3 oz) cooked.[^6] If you do not eat fish, algae-based supplements supply DHA and EPA directly.[^7]

Supplements are a separate question, and trials have given mixed results. We cover them in [omega-3, fish oil and your heart](/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-and-your-heart).

## The seed-oil question

The claim goes like this: sunflower, soybean, corn and canola oils are rich in an omega-6 fat called linoleic acid, linoleic acid drives inflammation, and inflammation drives chronic disease.

Each step has been tested, and the chain does not hold:

- **Inflammation.** A systematic review of 15 randomized trials found virtually no evidence that adding linoleic acid to the diet raised markers of inflammation in healthy people.[^9]
- **Heart disease.** A meta-analysis of 13 cohort studies with more than 300,000 participants found that people with the highest linoleic acid intake had a 15% lower risk of coronary heart disease events and a 21% lower risk of dying from it than those with the lowest.[^8]
- **Trials.** The randomized trials behind the saturated fat advice mostly used polyunsaturated vegetable oils as the replacement, and they are where the roughly 30% reduction comes from.[^4]

The evidence is not flawless. The cohort studies are observational, most of the trials are decades old, and a few of them showed no benefit, although the heart association's reviewers judged those too short or flawed in design.[^4] Still, nothing in the human data supports the idea that these oils are harmful.

There is a reasonable point buried in the panic. A large share of seed oil is eaten in deep-fried and [ultra-processed foods](/articles/ultra-processed-foods). People who cut out "seed oils" usually cut out those foods too, and may well feel better. That is a point about the food, not the oil.

## Cooking oils in practice

For everyday cooking, the American Heart Association suggests oils that are low in saturated fat: olive, canola, sunflower, soybean, corn, peanut and safflower.[^11] Extra-virgin olive oil has the strongest trial evidence, as the oil used in the best-known [Mediterranean diet](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide) study.

Tropical oils are a different matter. In a meta-analysis of 16 clinical trials, coconut oil raised LDL cholesterol by about 10 mg/dL (0.27 mmol/L) compared with other vegetable oils.[^12]

Two kitchen rules cover the rest. If oil starts to smoke, it is breaking down, so discard it and start again. And do not keep reusing the same frying oil.[^11]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Changing your fats lowers LDL cholesterol modestly. It does not replace medication you have been prescribed. If you have never had your cholesterol checked, or if close relatives had heart disease before about 60, ask for a blood test; see [high cholesterol](/conditions/high-cholesterol). Chest pain or pressure, or sudden breathlessness, needs your local emergency number, not a diet change.

## The bottom line

Do not fear fat; choose it. Make olive, canola and other unsaturated oils, nuts, seeds and fish your defaults, keep butter, fatty meat and coconut oil for occasional use, and avoid anything made with partially hydrogenated oil. The seed oil in your cupboard is fine. The deep fryer it sometimes ends up in is the part worth limiting.
