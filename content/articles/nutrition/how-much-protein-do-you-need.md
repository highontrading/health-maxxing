Protein is having a moment. It is printed on cereal boxes, stirred into coffee and argued about online. Meanwhile the official recommendation, 0.8 g per kilogram of body weight a day, sits well below almost every number you hear in a gym.

So who is right, the textbook or the tub of whey? Both, because they answer different questions. This guide covers what the minimum means, when more helps, how to spread it across your day, and who should be careful.

## The short answer

| Who | Daily protein | At 70 kg (154 lb) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Healthy adults, minimum (RDA) | 0.8 g/kg | 56 g |
| Adults over 65 | At least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg | 70 to 84 g |
| Regular strength training | About 1.6 g/kg (range 1.4 to 2.0) | About 112 g |
| US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030 goal | 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg | 84 to 112 g |

To find your own number, multiply your weight in kilograms (pounds divided by 2.2) by the figure for your group, or use our [protein calculator](/tools/protein).[^1][^2][^3][^4]

Needs also rise during pregnancy and breastfeeding, which our [pregnancy nutrition guide](/articles/pregnancy-nutrition-and-exercise) covers.[^1]

## What 0.8 g/kg really means

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is defined as the intake that meets the needs of nearly all healthy adults, about 97 to 98% of them. It sits a safety margin above the average requirement of 0.66 g/kg.[^1]

Notice what the definition leaves out. The RDA is the amount that keeps you out of deficiency, not the best intake for building muscle, aging well or losing weight. The same report sets a much wider acceptable range of 10 to 35% of calories from protein, which on a 2,000-calorie diet is 50 to 175 g.[^1]

In January 2026 the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans went further, setting a goal of 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg a day, adjusted for calorie needs, with protein foods at every meal.[^4] The RDA itself has not changed, so the US now has a floor and a goal side by side. Most of the trial evidence for the higher figure comes from people who exercise.

## If you lift: where the benefit levels off

The strongest evidence is a 2018 meta-analysis of 49 randomized trials in 1,863 people who did resistance training for at least six weeks. Adding protein produced somewhat bigger gains: about 0.3 kg (0.7 lb) more fat-free mass and 2.5 kg (5.5 lb) more on a one-repetition maximum than training alone.[^2]

The more useful finding was the ceiling. Gains rose with total protein intake up to about 1.6 g/kg a day and then flattened. The uncertainty around that point ran from roughly 1.0 to 2.2 g/kg, which is where the gym advice of "up to 2.2" comes from.[^2]

> [!STAT] 1.6 g/kg
> The daily protein intake beyond which extra protein brought no further muscle gain in a meta-analysis of 49 resistance-training trials.[^2]

Keep this in proportion: the added effect of protein was smaller in older participants and larger in experienced lifters, and the training did most of the work.[^2] The International Society of Sports Nutrition lands in the same range, recommending 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg a day for most people who exercise, and more for trained lifters who are cutting calories.[^5]

## After 65: a higher floor, with caveats

Muscle responds less readily to protein as we age, and many older adults eat less overall. For those reasons the PROT-AGE expert group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg a day for healthy people over 65, 1.2 g/kg or more for those who are active, and 1.2 to 1.5 g/kg during acute or chronic illness.[^3]

Be clear about how strong that advice is. It is a consensus statement, and the paper discloses that the group was funded by a grant from a nutrition company.[^3] When researchers tested the idea directly in 92 older men with physical limitations and low usual intakes, six months of eating 1.3 g/kg a day instead of 0.8 did not improve lean mass, strength or walking speed.[^11]

That does not make the advice wrong. It suggests protein works alongside resistance exercise, not instead of it. For the training half, see [muscle, strength and aging](/articles/muscle-strength-and-aging).

## Does timing matter?

Less than you have been told. A common rule of thumb, endorsed by the sports nutrition society, is 20 to 40 g of protein per meal (about 0.25 g/kg), repeated every three to four hours.[^5] It is a sensible way to organize a day.

But the claim that anything above 20 to 25 g in one meal is wasted has not held up. In a 2023 tracer study, 36 active adults drank either 25 g or 100 g of milk protein after a workout. The larger dose produced a bigger muscle-building response that was still under way more than 12 hours later.[^6] Get the daily total right first, then arrange it however suits your life.

> [!TIP] Pick the protein first
> Plan each meal around a protein food (eggs, yogurt, beans, fish, tofu, chicken), then add the rest. Three meals with about 30 g each plus one protein-rich snack takes a 70 kg person past 100 g.

## Plant or animal: does the source matter?

Protein quality depends on whether a food supplies all the essential amino acids in the proportions humans need, and on how well you digest it. DIAAS, the scoring method recommended by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, rates foods on exactly that.[^7] Animal proteins such as milk tend to score highest. Grains score lower, mainly because they are short of the amino acid lysine, which is why pairings such as rice and beans work so well.

In practice the gap matters less than the scores suggest, provided you eat enough in total. In a 12-week trial, 19 vegans and 19 omnivores followed the same supervised strength program while eating 1.6 g/kg a day, topped up with soy or whey protein. Both groups gained similar amounts of leg muscle and strength.[^8] The participants were untrained young men and the vegans relied partly on a soy supplement, so the result is reassuring rather than conclusive.

These figures are from the USDA food composition database. Firm tofu varies widely between products, so check the label.[^12]

A day built from this table goes further than you might expect: yogurt with almonds at breakfast (23 g), a lentil and tuna salad at lunch (35 g), chicken with rice and vegetables at dinner (31 g from the chicken) and milk with peanut butter toast as a snack (15 g). That is about 104 g before you count the bread, rice and vegetables. No shaker is required: even the sports nutrition society says whole foods can cover an active person's needs.[^5]

## Is a high-protein diet hard on your kidneys?

The worry comes from kidney disease care, where limiting protein is standard advice. In people whose kidneys are healthy, the evidence is reassuring. A meta-analysis of 28 trials with 1,358 participants compared higher-protein diets with normal or lower intakes and found no difference in how kidney filtration changed over time.[^9] None of the trials enrolled people with kidney disease, and they cannot tell us about decades of very high intake.

For chronic kidney disease the advice is different. The 2024 KDIGO guideline suggests 0.8 g/kg a day for adults with moderate to advanced disease (stages G3 to G5) and says to avoid intakes above 1.3 g/kg in people at risk of progression.[^10]

Early [chronic kidney disease](/conditions/chronic-kidney-disease) usually causes no symptoms. If you have diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of kidney disease, ask for a kidney check (a blood test and a urine test) before you move to a high-protein diet.[^10]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Get personal advice before raising your protein intake if you have kidney disease, reduced kidney function on a blood test, or any condition for which you have been told to limit protein. Losing weight or muscle without trying is a reason to see a doctor, not a reason to buy protein powder.

## The bottom line

Treat 0.8 g/kg a day as the floor. If you strength train, about 1.6 g/kg captures nearly all the measurable benefit; if you are over 65, aim for at least 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg and pair it with resistance exercise. Reach your total with ordinary food spread across your meals, do not stress about the clock, and get individual advice first if your kidneys are not healthy.
