Eight glasses a day. Two liters. A gallon, if you believe the gym. Water advice comes in confident round numbers, and almost none of them come from research.

The real answer is less tidy and more reassuring. Needs vary a lot from person to person, your body is good at telling you what it needs, and most healthy adults who drink when they are thirsty are fine. This explainer covers where the famous rule came from, what the official figures mean, when you need more, and when more becomes dangerous.

## Where "eight glasses" came from

The most likely source is a 1945 US nutrition recommendation of about 2.5 liters of water a day for adults. Its next sentence pointed out that most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods, and that part was forgotten.[^1]

In 2002 the kidney physiologist Heinz Valtin went looking for evidence behind "8 x 8", meaning eight 8-ounce glasses, or about 1.9 liters. He found none that supported it for healthy adults living in a temperate climate and doing little exercise.[^1]

## What the official numbers say

| Who says | Men | Women | What it counts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US National Academies (2005) | 3.7 L | 2.7 L | Total water: all drinks plus food |
| European Food Safety Authority (2010) | 2.5 L | 2.0 L | Total water: all drinks plus food |
| NHS (UK) | 6 to 8 cups or glasses | 6 to 8 cups or glasses | Drinks, including tea and coffee |

Three things about these figures are easy to miss.[^2][^3]

First, the US and European numbers are totals. They include the water in food, which supplies roughly a fifth of what you take in, as well as every kind of drink. Fruit, vegetables, soup, yogurt and cooked grains all contribute. Counted as drinks alone, the US figures come to about 3.0 liters (13 cups) for men and 2.2 liters (9 cups) for women.[^2]

Second, they are "adequate intakes". They describe what healthy, well-hydrated people typically consume. They are not minimums that you fall ill below.[^2]

Third, they are averages across very different people. A 2022 study in Science measured how much water actually passes through the body in more than 5,600 people from over 20 countries, aged from 8 days to 96 years. Turnover varied widely with body size, physical activity, climate, age and pregnancy.[^4] No single number fits everyone.

> [!NOTE]
> US reference intakes are higher in pregnancy (3.0 liters of total water a day) and while breastfeeding (3.8 liters).[^2]

## How to tell if you are drinking enough

For most healthy adults, two free tools work well.

**Thirst.** Your brain tracks the concentration of your blood closely and makes you thirsty well before dehydration becomes a problem. Valtin's review and the US reference report both concluded that healthy people generally meet their needs by drinking when thirsty.[^1][^2]

**Urine color.** Pale yellow means you are doing fine. Dark yellow, strong-smelling urine, along with thirst, a dry mouth, tiredness or dizziness, suggests you need more.[^5]

Thirst is less dependable at the two ends of life. Babies and young children cannot tell you, and older adults often feel less thirsty than they should, so both groups are at higher risk of dehydration and may need prompting.[^5]

> [!TIP] Make it easy, not heroic
> Keep water where you will see it, drink a glass with each meal, and have more when it is hot or you are active. If you would like a personal starting figure, try our [water intake calculator](/tools/water).

## When you need more

**Heat and exercise.** Sweat losses range from about 0.5 to 2 liters an hour depending on the person, the intensity and the weather. The American College of Sports Medicine advises drinking enough during exercise to avoid losing more than about 2% of your body weight. To learn your own rate, weigh yourself before and after a typical session: each kilogram lost is roughly a liter of sweat (a pound is about 16 fl oz). After heavy sweating, drinking about 1.5 liters for each kilogram lost over the next few hours restores the balance.[^6] Our guide to [heat, cold and sauna](/articles/heat-cold-and-sauna) covers hot conditions in more detail.

**Illness.** Fever, vomiting and diarrhea all raise fluid losses. Oral rehydration solution from a pharmacy replaces water and salts together.[^5]

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Both raise requirements, as the note above shows.[^2]

## Does extra water do anything?

If you already drink to thirst, will more help? Less is known than the marketing suggests. A 2024 systematic review found only 18 randomized trials that had tested changing people's daily water intake.[^9]

The clearest benefits were fewer repeat [kidney stones](/conditions/kidney-stones) and somewhat greater weight loss in a few trials. Single studies suggested fewer migraines, and fewer urinary tract infections in women prone to them. The reviewers described the evidence as limited and called for better trials.[^9]

Claims about glowing skin or "flushing out" waste were not what those trials tested. Healthy kidneys remove waste across a very wide range of fluid intakes, concentrating or diluting urine as needed.[^1]

## Do coffee and tea count?

Yes. In one trial, 50 men who regularly drank coffee spent three days drinking four mugs of it a day and three days drinking the same amount of water. Measures of hydration did not differ.[^7] Another study compared 13 common drinks and found that tea, coffee and cola led to about the same urine output as still water, while milk and oral rehydration solution were retained for longer.[^8]

The NHS counts tea, coffee, lower-fat milk and sugar-free drinks toward its 6 to 8 cups or glasses a day.[^3] For how much caffeine is sensible, see [caffeine: benefits and limits](/articles/caffeine-benefits-and-limits).

## Can you drink too much?

Yes. Your kidneys can only clear water so fast, and drinking well beyond your losses dilutes the sodium in your blood. This is hyponatremia, and it is the main fluid-related danger in endurance sport.[^10]

When researchers tested 488 runners at the finish of the Boston Marathon, 13% had low blood sodium and 0.6% had critically low levels. The runners at greatest risk were those who gained weight during the race, which means they drank more than they sweated, and those who took longer than four hours.[^11]

International experts now recommend drinking according to thirst during exercise, not forcing fluids on a schedule.[^10]

> [!WARNING]
> Nausea, vomiting, a worsening headache, puffy hands or confusion during or after a long event, especially if you have been drinking a lot, can signal low blood sodium. Stop drinking plain water and get medical help.[^10]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Get urgent help for signs of severe dehydration: confusion or unusual drowsiness, dizziness that does not pass, a rapid heartbeat, not urinating all day, or a seizure. Babies, young children and older adults who cannot keep fluids down need prompt care. If you have heart failure or kidney disease, or take diuretics, follow your own doctor's advice on fluids.[^5]

## The bottom line

Forget the magic number. Drink when you are thirsty, aim for pale yellow urine, and take in more when it is hot, when you exercise, or when you are ill. Tea and coffee count. And during long endurance events, drink to thirst, because too much water is a real danger.
