A decade ago almost nobody outside nutrition research used the phrase "ultra-processed food". Now it is on front pages, in government reports and in arguments over dinner. The term has spread faster than agreement about what it means, or how worried you should be.

This explainer covers what counts as ultra-processed, what the best trials and long-term studies have found, where the science is still thin, and which changes are worth making.

## What "ultra-processed" means

The term comes from NOVA, a system developed by researchers in Brazil that sorts foods by how much and why they have been processed, not by their nutrients.[^1]

| NOVA group | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Unprocessed or minimally processed | Whole foods, changed only by steps such as drying, freezing or pasteurizing | Fruit, vegetables, grains, beans, eggs, milk, plain yogurt, fresh or frozen meat and fish |
| 2. Culinary ingredients | Substances extracted from foods and used in cooking | Oils, butter, sugar, salt |
| 3. Processed foods | Group 1 foods with group 2 ingredients added | Canned vegetables and fish, cheese, freshly made bread |
| 4. Ultra-processed foods | Industrial formulations of extracted or modified substances plus additives | Soft drinks, packaged snacks, sweets, mass-produced bread, many breakfast cereals, instant noodles, chicken nuggets, many ready meals |

The practical test is the ingredient list. Ultra-processed products typically contain substances you would not use at home, such as protein isolates, hydrogenated oils, high-fructose corn syrup or maltodextrin, or additives whose job is to make the product more appealing: flavorings, colors, emulsifiers, sweeteners and thickeners.[^1]

> [!STAT] 55%
> The share of calories in the US diet that came from ultra-processed foods in 2021 to 2023. Among children and teenagers it was about 62%.[^5]

## What the trials show

Only a few experiments have compared ultra-processed and unprocessed diets directly. Two stand out.

**The NIH ward study (2019).** Twenty adults lived in a research unit for four weeks. For two weeks they were offered ultra-processed meals and for two weeks unprocessed meals, in random order. The menus were matched for the calories, sugar, fat, fiber and salt on offer, and people could eat as much or as little as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 500 calories more each day and gained about 0.9 kg (2 lb). On the unprocessed diet they lost about the same amount.[^2]

**The UK home study (2025).** Fifty-five adults were given eight weeks of minimally processed meals and eight weeks of ultra-processed meals, delivered to their homes. Both menus were designed to meet UK healthy eating guidelines. People lost weight on both, but about twice as much on the minimally processed diet: roughly 2% of body weight compared with 1%.[^4]

Together these suggest that something about ultra-processed food leads people to eat more, even when the nutrients look similar on paper. Softer textures, faster eating and more calories per bite are the leading suspects, though none is proven.[^2] Both trials were small and short, so they cannot tell us about long-term disease.

## The long-term picture

For that we rely on observational research, and there is a lot of it. A 2024 umbrella review in The BMJ combined 45 pooled analyses covering nearly 10 million people. Higher intakes of ultra-processed food were associated with a higher risk of 32 health outcomes. The most convincing links were with death from cardiovascular disease (about 50% higher risk), type 2 diabetes, and anxiety and other common mental health problems (about 50% higher odds).[^3]

Now the caveats, which matter. Research of this kind shows association, not cause. People who eat the most ultra-processed food differ from those who eat the least in income, smoking, exercise and much else, and statistics can only partly adjust for that. The reviewers themselves rated most of the evidence as low or very low in certainty.[^3]

## Where the science is unsettled

**Is it the processing or the recipe?** Many ultra-processed foods are high in sugar, salt, saturated fat and calories and low in fiber. Researchers still disagree about how much of the harm is explained by that alone.

**The category is blurry.** NOVA puts cola and supermarket whole-grain bread in the same group. When nutrition specialists were asked to classify the same list of foods, they often disagreed with one another.[^9]

**Official verdicts are cautious.** The scientific committee advising on the US dietary guidelines graded the evidence linking ultra-processed diets with greater body fat and obesity as "limited".[^8] The UK's nutrition advisory committee called the associations concerning but said the evidence had too many limitations to pin the blame on processing itself.[^12] The final 2025–2030 US guidelines nonetheless advise people to limit highly processed foods.[^11]

## Not all ultra-processed foods are equal

When researchers split the category by type, a clearer picture emerges.

In a study of about 267,000 adults in seven European countries, higher ultra-processed intake was linked with a greater risk of developing a combination of cancer, heart disease and diabetes. The link was driven by animal-based products and by sugary and artificially sweetened drinks. Ultra-processed breads and cereals, and plant-based alternatives, showed no such association.[^6]

Three large US cohorts told a similar story for heart disease: sweetened drinks and processed meats were linked with higher risk, while breads, cold cereals, yogurt and dairy desserts were linked with slightly lower risk.[^7]

The American Heart Association's 2025 advisory draws the practical conclusion. Most ultra-processed foods are high in saturated fat, added sugar or salt and are worth cutting back. A minority, such as some whole-grain breads, low-sugar yogurts, tomato sauces and nut or bean spreads, can be part of a healthy diet.[^10]

## Realistic swaps

You do not need to cook everything from scratch. Start where the evidence is strongest and the swap is easiest.

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| Sugary soft drinks | Water, sparkling water, unsweetened tea or coffee |
| Flavored yogurt | Plain yogurt with fruit |
| Sugary breakfast cereal | Oats or a plain whole-grain cereal |
| Bacon, ham, sausages, deli meats | Eggs, canned fish, leftover chicken, beans |
| Chips, biscuits and snack bars | Nuts, fruit, plain popcorn |
| Instant noodles | Plain noodles with frozen vegetables and an egg |

> [!TIP] Convenient is not the same as ultra-processed
> Frozen vegetables, fruit and fish, canned beans and tomatoes, tinned fish, eggs and plain yogurt are all quick, cheap and fall into NOVA groups 1 to 3.[^1] A meal assembled from them takes ten minutes.

## The bottom line

Ultra-processed foods make it easy to eat more than you need, and diets built on them are linked with worse health. The evidence is strong enough to act on and not strong enough to panic over. Cut back on sugary drinks, processed meats and packaged snacks first, build more meals from whole and minimally processed foods, and do not worry about the occasional ready meal or slice of supermarket bread.
