Type 2 diabetes rarely arrives overnight. For years beforehand, the body's handling of sugar slips quietly out of tune: first insulin resistance, then prediabetes. Neither one hurts. Most people find out by accident, on a routine blood test.

That quiet stretch is an opportunity, because this is one of the few conditions where strong trials show that ordinary changes can stop the slide. This guide explains what is happening, who is at risk, and what to change first.

## What insulin resistance is

Insulin is the hormone that moves glucose out of your blood and into your cells. In insulin resistance, the cells of your muscles, fat and liver respond poorly to it. Your pancreas compensates by making more. As long as it keeps up, your blood sugar stays normal and nothing shows on a standard test.[^1]

Prediabetes begins when the pancreas can no longer fully keep up, and blood sugar creeps above normal without reaching the level that defines diabetes.[^1] On American Diabetes Association criteria, that means a fasting glucose of 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) or an HbA1c of 5.7% to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol).[^3] Other bodies draw the lines slightly differently, as [blood sugar basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explains.

> [!NOTE] There is no routine "insulin resistance test"
> The most accurate measure of insulin resistance is a research procedure. In everyday practice doctors do not test for it directly. They test for prediabetes and look at your risk factors.[^1]

## Who is at risk, and the signs to notice

The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases lists these risk factors:[^1]

- overweight or obesity, particularly fat carried around the waist
- age 45 or older
- a parent, brother or sister with diabetes
- physical inactivity
- high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol levels
- a history of diabetes in pregnancy (gestational diabetes)
- polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- sleep problems, especially sleep apnea
- African American, Hispanic or Latino, American Indian, Asian American or Pacific Islander heritage

Waist size is a useful clue. A waist of 102 cm (40 inches) or more in men, or 88 cm (35 inches) or more in women, is linked to insulin resistance, and people of Asian descent can be at risk even without a high body-mass index.[^1] Our [waist-to-height tool](/tools/waist-to-height) takes a minute.

There are usually no symptoms. Some people develop acanthosis nigricans, which is dark, velvety skin in the armpits or on the back and sides of the neck, or clusters of small skin tags in the same places.[^1]

## Prediabetes: common, quiet and not harmless

About 98 million US adults, more than 1 in 3, have prediabetes, and more than 8 in 10 of them do not know it.[^2]

The label is a statement about risk, not destiny. Each year, roughly 5% to 10% of people with prediabetes go on to develop diabetes, and a similar share return to normal blood sugar.[^4]

It is also not a harmless waiting room. A meta-analysis of 129 studies found that prediabetes itself was associated with a 13% higher risk of death from any cause and a 15% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, compared with normal blood sugar.[^5] Those are modest increases, drawn from observational data, but they are a reason to act early.

## The trial that showed it can be turned around

The Diabetes Prevention Program is the reason doctors can be confident here. It enrolled 3,234 US adults with prediabetes and excess weight and randomly assigned them to one of three groups: an intensive lifestyle program, the medicine metformin, or a placebo.[^6]

The lifestyle group had two goals: lose at least 7% of body weight, and do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, each week. After an average of 2.8 years, they were 58% less likely to have developed type 2 diabetes than the placebo group. Metformin lowered the risk by 31%.[^6]

> [!STAT] 58%
> The reduction in new cases of type 2 diabetes with modest weight loss and 150 minutes of activity a week in the Diabetes Prevention Program.[^6]

The benefit lasted. When researchers followed participants for about 15 years, the lifestyle group still had a 27% lower rate of diabetes and the metformin group 18% lower.[^7]

## What to change first

The trial's recipe is still the standard advice.[^8]

1. **If you carry extra weight, aim to lose about 7% of it.** For someone who weighs 90 kg (198 lb), that is roughly 6 kg (14 lb). See [weight loss that lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts).
2. **Build up to 150 minutes of brisk walking a week,** or the equivalent. Thirty minutes on five days does it.
3. **Change the carbohydrates before you count them.** Swap sugary drinks for water, and refined grains for vegetables, beans, whole grains and whole fruit. No single diet has proved best, so pick a pattern you can keep.
4. **Join a structured program if you can.** Group programs modeled on the trial exist in many countries, and the American Diabetes Association recommends referring people with prediabetes to them.
5. **Get retested every year** so you know which way you are heading.[^3]

## Where medication fits

Lifestyle change comes first, and it outperformed medication in the trial. The American Diabetes Association says metformin can be considered for adults at high risk, particularly those aged 25 to 59 with a body-mass index of 35 or more, those with higher glucose or HbA1c levels, and women who have had gestational diabetes.[^8]

Newer weight-loss medicines may matter here too. In a three-year trial of people with obesity and prediabetes, tirzepatide cut progression to type 2 diabetes by more than 90% compared with placebo.[^9] These medicines are prescribed for obesity or diabetes, not for prediabetes alone, and whether to use any medicine is a decision for you and your doctor.

## If it has already become type 2 diabetes

Even after diagnosis, the direction can sometimes be reversed. Remission is defined as an HbA1c below 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) for at least three months without glucose-lowering medication.[^12]

The DiRECT trial tested this in UK general practice. About 300 adults diagnosed with type 2 diabetes within the previous six years were assigned to usual care or to a supervised program: a low-calorie liquid diet for three to five months, a gradual return to food and long-term support. After a year, 46% of the program group were in remission, compared with 4% of the usual-care group. Among those who lost 15 kg or more, 86% were in remission.[^10]

Weight was the hinge. At two years, 36% of the program group were still in remission, and staying in remission was closely tied to keeping the weight off.[^11]

> [!WARNING]
> Do not attempt a very-low-calorie diet on your own if you take medicines for diabetes or blood pressure. In DiRECT those medicines were stopped or adjusted under medical supervision, because blood sugar and blood pressure can fall quickly.[^10]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ask for a blood sugar test if you are 35 or older, or younger with excess weight and another risk factor.[^3] See a doctor soon if you are very thirsty, urinating far more than usual, losing weight without trying or noticing blurred vision. Seek emergency care if those symptoms come with vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid breathing or confusion.

## The bottom line

Insulin resistance and prediabetes are warnings, and unusually actionable ones. Get tested if you have risk factors. If your numbers are raised, aim to lose about 7% of your weight, walk briskly for 150 minutes a week, and swap refined carbohydrates for whole foods. That combination cut diabetes risk by more than half in a rigorous trial. Recheck every year, and ask about medication if you are at high risk.
