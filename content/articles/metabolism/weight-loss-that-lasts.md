If you have lost weight and watched it return, you are in the majority, and the diet industry has probably implied that the fault was yours. The research tells a different story. Losing weight is achievable by many routes. Keeping it off means working against a body that is built to regain it.

This guide covers what a realistic goal looks like, why regain is so common, what people who succeed long term tend to do, and where medication and surgery fit.

## Energy balance is true, and incomplete

Body weight does follow energy balance: take in less than you use and you lose weight. But the popular rule that cutting 3,500 kcal melts a pound of fat is too simple, because your body's energy use shifts as your weight does.

Modeling work published in The Lancet gives a better rule of thumb. A lasting change of about 24 kcal a day eventually shifts body weight by about 1 kg, but half of that change takes around a year to arrive and 95% takes around three years.[^2] Weight loss slows and plateaus even when you are doing everything "right".

The other half of the equation fights back too. After weight loss, appetite rises by roughly 100 kcal a day for every kilogram lost, a much stronger push than the small fall in energy expenditure.[^5] Our [metabolism explainer](/articles/metabolism-101) covers that side.

## What a modest loss buys you

You do not need to reach an "ideal" weight to benefit. The US obesity guideline concluded that a sustained loss of just 3% to 5% of body weight produces meaningful reductions in triglycerides, blood sugar and the risk of type 2 diabetes. Larger losses add improvements in blood pressure and cholesterol and can reduce the need for medication.[^1]

> [!STAT] 5% to 10%
> The amount of weight loss that comprehensive lifestyle programs typically achieve in six months, and enough to improve blood sugar, blood pressure and blood fats.[^1]

For someone who weighs 100 kg (220 lb), that is 5 to 10 kg, not 30.

## Why most diets work, briefly

The diet wars are mostly a draw. A network meta-analysis of 121 randomized trials with about 22,000 participants compared 14 named diets. Low-carbohydrate and low-fat approaches both produced around 4 to 5 kg more weight loss than usual eating at six months. By 12 months the weight advantage had shrunk and most of the improvements in cardiovascular risk factors had disappeared.[^3]

A year-long trial of 609 adults makes the same point from another angle. People assigned to a healthy low-fat diet lost 5.3 kg on average and those on a healthy low-carbohydrate diet lost 6.0 kg, a difference that was not significant. Within each group, results ranged from losing about 30 kg to gaining about 10 kg.[^4]

> [!NOTE] There is no best diet, only a best diet for you
> Average results between diets are similar, while individual results within any one diet vary enormously. The approach you can keep following matters more than its macronutrient ratio.[^4]

Then comes the drift. In long-term follow-up, more than half of lost weight is typically regained within two years, and more than 80% within five.[^5] That pattern reflects the appetite and energy changes described above. It is physiology doing its job, not a collapse of character.

## What people who keep it off have in common

By one estimate, about 20% of people with overweight manage to lose at least 10% of their weight and keep it off for a year or more.[^6] The US National Weight Control Registry has followed thousands of them. Its members lost an average of 33 kg and had kept it off for more than five years. They are volunteers, so this is description, not proof, but the patterns are consistent:[^6]

- **A lot of physical activity**, around an hour a day, most often walking.
- **Regular self-monitoring**, including frequent weighing.
- **A consistent eating pattern** across weekdays and weekends.
- **Eating breakfast.**
- **Catching small regains early**, before they become large ones.

Encouragingly, it gets easier. People who had maintained their loss for two to five years were much more likely to keep going.[^6]

Guidelines back the same ingredients: 200 to 300 minutes of activity a week for maintenance, weighing at least weekly, and continued contact with a program or professional for a year or more.[^1] Our guide to [building habits that stick](/articles/how-to-build-habits-that-stick) can help with the mechanics.

## Protect your muscle

Not all weight lost is fat. With dieting alone, typically around a quarter of it is lean tissue, including muscle.[^7] That matters most for older adults, who have less to spare.

Two things reduce the loss: eating more protein than the bare minimum, and exercising, particularly resistance training.[^7] See [how much protein you need](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need) and our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners).

## Sleep and stress count

Short sleep makes weight control harder, largely by increasing appetite. In a randomized trial, 80 adults with overweight who habitually slept less than 6.5 hours were coached to extend their sleep. They gained about 1.2 hours a night and, without any dietary advice, ate about 270 kcal a day less than the control group.[^8] Stress matters too. Many people eat more under pressure, which is why structured programs teach stress-management skills alongside diet and activity.[^1]

## Medication and surgery are legitimate tools

Obesity is a chronic condition with strong biological drivers, and treating it with medicine is no more "cheating" than treating high blood pressure.[^11]

- **Medication.** Prescription weight-management medicines are generally considered for adults with a body-mass index (BMI) of 30 or more, or 27 or more with a weight-related health problem, alongside changes in eating and activity.[^9] Newer drugs produce much larger losses than older ones. See [GLP-1 drugs explained](/articles/glp-1-weight-loss-drugs-explained).
- **Surgery.** Bariatric surgery produces the largest and most durable losses. In a US study, people who had gastric bypass were still about 27% below their starting weight 12 years later, while comparison groups had barely changed.[^10]

Both work best with long-term follow-up, and both have risks to weigh with a specialist.

## Health is not only the number on the scale

Weight stigma is common, including in healthcare, and an international consensus statement concluded that it harms health and is not supported by the science of how body weight is regulated.[^11] Shame is not a treatment.

It also helps to widen the goal. In a meta-analysis of ten studies, people who were unfit had about twice the risk of death of fit people whatever their BMI, and fit people with overweight or obesity fared about as well as fit people of normal weight.[^12] Moving more, eating better and sleeping well improve health even when weight changes less than you hoped.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor before a major weight-loss effort if you have diabetes, heart or kidney disease, are pregnant, or take medicines that may need adjusting. Ask about treatment options if your BMI is 30 or above, or 27 or above with a related condition.[^9] Seek help promptly if eating or weight is taking over your thoughts, or if you are restricting, bingeing or purging: these can be signs of an [eating disorder](/conditions/eating-disorders). Weight loss you did not intend should always be checked.

## The bottom line

Aim first for 5% to 10%, by a way of eating you could imagine keeping for years. Expect hunger to push back, and plan for maintenance from the start: plenty of activity, regular weighing, enough protein and sleep, and support that continues after the diet "ends". If that is not enough, medication and surgery are reasonable next steps, not failures. Whatever the scale does, getting fitter is a win in its own right.
