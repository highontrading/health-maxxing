For decades, weight-loss medicines were a disappointment: modest results, and a history of drugs withdrawn for safety. Then came a class of diabetes medicines that turned out to shrink appetite as well as blood sugar, and the conversation about obesity changed.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide are now among the most talked-about medicines in the world. This explainer covers how they work, what the big trials found, what they cost you in side effects, and what is still unknown.

## How they work

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is a hormone your gut releases after you eat. It tells the pancreas to release insulin, slows the emptying of your stomach and signals fullness to the brain. Your own GLP-1 is broken down within minutes. The medicines are engineered copies that last for days, which is why most are injected once a week.[^1]

- **Semaglutide** mimics GLP-1. It is sold under different brand names for type 2 diabetes and for weight management.
- **Tirzepatide** mimics two hormones, GLP-1 and GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide).[^3]

The net effect is that people feel less hungry, feel full sooner and eat less. These medicines do not "melt fat" or speed up metabolism.

## What the trials show

The main trials were large, randomized and placebo-controlled. They were funded by the manufacturers, and every participant, including those on placebo, was given diet and activity counseling.

| Trial | Who took part | Main result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| STEP 1 (semaglutide)[^2] | 1,961 adults with obesity or overweight, without diabetes | 14.9% average weight loss at 68 weeks, compared with 2.4% on placebo |
| SURMOUNT-1 (tirzepatide)[^3] | 2,539 adults with obesity or overweight, without diabetes | 15.0% to 20.9% at 72 weeks depending on dose, compared with 3.1% on placebo |
| SURMOUNT-5 (head to head)[^4] | 751 adults with obesity, without diabetes | 20.2% with tirzepatide and 13.7% with semaglutide at 72 weeks |
| SELECT (semaglutide)[^5] | 17,604 adults with cardiovascular disease and a BMI of 27 or more, without diabetes | 20% fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths over about three years |

> [!STAT] 14.9%
> Average weight loss with weekly semaglutide over 68 weeks in STEP 1, compared with 2.4% with placebo.[^2]

Two things are worth drawing out. First, averages hide a wide spread: in STEP 1 about a third of people on semaglutide lost 20% or more, while roughly one in seven lost less than 5%.[^2] Second, SELECT matters because it measured events, not just kilograms. Major cardiovascular events occurred in 6.5% of people on semaglutide and 8.0% on placebo.[^5]

## Side effects and warnings

The common side effects come from the gut. In STEP 1, nausea affected about 44% of people on semaglutide and 17% on placebo, and diarrhea, vomiting and constipation were also much more frequent. Symptoms were mostly mild to moderate, clustered around dose increases and eased with time, but about 7% of participants stopped treatment because of side effects.[^2]

Less common problems are more serious:[^1][^2]

- **Gallbladder disease,** including gallstones, which is also a known effect of rapid weight loss.
- **Pancreatitis,** an inflamed pancreas.
- **Low blood sugar** when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas in people with diabetes.
- **Dehydration and kidney strain** if vomiting or diarrhea is severe.

> [!WARNING]
> These medicines carry a warning about a rare type of thyroid tumor seen in animal studies. They are not prescribed to people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or the inherited syndrome MEN2. They should not be used during pregnancy, and anyone planning a pregnancy should discuss when to stop.[^1] Because they slow stomach emptying, tell your surgical team you take one before any procedure involving sedation or anesthesia.

## What happens when you stop

This is the least advertised finding. When 327 participants from STEP 1 were followed for a year after their last dose, they regained about two-thirds of the weight they had lost, and their blood pressure and blood sugar improvements faded with it.[^6]

Tirzepatide shows the same pattern. In SURMOUNT-4, people who had lost about 21% of their weight over 36 weeks were then switched to placebo or kept on treatment. Over the next year the placebo group regained 14%, while the group that continued lost a further 5.5%.[^7]

The medicines treat obesity. They do not cure it. That is why doctors increasingly describe them as long-term therapy, like treatment for blood pressure, and why cost and supply matter so much. ## Protecting muscle

Rapid weight loss takes some lean tissue with it. In body-scan subgroups of the two main trials, lean mass made up roughly a quarter to 40% of the weight lost.[^2][^3] Whether that harms strength or function in the long run is not yet clear.

The sensible response is the standard one for any weight loss: resistance exercise and enough protein, which can be hard when appetite is suppressed. See [strength training for beginners](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) and [how much protein you need](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need).

## Who qualifies

In the US, these medicines are approved for long-term weight management in adults with a body-mass index (BMI) of 30 or more, or 27 or more with at least one weight-related condition, alongside changes to diet and activity.[^1] Publicly funded systems tend to be stricter. In England, NICE recommended semaglutide for adults with a weight-related condition and a BMI of 35 or more, with lower thresholds for people from some ethnic backgrounds, as part of a specialist weight-management service.[^8]

They are prescription medicines for good reason. The dose is built up gradually under supervision, other medicines may need adjusting, and side effects need follow-up.

## Compounded and counterfeit products

High prices and shortages created a gray market. The US Food and Drug Administration warns that compounded versions of semaglutide and tirzepatide are not FDA-approved, meaning nobody has reviewed their safety, quality or effectiveness. It has received reports of people being hospitalized after measuring the wrong dose from multi-dose vials, has found counterfeit pens in the supply chain, and has flagged products sold online without a prescription or labeled "for research use".[^9]

Only use a product dispensed by a licensed pharmacy on a valid prescription.

## Pills and what comes next

Injections are not the end of the story.

- **Oral semaglutide.** A daily tablet produced about 15% weight loss over 68 weeks in a phase 3 trial, similar to the injection.[^10]
- **Orforglipron.** A different kind of once-daily pill that is not a peptide, so it can be taken without the fasting rules oral semaglutide needs. In a 72-week trial, weight fell by about 11% at the highest dose, compared with about 2% on placebo.[^11]
- **Retatrutide.** An injection that targets three hormone receptors. In a phase 2 trial, the highest dose produced about 24% weight loss in 48 weeks, and larger trials are under way.[^12]

Approvals and availability differ by country and change quickly, so check what is licensed where you live.

> [!DOCTOR]
> If you are taking one of these medicines, get urgent medical help for severe abdominal pain that does not go away (especially if it spreads to your back or comes with vomiting), yellowing of the skin or eyes, a sudden change in vision, signs of a severe allergic reaction, or vomiting or diarrhea so persistent that you cannot keep fluids down. Tell your doctor about a lump or swelling in your neck, low mood, or plans to become pregnant.

## The bottom line

GLP-1 medicines are the most effective drug treatment for obesity so far: about 15% to 21% average weight loss, and fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease. They also cause frequent gut side effects, carry rare serious risks, and work only for as long as you take them. If you meet the criteria, they are a legitimate medical option to discuss with a doctor, alongside eating well, strength training and enough protein. Skip anything sold without a prescription.
