Body-mass index is the health number people love to hate. It labels rugby players as obese, misses slim people carrying risky fat around their organs, and reduces a body to one figure. It is also still on almost every medical chart.

Both things can be true. BMI is a blunt but useful first look, and it gets much better when you add a tape measure. This explainer covers what BMI, waist size and body-fat percentage each tell you, and how to use them together.

## What BMI is

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by the square of your height in meters. Someone who is 1.75 m tall and weighs 80 kg has a BMI of 26.1. Our [BMI calculator](/tools/bmi) does the arithmetic. The WHO categories for adults are:[^1]

| Category | BMI | For some ethnic backgrounds (NICE) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underweight | Below 18.5 | Below 18.5 |
| Healthy weight | 18.5 to 24.9 | 18.5 to 22.9 |
| Overweight | 25 to 29.9 | 23 to 27.4 |
| Obesity | 30 or more | 27.5 or more |

The third column is explained below. On the standard cut-offs, the WHO estimates that 2.5 billion adults were overweight in 2022, including 890 million living with obesity.[^1]

## Why doctors still use it

BMI costs nothing, needs only a scale and a height measure, and tracks health outcomes well across large groups. In a pooled analysis of 239 studies and more than 10 million people, death rates were lowest at a BMI of 20 to 25. Compared with that range, they were about 45% higher at 30 to 35 and about 94% higher at 35 to 40. They were also higher below 20.[^2]

For populations, then, BMI works. The trouble starts when a population tool is used to judge one person.

## Where BMI goes wrong

**It cannot tell fat from muscle.** BMI is a measure of size, not composition. Muscular people can land in the overweight range with little body fat, and older adults who have lost muscle can have a "healthy" BMI with a lot.[^3]

**It ignores where fat sits.** Fat stored deep in the abdomen, around the organs, is more strongly tied to diabetes and heart disease than fat on the hips and thighs. BMI treats them the same.[^3]

**The cut-offs do not fit everyone.** At the same BMI, people of Asian descent tend to carry more body fat and develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower weights. A WHO expert consultation therefore suggested extra action points at 23 and 27.5 for Asian populations.[^4]

A study of 1.5 million adults in England put numbers on it. The BMI carrying the same diabetes risk as 30 in White adults was about 24 in South Asian adults, 27 in Chinese and Arab adults, and 28 in Black adults.[^5] NICE now recommends the lower thresholds in the table for people with a South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean family background.[^6]

> [!NOTE]
> Adult BMI categories do not apply to children and teenagers, who are assessed with age- and sex-specific growth charts, or during pregnancy.[^1]

## Waist: the measurement that adds the most

A tape measure captures what BMI misses, which is fat around the middle.

The evidence that it matters is strong. In a pooled analysis of 650,000 adults, a larger waist was linked to higher mortality at every level of BMI, including the "healthy" range. Each extra 5 cm of waist was associated with a 7% higher risk of death in men and 9% in women.[^7]

There are two ways to read the tape:

- **Waist circumference.** US guidance flags higher risk above 102 cm (40 inches) in men and 88 cm (35 inches) in women.[^8] The International Diabetes Federation draws the line lower: 94 cm (37 inches) for men and 80 cm (31.5 inches) for women of European origin, and 90 cm for South Asian and Chinese men.[^12]
- **Waist-to-height ratio.** Divide your waist by your height in the same units. NICE classes 0.4 to 0.49 as healthy, 0.5 to 0.59 as increased central adiposity and 0.6 or more as high, and it recommends the ratio for adults with a BMI under 35.[^6]

> [!STAT] 0.5
> The waist-to-height ratio to stay under. NICE's plain-language version: keep your waist to less than half your height.[^6]

The ratio has two advantages. One rule works for both sexes and all ethnic groups, and in a meta-analysis of more than 300,000 adults it predicted diabetes, high blood pressure and cardiovascular risk better than BMI or waist circumference alone.[^9] Try our [waist-to-height tool](/tools/waist-to-height).

> [!TIP] Measure in the right place
> Find the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hip bones, and wrap the tape halfway between, usually near your navel. Breathe out normally and do not pull the tape tight. If you are 170 cm tall, the target is a waist under 85 cm.

## Body-fat percentage: how far to trust it

Body-fat percentage sounds like the number that should settle the argument. In practice it is hard to measure well outside a lab.[^3]

| Method | How it works | How far to trust it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DXA scan | Low-dose X-rays separate fat, lean tissue and bone | The usual reference in clinics and research. Also shows where fat sits |
| Bioimpedance (smart scales) | A tiny electrical current estimates body water, and from that, fat | Shifts with hydration, meals and exercise. Better for trends than single readings |
| Skinfold calipers | Pinches at set sites go into an equation | Depends heavily on the skill of the person measuring |
| Tape-measure formulas | Girths such as neck and waist go into an equation | A rough estimate that is easy to repeat |
| Underwater weighing, air displacement | Body density is measured directly | Accurate, but confined to labs |

There are also no official cut-offs. One widely cited set of ranges, derived from BMI categories, puts a healthy body-fat percentage at roughly 8% to 19% for men and 21% to 32% for women aged 20 to 39, rising with age.[^10]

If you use a home device or our [body-fat estimator](/tools/body-fat), measure under the same conditions each time and watch the direction of travel over weeks.

## The 2025 rethink: clinical obesity

In 2025, an international commission published in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology proposed changing how obesity is diagnosed. Its main points:[^11]

- BMI should be used for screening and population statistics, not as the sole basis for diagnosing an individual.
- Excess body fat should be confirmed with a direct measurement or at least one other measure, such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio or waist-to-height ratio. A BMI above 40 can be taken as confirmation on its own.
- **Clinical obesity** is excess fat that is already impairing organs or everyday functioning, such as breathlessness, joint problems or raised blood sugar. **Preclinical obesity** is excess fat with normal organ function but higher future risk.

The aim is to direct treatment to people who are being harmed, and to stop labeling people as ill purely on a height-and-weight calculation. Health systems are still deciding how far to adopt it.

## Which number should you use?

For most adults the practical answer is two numbers, not one.

1. **Work out your BMI** as a first screen, using the lower thresholds if they apply to your background.
2. **Measure your waist** and compare it with your height. A ratio of 0.5 or more deserves attention even if your BMI is "healthy".
3. **Treat body-fat readings as optional extras** for tracking trends.

None of these is a verdict on your health. Blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and fitness complete the picture.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor if your BMI is 30 or more (27.5 or more with the lower thresholds), or if your waist is at least half your height, so that your blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol can be checked. Also seek advice if your BMI is below 18.5, or if you are losing weight without trying.

## The bottom line

BMI is a quick screen, not a diagnosis. Add your waist-to-height ratio, aim to keep your waist under half your height, and use lower BMI thresholds if you have an Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background. Home body-fat numbers are fine for following trends. Whatever the readings say, the next step is the same: have the numbers that really drive risk checked, then act on those.
