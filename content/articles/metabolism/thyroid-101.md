The thyroid gets blamed for a lot: stubborn weight, afternoon slumps, thinning hair. Sometimes the blame is deserved. Thyroid disease is common, especially in women, and its symptoms are easy to mistake for stress or aging. Often, though, the gland is fine and the supplements sold to "support" it are the real problem.

This explainer covers what the thyroid does, how fast and slow feel, what the tests mean, and how treatment works.

## What the thyroid does

The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland at the front of your neck. It makes two hormones, thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), that control how your body uses energy. They affect nearly every organ, including how fast your heart beats.[^1]

A gland in the brain, the pituitary, keeps it in check with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). When thyroid hormone runs low, the pituitary sends more TSH to push the thyroid harder. When there is too much, TSH falls.[^3] That seesaw is why TSH is the most useful single test.

## Running slow, running fast

Too little hormone is [hypothyroidism](/conditions/hypothyroidism). Too much is [hyperthyroidism](/conditions/hyperthyroidism). In the US, nearly 5 in 100 people aged 12 and over have hypothyroidism, mostly mild, and about 1 in 100 have hyperthyroidism. Both are more common in women and in people over 60.[^1][^2]

| | Underactive (hypothyroidism) | Overactive (hyperthyroidism) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weight | Gain | Loss, despite a bigger appetite |
| Temperature | Trouble tolerating cold | Trouble tolerating heat, sweating |
| Heart | Slow heart rate | Fast or irregular heartbeat |
| Energy and mood | Fatigue, low mood | Nervousness, irritability, poor sleep |
| Gut | Constipation | Frequent bowel movements |
| Muscles, skin and hair | Aches, dry skin, thinning hair | Shaky hands, muscle weakness |
| Other | Heavy or irregular periods | Swelling at the front of the neck (goiter) |

Hypothyroidism tends to creep in over months or years, so many people simply adjust to feeling slower.[^1] Older adults with an overactive thyroid may not look "sped up" at all, and can instead lose their appetite or withdraw.[^2]

## Why it happens

In countries where people get enough iodine, the usual cause of an underactive thyroid is Hashimoto's disease, in which the immune system attacks the gland. Other causes include thyroid surgery, radiation treatment and some medicines.[^1] The most common cause of an overactive thyroid is Graves' disease, another autoimmune condition, followed by overactive nodules and thyroid inflammation.[^2]

Worldwide, iodine matters more. The thyroid cannot make its hormones without it, and deficiency remains a major cause of goiter and hypothyroidism in parts of the world without iodized salt. Adults need about 150 micrograms a day, rising to 220 in pregnancy and 290 while breastfeeding. Iodized salt, seafood, dairy and eggs are the main sources.[^4]

More is not better. Very high iodine intakes, which are easy to reach with kelp or seaweed supplements, can trigger the same problems as too little, including goiter and thyroid dysfunction.[^4]

## How it is tested

Testing starts with TSH. A high TSH usually means the thyroid is underactive, and a low TSH usually means it is overactive. If TSH is abnormal, the lab measures the thyroid hormones themselves, usually free T4, and sometimes antibodies that point to Hashimoto's or Graves' disease.[^3]

Most labs use a TSH reference range of roughly 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L, though ranges vary a little between labs.[^5]

> [!WARNING] Biotin can distort your results
> High-dose biotin, found in many hair, skin and nail supplements, interferes with some lab methods. It has produced thyroid results that falsely suggested Graves' disease.[^8] Tell whoever orders your test about every supplement you take, and ask whether to pause biotin beforehand.

## The gray zone: subclinical hypothyroidism

Sometimes TSH is mildly raised while free T4 is still normal. This is called subclinical hypothyroidism, and it is the most debated corner of thyroid care, because a mildly raised TSH can settle without treatment.[^6]

US guidelines recommend considering treatment when TSH is above 10 mIU/L, and judging milder elevations on symptoms, thyroid antibodies and heart risk.[^5] In the UK, NICE advises confirming a raised TSH with a second test three months later before treating.[^6]

The caution is backed by evidence. In a trial of 737 adults aged 65 and over with subclinical hypothyroidism, a year of levothyroxine brought no improvement in thyroid symptoms or tiredness compared with a placebo.[^7] Pregnancy, or planning one, changes the calculation and needs individual advice.[^12]

## How treatment works

**Underactive thyroid.** The standard treatment is levothyroxine, a synthetic form of T4, taken once a day.[^1] It is absorbed poorly alongside food and some supplements, so US guidelines recommend taking it with water 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, or at bedtime at least four hours after your last meal, and keeping it apart from calcium and iron supplements.[^5] The right dose is found by rechecking TSH several weeks after each change. Most people take it for life.

**Overactive thyroid.** Options include antithyroid medicines, radioactive iodine and surgery, often with a beta blocker to calm symptoms in the meantime. Left untreated, hyperthyroidism can cause an irregular heartbeat and thin the bones.[^2]

> [!TIP] Make levothyroxine boring
> Same time, same way, every day. If you prefer taking it at night or cannot manage the gap before breakfast, tell your doctor instead of improvising. Consistency matters more than the exact routine.[^5]

## Three thyroid myths

**"My thyroid is why I cannot lose weight."** It can contribute, but less than people hope. The American Thyroid Association says about 5 to 10 pounds (2 to 5 kg) may be attributable to an underactive thyroid, much of it salt and water, and that massive weight gain is rarely due to hypothyroidism.[^9] See [metabolism 101](/articles/metabolism-101).

**"Thyroid-support supplements are a gentle natural option."** When researchers tested ten such products, nine contained measurable T3 or T4, real thyroid hormone in unlabeled amounts.[^10] That can cause symptoms of an overactive thyroid and wreck your test results.

**"More iodine means a healthier thyroid."** Only if you are deficient. In people who get enough, extra iodine can cause thyroid problems.[^4] Our guide to [who actually needs supplements](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them) has more.

## Nodules and pregnancy

Lumps in the thyroid are very common. By age 60, about half of people have a nodule that can be found by examination or imaging, and more than 90% are benign.[^11] They still need checking, usually with a TSH test and an ultrasound.

Pregnancy raises the stakes, because a baby depends on its mother's thyroid hormone in the first months. Untreated thyroid disease increases the risk of miscarriage, preterm birth and preeclampsia. If you take levothyroxine, contact your doctor as soon as you know you are pregnant, because the dose often needs to rise. About 1 in 20 women develop thyroid inflammation in the year after giving birth, which is easily mistaken for exhaustion or low mood.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ask for a thyroid blood test if you have several of the symptoms above for more than a few weeks, a family history of thyroid disease, or a lump or swelling in your neck. See a doctor promptly for a neck lump with hoarseness or trouble swallowing or breathing. Get emergency care for a very fast or irregular heartbeat with fever, agitation or confusion, or for extreme drowsiness and feeling very cold in someone with known thyroid disease.

## The bottom line

If you feel persistently slowed down or sped up, a TSH test is simple and usually gives a clear answer. Clearly abnormal results are very treatable, and borderline ones often just need repeating. Take levothyroxine consistently, mention biotin before any blood test, and skip the thyroid-support supplements and kelp tablets. They can cause thyroid problems and are unlikely to fix one.
