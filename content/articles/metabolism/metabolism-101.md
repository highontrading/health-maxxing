"I have a slow metabolism" may be the most common explanation people give for their weight, and "boost your metabolism" the most common promise on a supplement label. Both treat metabolism as a dial that is set low in some people and can be turned up with the right tea.

The real thing is more interesting and less mysterious. This explainer covers where your daily calories go, what sets the total, how it changes with age, and which popular claims survive contact with the evidence.

## Where your calories go

Metabolism is all the chemical work that keeps you alive. In everyday use it means your total daily energy expenditure, which has three parts:[^2][^5]

| Component | Typical share | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resting metabolic rate | About 60% to 70% | Running your brain, heart, liver, kidneys and every other cell while you do nothing |
| Thermic effect of food | About 10% | The cost of digesting and processing what you eat |
| Physical activity | The rest | Planned exercise plus all other movement |

The activity slice has two layers. One is deliberate exercise. The other is non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT: walking to the shops, climbing stairs, cooking, fidgeting. NEAT is the most variable part of the whole budget. It can differ by up to 2,000 kcal a day between two people of the same size, depending mostly on their jobs and how they spend their free time.[^2]

## Size sets the baseline

The biggest influence on your resting metabolic rate is simply how much of you there is, and especially how much fat-free mass you carry: muscle, organs, bone and water.

In a study of 150 Scottish adults, fat-free mass explained 63% of the differences in basal metabolic rate between people. Fat mass explained 6% and age 2%. Sex explained nothing extra once body composition was accounted for. About a quarter of the variation was left unexplained, so some people do run slightly hotter or cooler than predicted, but not dramatically.[^3]

This turns the "slow metabolism" story on its head. Larger bodies burn more energy at rest, not less.

It is also organs, not muscles, that do most of the resting work. Per kilogram, the heart and kidneys burn about 440 kcal a day, the brain about 240 and the liver about 200. Resting muscle burns about 13 kcal per kilogram and fat about 4.5.[^4]

## Age: steadier than you think

The best data on aging come from a 2021 analysis in Science that pooled measurements from 6,421 people in 29 countries, aged from 8 days to 95 years. All were measured with doubly labeled water, the reference method for energy use in daily life.[^1]

Adjusted for body size, energy expenditure peaks around the first birthday, at about 50% above adult levels. It declines through childhood and the teens, then stays remarkably flat from about 20 to 60. Only after 60 does it fall, by roughly 0.7% a year. People in their 90s burn about 26% less than people in midlife.[^1]

> [!STAT] 20 to 60
> The age span over which size-adjusted metabolism stayed stable in a study of 6,421 people.[^1]

So the familiar creep of weight through your 30s and 40s is not explained by a metabolism that has given up. Something else has changed, and how much you move and eat are the obvious candidates.

## Four popular claims, checked

**"Certain foods rev up your metabolism."** Slightly and briefly. Caffeine, green tea and the capsaicin in chili peppers nudge energy expenditure up, by around 50 kcal a day for capsaicin in one review, and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements judges their effect on body weight to be small at best.[^6] Protein does cost more to process than other nutrients: 20% to 30% of its calories go on digestion, compared with 5% to 10% for carbohydrate and 0% to 3% for fat.[^5] That is useful, not magic.

**"Muscle burns loads of calories at rest."** At about 13 kcal per kilogram a day, gaining 2 kg of muscle adds roughly 26 kcal a day at rest.[^4] [Strength training](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) earns its place for other reasons, including strength, blood sugar control and staying capable as you age.

**"Eating little and often stokes the fire."** The thermic effect of food depends on how much you eat in total, not on how you divide it up. A position stand from the International Society of Sports Nutrition concluded that eating more often does not meaningfully raise diet-induced thermogenesis, resting metabolic rate or total energy expenditure.[^7]

**"Dieting puts you into starvation mode."** Half true. When you lose weight you burn less, mostly because a smaller body costs less to run. On top of that, expenditure drops a little further than the lost tissue can explain, a response called adaptive thermogenesis.[^8]

How big is it? At the extreme, 14 contestants from the TV show The Biggest Loser were burning about 500 kcal a day less than expected for their size six years after their crash weight loss.[^9] More typically, a study of 171 women who lost about 12 kg on a low-calorie diet found an adaptation of roughly 50 kcal a day once their weight had stabilized, and it did not predict who regained.[^10]

What does not happen is the internet version: your body does not stop losing fat, or start storing it, because you ate too little. Rising appetite is the bigger obstacle, as we explain in [weight loss that lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts).

## The part you can actually move

You cannot do much about the running costs of your organs. Activity is the adjustable part, and everyday movement counts as much as gym time. Standing, walking and taking stairs all feed the NEAT side of the ledger.[^2]

There is a wrinkle. The body appears to budget. In a study of 332 adults from five populations, total energy expenditure rose with activity at the lower end, then flattened out among the most active people, as if the body trimmed spending elsewhere to compensate.[^11] This helps explain why exercise alone tends to produce less weight loss than calorie arithmetic predicts.

It is not a reason to skip it. Activity remains one of the best-supported things you can do for your heart, mood, blood sugar and lifespan, whatever the scale says. See [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need).

> [!TIP] Build movement into the day
> A desk job and an evening on the sofa can leave NEAT close to its floor. Walking meetings, stairs, a standing spell each hour and errands on foot add up without needing gym kit.

## Estimating your own number

Calculators estimate resting metabolic rate from your weight, height, age and sex, then multiply by an activity factor. In a systematic review, the Mifflin-St Jeor equation was the most reliable, landing within 10% of measured resting metabolic rate for more people than its rivals, though it can still be well off for an individual.[^12]

Our [TDEE calculator](/tools/tdee) gives you a starting estimate. Treat it as a first guess, and let your weight trend over a few weeks be the real test.

> [!DOCTOR]
> True metabolic problems exist, and the thyroid is the usual suspect. See a doctor about weight change you cannot explain, persistent tiredness, feeling unusually cold or hot, a racing heart, tremor or changes in your hair and skin. Our [thyroid guide](/articles/thyroid-101) lists the signs. Losing weight without trying always deserves a check.

## The bottom line

Your metabolism is mostly the cost of running your organs. It is set largely by your size, it is steady through adult life, and no food or supplement changes it much. What you can change is how much you move, in and out of the gym. If your weight is shifting, look at activity, appetite and eating before blaming a broken metabolism, and see a doctor if the change is unexplained.
