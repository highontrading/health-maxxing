Intermittent fasting has an appealing pitch: do not change what you eat, only when. Its fans credit it with weight loss, sharper thinking, cell renewal and a longer life.

Over the past decade the weight-loss claim has been put through proper randomized trials, and the answer is clear, if less exciting than the pitch. This evidence check covers what those trials found, where the bolder claims stand, and who should steer clear.

## What counts as intermittent fasting

The term covers several patterns that alternate eating with extended periods of little or no food:[^6]

| Type | How it works | Typical version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time-restricted eating | Eat within a set window each day and fast for the rest | 16:8, such as eating between noon and 8 pm |
| The 5:2 diet | Eat normally five days a week and very little on two | About 500 to 600 kcal on two non-consecutive days |
| Alternate-day fasting | Alternate "fast" days with unrestricted days | About 500 kcal, or nothing, every other day |

Water, black coffee and unsweetened tea are generally allowed during fasting periods.[^10]

## Does it beat ordinary dieting?

For weight loss, the fairest test is fasting against plain calorie restriction. Four trials tell the story.

**Eight-hour window plus calorie-cutting, for a year.** In China, 139 adults with obesity were all asked to cut calories. Half also had to eat only between 8 am and 4 pm. After 12 months the time-restricted group had lost 8.0 kg and the calorie-only group 6.3 kg. The 1.8 kg gap was not statistically significant, and body fat, blood pressure and blood sugar did not differ either.[^1]

**Alternate-day fasting, for a year.** In a US trial of 100 adults, alternate-day fasting produced a 6.0% weight loss and daily calorie restriction 5.3%, with no significant difference. More people quit the fasting group: 38% compared with 29%.[^2]

**Eight-hour window with no calorie counting.** In another US trial, 90 adults with obesity either ate between noon and 8 pm without counting anything, or cut calories by a quarter. After a year the two groups had lost similar amounts, about 4.6 kg and 5.4 kg more than a control group. The time-restricted group ended up eating around 425 kcal a day less without trying to.[^3]

**Same calories, different timing.** The cleanest test gave 41 adults all their food for 12 weeks, with identical calories. One group ate most of it early in a 10-hour window and the other followed a usual pattern. Weight loss was the same: 2.3 kg and 2.6 kg.[^4]

> [!STAT] 8.0 vs 6.3 kg
> Weight lost over a year with calorie restriction plus an eight-hour eating window, compared with calorie restriction alone. The difference was not statistically significant.[^1]

Pooled evidence agrees. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 99 randomized trials with about 6,600 adults found that every form of intermittent fasting lowered weight compared with unrestricted eating, and none clearly beat continuous calorie restriction. Alternate-day fasting came out about 1.3 kg ahead, a margin the authors judged too small to matter clinically.[^5] The American Diabetes Association's summary is similar: fasting regimens produce modest weight loss over two to three months, with no significant difference from continuous calorie restriction.[^6]

The fair conclusion is that fasting works, and it works by helping people eat less. For some, a simple rule about the clock is easier to follow than counting calories. For others it is harder. Neither group is doing it wrong.

## Is there something special about timing?

Advocates point to benefits beyond weight: better insulin sensitivity, lower blood pressure, cellular "housekeeping" and longer life. A widely cited review describes how fasting shifts the body from burning glucose to burning fat and ketones, and triggers stress-response pathways in cells. Much of that evidence comes from animals, and the authors acknowledged that long-term human data are lacking.[^7]

One intriguing human study confined eating to a six-hour window ending by 3 pm. Over five weeks, insulin sensitivity and blood pressure improved even though weight was held steady. It involved eight men with prediabetes, so it is a reason for more research, not a basis for health claims.[^8] The larger trials above found no metabolic advantage over calorie restriction alone.[^1][^4]

No trial has yet shown that intermittent fasting helps humans live longer.

## The downsides

**Lean mass.** In a 12-week US trial of 116 adults, eating between noon and 8 pm produced no significant weight loss compared with three regular meals (0.94 kg and 0.68 kg). In the subgroup who had body scans, about 65% of the weight lost on the fasting schedule was lean mass, well above the usual quarter or so.[^9] Other trials have not found excess lean loss, including the year-long Chinese study, so the question is open.[^1] It is still a good reason to keep protein up and do resistance training. See [how much protein you need](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need).

**Side effects and sticking with it.** Hunger, irritability and trouble concentrating are common in the first weeks while the body adapts.[^7] The higher dropout rate with alternate-day fasting suggests the stricter versions are hard to live with.[^2]

**What goes in the window.** Fasting says nothing about food quality. An eight-hour window of pastries is still a diet of pastries.

## Who should not fast

Johns Hopkins Medicine advises against intermittent fasting for:[^10]

- children and teenagers under 18
- people who are pregnant or breastfeeding
- people with type 1 diabetes who take insulin
- anyone with a history of an eating disorder

> [!WARNING]
> If you have diabetes and take insulin or a sulfonylurea, fasting can cause dangerously low blood sugar. Doses usually need adjusting and glucose needs closer monitoring, so plan any fasting with your diabetes team first.[^11][^6] The same goes for medicines that must be taken with food.

Fasting can also be a slippery slope for people prone to restricting and bingeing. If rules about eating start to dominate your day, stop and talk to someone. Our page on [eating disorders](/conditions/eating-disorders) lists the warning signs.

## If you want to try it

- **Start gently.** A 12-hour overnight gap, say 7 pm to 7 am, is a low-risk first step before trying a shorter window.
- **Pick a window you can keep** on workdays, weekends and social occasions. The best schedule is the one that survives real life.
- **Eat proper meals.** Build them around protein, vegetables, whole grains and beans, and keep up [strength training](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners).
- **Drink water** through the fasting hours.
- **Judge it after a month or two.** If you are hungrier, moodier or more preoccupied with food, a conventional approach will serve you just as well, as [weight loss that lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts) explains.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor before fasting if you have diabetes, take regular medication, are over 65 and losing weight unintentionally, or have any long-term condition. Stop and seek advice if you feel faint, shaky, confused or unwell while fasting. People with diabetes should treat those symptoms as possible low blood sugar and act on them immediately.

## The bottom line

Intermittent fasting is a legitimate way to lose weight, roughly as effective as cutting calories any other way, and no more. If a fixed eating window makes eating less feel easier, it is a reasonable tool. If it makes you miserable, you are missing nothing. Keep protein and strength training in the plan, and do not fast if you are pregnant, under 18, have a history of eating disorders, or take glucose-lowering medicines without medical guidance.
