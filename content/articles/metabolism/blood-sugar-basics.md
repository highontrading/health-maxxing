Blood sugar used to be something you thought about only if you had diabetes. Now glucose sensors are sold to gym-goers, and "spikes" have become something to fear at breakfast.

Some of that attention is useful, because raised blood sugar is common and silent for years. Some of it is noise. This explainer covers how your body handles glucose, what the test results mean, what is normal after a meal, and what helps.

## How glucose and insulin work

Glucose is the sugar your cells burn for energy. Most of it comes from the carbohydrates you eat, which are broken down in the gut and absorbed into the blood.

When blood glucose rises, the pancreas releases insulin. Insulin acts like a key, letting glucose move out of the blood and into muscle, fat and liver cells, where it is used or stored. Between meals, the liver releases stored glucose to keep the level steady. Diabetes develops when the pancreas makes too little insulin, or when cells stop responding to it properly, so glucose builds up in the blood.[^2]

## The tests, and where the lines are

Three tests are used. Fasting plasma glucose is measured after at least eight hours without food. The oral glucose tolerance test measures glucose two hours after a standard sugary drink. HbA1c (also written A1C) needs no fasting.[^2]

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria are:[^1]

| Test | Normal | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting glucose | Below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) | 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) | 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) | 5.7% to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol) | 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or higher |
| Two-hour glucose tolerance | Below 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) | 140 to 199 mg/dL (7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L) | 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher |

A random glucose of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or more in someone with classic symptoms also counts as diabetes. Without clear symptoms, the diagnosis needs two abnormal results.[^1]

> [!NOTE] The prediabetes line moves with the map
> Diabetes thresholds are the same almost everywhere, but prediabetes is defined differently. The WHO starts impaired fasting glucose at 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L), not 100.[^1] In the UK, NICE treats an HbA1c of 42 to 47 mmol/mol (6.0% to 6.4%) as high risk.[^4] A fasting result of 104 mg/dL (5.8 mmol/L) is prediabetes in the US and normal on WHO criteria.

### What HbA1c tells you

Glucose sticks to hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells. Because those cells live for about three months, the share of hemoglobin with glucose attached reflects your average blood sugar over the past two to three months.[^3]

That makes HbA1c convenient, but it is not foolproof. Anemia, kidney failure, liver disease, recent blood loss or transfusion, pregnancy and some inherited hemoglobin variants can push the result falsely high or low. In those cases, doctors rely on glucose tests instead.[^3]

## Do healthy people "spike"?

Yes, and it is normal. Blood glucose is meant to rise after you eat and fall again as insulin does its work.

The best picture of normal comes from a study in which 153 people without diabetes, aged 7 to 80, wore continuous glucose monitors. Their average glucose was 98 to 99 mg/dL (about 5.5 mmol/L). They spent a median of 96% of the time between 70 and 140 mg/dL (3.9 to 7.8 mmol/L), and went above 140 mg/dL for a median of 2.1% of the time, roughly 30 minutes a day.[^5]

> [!STAT] 96%
> The share of the day that healthy people without diabetes spent with glucose between 70 and 140 mg/dL (3.9 to 7.8 mmol/L).[^5]

So a brief rise to 140 or 150 mg/dL after a bowl of pasta is ordinary physiology, not a warning sign.

## Glucose monitors for people without diabetes

Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) are small sensors worn on the arm that estimate glucose every few minutes from the fluid under the skin. For people with diabetes who use insulin, they are a major advance. In 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration cleared the first over-the-counter CGM, for adults who do not use insulin.[^6]

Whether they help people with normal blood sugar is another matter. A 2023 expert review found the evidence limited and inconsistent, with no good trials showing that wearing one improves health in people without diabetes.[^7]

The data are also hard to interpret. In a study of 800 adults, the same foods produced strikingly different glucose responses in different people, so one person's "bad" food is another's non-event.[^8] Without clear thresholds for what is harmful in healthy people, it is easy to end up anxious about normal readings or cutting out nutritious foods.

If you are curious, treat a sensor as an interesting gadget, not a diagnostic test. For screening and diagnosis, doctors rely on fasting glucose and HbA1c.[^1]

## What actually helps

The habits with the best evidence are not exotic.

- **Favor fiber-rich, minimally processed carbohydrates.** The ADA advises building meals around non-starchy vegetables, whole grains, beans and whole fruit, cutting back on refined grains and added sugar, and choosing water over sugary drinks.[^9] See [carbs explained](/articles/carbs-explained) and our [fiber guide](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient).
- **Move after you eat.** Working muscles pull glucose out of the blood. A meta-analysis found that breaking up sitting with short bouts of light walking lowered post-meal glucose and insulin compared with sitting still, and worked better than simply standing.[^10] The ADA recommends interrupting long periods of sitting every 30 minutes.[^9]
- **Protect your sleep.** In a lab study of healthy young adults, nights limited to five hours of sleep reduced whole-body insulin sensitivity by about 13%, and sleeping in at the weekend did not undo the effect.[^11]
- **Stay active and manage weight.** Regular exercise and modest weight loss are the most effective ways to reverse early problems. See [insulin resistance and prediabetes](/articles/insulin-resistance-and-prediabetes).

## When to get tested

Raised blood sugar usually causes no symptoms until it is quite high, so testing matters more than how you feel. The ADA recommends screening all adults from age 35, and earlier for people with overweight or obesity plus another risk factor, such as a close relative with diabetes or a history of diabetes in pregnancy. If the result is normal, it should be repeated at least every three years.[^1] The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults aged 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ask for a test if you are very thirsty, pass urine much more than usual, are losing weight without trying, feel unusually tired or have blurred vision.[^1][^2] Get emergency care if these come with vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, breath that smells fruity, or drowsiness and confusion. These can be signs of diabetic ketoacidosis, which is life-threatening.

## The bottom line

Know one of two numbers: your fasting glucose or your HbA1c. A fasting glucose below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or an HbA1c below 5.7% is normal on US criteria, and anything higher is worth a conversation, not a panic. Glucose rising after meals is how a healthy body works. You do not need a sensor to look after your blood sugar: eat more fiber and fewer refined carbohydrates, walk after meals, sleep enough and get tested on schedule.
