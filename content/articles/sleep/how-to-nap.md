A good nap is one of the few performance boosters that is free, legal and quicker than the queue for coffee. A bad one leaves you groggy at 4 p.m. and wide awake at midnight.

The difference comes down to three things: how long you sleep, what time you do it, and whether you should be napping at all. Here is how to get each one right.

## Why the afternoon slump happens

Two systems decide how sleepy you feel. The first is sleep pressure, which builds steadily from the moment you wake up. The second is your [body clock](/articles/circadian-rhythm-and-light), which sends out alerting signals through the day to hold that pressure back.

In the early afternoon the alerting signal sags for a while as sleep pressure keeps climbing. The result is the post-lunch dip, which has more to do with your clock than with your lunch.[^1] A nap in that window works with your biology: it takes the edge off sleep pressure at the time of day when dozing off is easiest.

## Choose your nap length

The best-known experiment on nap length took 24 young adults who had slept only about five hours the night before and gave them afternoon naps of 5, 10, 20 or 30 minutes, or no nap at all.[^2]

- **5 minutes** did very little.
- **10 minutes** improved alertness and mental performance straight away, and some of the benefits lasted more than two hours.
- **20 minutes** also helped, but the benefits took about half an hour to show up.
- **30 minutes** left people less alert than before for a while, then produced benefits that lasted more than two hours.

That temporary slump has a name: sleep inertia. It is the heavy, confused feeling you get on waking, it is worst when you are pulled out of deep sleep, and it usually fades within about half an hour.[^3] It is also the reason a long nap just before driving or an exam is a poor idea.

Longer naps have their uses. In one experiment, naps of 60 to 90 minutes that included both deep sleep and REM sleep improved performance on a learning task as much as a full night of sleep did.[^4] The cost is that a long nap uses up sleep pressure you may want at bedtime.

| Nap length | Best for | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 to 20 minutes | A fast lift in alertness | Benefits last a couple of hours, not all day |
| 30 to 60 minutes | Extra recovery if you have time to wake up slowly | Highest risk of grogginess |
| About 90 minutes | Catching up after a very short night, or before a night shift | Can make it harder to fall asleep that night |

> [!STAT] 10 minutes
> The nap length that produced the quickest improvement in alertness, with no grogginess, in a study comparing naps of 5 to 30 minutes.[^2]

## How to nap, step by step

1. **Go early.** Nap in the early afternoon, when the dip makes it easy to drop off. The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) advises against naps after 3 p.m., because late naps make it harder to fall asleep at night.[^5]
2. **Set an alarm.** The NHLBI suggests that adults nap for no more than 20 minutes.[^6] Allow a few minutes to fall asleep and set the alarm for about 25.
3. **Get comfortable.** Find somewhere dim, quiet and cool. Lie down if you can. An eye mask and earplugs turn a parked car or a quiet office into a decent nap spot.
4. **Do not force it.** If you only drift, that is fine. Get up when the alarm goes rather than chasing more sleep.
5. **Wake up properly.** Stand, move and get into bright light. Give any grogginess time to clear before you drive or make important decisions.[^3]

## The coffee nap

It sounds backwards, but it is one of the better-tested alertness tricks. Drink a cup of coffee, then nap straight away for about 15 minutes. Caffeine takes a while to reach full effect, so it starts working around the time you wake up.

In a driving-simulator study of sleep-deprived drivers, caffeine followed by a nap of less than 15 minutes reduced driving errors more than caffeine on its own.[^7]

Keep coffee naps to the early afternoon. Caffeine lingers for many hours, as we explain in [caffeine, alcohol and your sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep).

## What NASA learned from sleepy pilots

In the early 1990s, NASA researchers studied airline crews on long-haul flights. Some pilots were given a planned 40-minute rest period in their seats during the cruise, taking turns so that someone was always flying. They fell asleep within a few minutes and slept for an average of about 26 minutes.[^8]

Compared with crews who had no rest break, the pilots who napped reacted faster on performance tests and showed far fewer signs of sleepiness, such as brief lapses into sleep, during the last part of the flight.[^8]

That study is where the "26-minute NASA nap" comes from. Bear in mind who was tested: tired crews crossing time zones. A rested office worker should expect a smaller effect.

> [!WARNING]
> A nap is a stopgap, not a substitute for a night's sleep. If you are fighting sleep at the wheel, pull off the road somewhere safe and nap for 15 to 20 minutes before you carry on.[^5]

## When not to nap

**If you have insomnia.** Sleep pressure is what gets you to sleep at night, and a nap spends some of it. Avoiding daytime naps is a standard instruction in the behavioral treatment for insomnia.[^9] See [what actually works for insomnia](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works).

**Late in the day.** A nap after about 3 p.m. is borrowing from tonight.[^5]

**Instead of enough sleep.** If you need a nap to function every day, first check whether you are getting [the sleep you need](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need) at night.

### When napping is a warning sign

An occasional nap is a healthy habit. A growing need for long daily naps is different. A meta-analysis found that people who napped for an hour or more a day had a higher risk of cardiovascular disease and of death from any cause than people who did not nap. Naps shorter than an hour showed no such link.[^10]

Nobody has shown that the naps cause the harm. Long naps may simply reflect poor sleep at night or an illness that has not yet been diagnosed.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you often doze off without meaning to, if you need long naps on most days even though you spend enough time in bed, or if you wake from a full night still unrefreshed.[^11] Loud snoring with pauses in breathing suggests [sleep apnea](/articles/snoring-and-sleep-apnea). Sudden, irresistible sleep attacks also need a medical assessment.

## The bottom line

Nap early in the afternoon, keep it to 10 to 20 minutes, and set an alarm. Try a coffee nap when you really need to be sharp, and skip naps altogether if you struggle to sleep at night. If long naps have become a daily necessity, treat that as information and find out why.
