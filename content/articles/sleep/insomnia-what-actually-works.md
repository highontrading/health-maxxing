At 3 a.m., insomnia feels like a personal failing. It is in fact one of the most common health complaints there is. Roughly 6% to 10% of adults have insomnia that meets the criteria for a disorder, and many more have a bad patch now and then.[^2]

The good news is that chronic insomnia is very treatable, and the best-proven treatment is not a pill. This guide explains why sleeplessness becomes self-sustaining, what the guidelines recommend first, where medication and melatonin fit, and when to see a doctor.

## What counts as insomnia

Insomnia means trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or getting good-quality sleep, even though you have the time and the right conditions to sleep.[^1] That last part matters. A new parent or a shift worker who cannot get enough sleep has a different problem.

Doctors separate two kinds:[^1]

- **Short-term insomnia** lasts days or weeks and usually has an obvious trigger, such as stress or a change in your schedule or surroundings.
- **Chronic insomnia** happens on three or more nights a week, lasts more than three months and cannot be fully explained by another health problem.

## Why it sticks: the three Ps

Why does a rough week turn into a rough year for some people? Sleep researchers use a model known as the three Ps, set out by the psychologist Arthur Spielman in the 1980s.[^4]

- **Predisposing** factors make you vulnerable, such as a family tendency to poor sleep or a mind that runs hot with worry.
- **Precipitating** factors set off a bad spell: a deadline, an illness, a bereavement, a new baby.
- **Perpetuating** factors keep it going after the trigger has passed.

The third P is the cruel one, because it is made of the things people naturally do to cope: going to bed early, lying in, napping, and spending long hours in bed "at least resting". More time in bed spreads the same sleep more thinly, and hours spent awake there teach your brain to treat the bed as a place for frustration. That is why you can doze off on the sofa and be wide awake the moment your head touches the pillow.

Treatment aims at the third P, because it is the one you can change.

## What works best: CBT-I

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a short, structured program, usually four to eight sessions, that dismantles those perpetuating habits.[^3] The American College of Physicians (ACP) recommends it as the first treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the 2023 European guideline agree.[^2][^3][^6]

| Component | What you do | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stimulus control | Go to bed only when sleepy, get up if you cannot sleep, rise at a fixed time | Rebuilds the link between bed and sleep |
| Sleep restriction | Limit time in bed to roughly the hours you actually sleep, then extend it gradually | Builds sleep pressure and consolidates sleep |
| Cognitive therapy | Examine beliefs such as "I'll be useless tomorrow" | Lowers the anxiety that keeps you awake |
| Relaxation | Breathing or muscle-relaxation exercises | Calms a revved-up body |

How well does it work? A meta-analysis of 20 trials found that CBT-I helped people fall asleep 19 minutes faster and cut the time they spent awake in the night by 26 minutes, with the gains still present at later follow-up.[^5] Total sleep time rose by only about 8 minutes. That sounds underwhelming until you see what changed: much the same sleep, with far less lying awake.

> [!STAT] 26 minutes
> The average drop in time spent awake during the night after CBT-I, across 20 trials.[^5]

CBT-I is recommended whether insomnia occurs on its own or alongside another condition, such as depression, anxiety or chronic pain.[^3]

> [!WARNING]
> Sleep restriction makes you sleepier before it makes you better. Take extra care with driving in the first weeks. The AASM notes it may not be suitable for people with bipolar disorder or poorly controlled seizures, or for those in jobs where drowsiness is dangerous, so check with a clinician first.[^3]

### No therapist nearby?

Trained CBT-I therapists are scarce. App-based and online programs deliver the same components, and the European guideline accepts digital CBT-I as a first-line option.[^6] In 2022 England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommended one program, Sleepio, as a cost-saving alternative to sleep hygiene advice or sleeping pills in primary care.[^7]

Good [sleep habits](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) are part of CBT-I too, but the AASM advises against relying on them alone to treat chronic insomnia.[^3]

## Where medication fits

Guidelines put sleep medication second. The ACP suggests considering it only when CBT-I alone has not worked, after a frank discussion of benefits, harms and costs, and for short-term use.[^2]

The prescription options include the "Z-drugs" (zolpidem, zaleplon and eszopiclone), benzodiazepines, low-dose doxepin, ramelteon and a newer class called orexin receptor antagonists (suvorexant, lemborexant and daridorexant). The last group works by blocking orexin, a brain chemical that keeps you awake.[^8][^9]

The evidence is thinner than you might expect. The AASM's own drug guideline rated every one of its recommendations as "weak".[^8] A network meta-analysis of 154 trials and more than 44,000 people found that eszopiclone and lemborexant had the best overall record. But eszopiclone can cause substantial side effects, the safety data on lemborexant were inconclusive, and long-term data were missing for many drugs.[^9]

All sleeping pills can leave you drowsy the next day, and some can be habit-forming, which is why they are meant for short courses. Rare but serious side effects include walking, eating or driving while not fully awake.[^12]

### Melatonin and over-the-counter sleep aids

Melatonin is more of a body-clock signal than a sedative, and for insomnia its effect is small. Pooled trials found that it helped people fall asleep about 7 minutes faster and sleep about 8 minutes longer than a placebo.[^10]

The AASM suggests not using melatonin for chronic insomnia. The European guideline is slightly more permissive, allowing prolonged-release melatonin for up to three months in people aged 55 and over.[^8][^6] Melatonin has a better case for clock problems such as [jet lag](/articles/circadian-rhythm-and-light).

Short-term use appears to be safe for most adults, but information on long-term safety is lacking. In the United States melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, which is regulated less strictly than a medicine, and some products do not contain what the label says.[^11]

The AASM also suggests not using the antihistamine diphenhydramine or the herbal remedy valerian for chronic insomnia.[^8]

## When to see a doctor

Insomnia is sometimes a symptom of something else. Other sleep disorders such as sleep apnea and restless legs, physical and mental health conditions, alcohol and a long list of medicines can all disturb sleep, which is why guidelines ask doctors to check for them before settling on a treatment.[^6]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you have trouble sleeping on three or more nights a week for more than a few weeks, or sooner if tiredness is affecting your mood, your work or your driving. Mention loud snoring or gasping in your sleep (possible [sleep apnea](/articles/snoring-and-sleep-apnea)), an urge to move your legs at night, low mood or worry, and everything you take, including caffeine and alcohol.

If [anxiety](/articles/understanding-anxiety) or low mood is part of the picture, say so. Insomnia that comes with another condition still deserves treatment in its own right.[^3]

## The bottom line

A few bad nights are normal and usually pass. If insomnia has settled in for months, ask your doctor about CBT-I, in person or as a digital program, before reaching for pills or supplements. Medication can help for a short stretch, but the durable fix is retraining your sleep.
