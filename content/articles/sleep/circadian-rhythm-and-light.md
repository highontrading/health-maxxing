You have a clock in your head. It decides when you feel sharp, when you feel sleepy, when your body temperature dips and when certain hormones surge, and it keeps doing so even if you are shut in a windowless room.

That clock is your circadian rhythm, from the Latin for "about a day". Understanding it explains a lot: why Monday mornings hurt, why teenagers cannot get up, why night shifts are so hard on the body, and why a morning walk can do more for your sleep than an expensive mattress.

## A clock in almost every cell

Nearly every tissue and organ in your body contains its own biological clock, built from genes and proteins that switch each other on and off in a loop lasting about 24 hours.[^1]

Keeping them in step is the job of a master clock: a cluster of about 20,000 nerve cells called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), which sits in the hypothalamus and receives signals directly from the eyes. Among its jobs is controlling the release of melatonin, the hormone that makes you sleepy at night.[^1]

There is a catch. The clock does not run on exactly 24 hours. In a tightly controlled Harvard study, the natural day of the human clock averaged 24.18 hours, or about 24 hours and 11 minutes.[^2] Left to itself, your clock would drift a little later every day. Something has to reset it.

> [!STAT] 24.2 hours
> The average length of the human body clock's natural day, measured under tightly controlled laboratory conditions.[^2]

## Light is the reset button

That something is light. Specialized cells in the retina, most sensitive to blue wavelengths, report directly to the SCN and act as the clock's main time cue.[^3]

Timing decides the direction. Light in the morning pulls the clock earlier. Light in the evening and at night pushes it later.[^3] Meals and physical activity also act as cues, but light is the strongest.[^9]

Evening light also holds back melatonin, which your brain normally releases as darkness falls. In an experiment with 116 volunteers, ordinary room lighting in the hours before bedtime delayed the start of melatonin release in 99% of people and shortened its duration by about 90 minutes, compared with dim light.[^4]

Modern life gets the pattern backwards: dim days indoors, bright evenings. Daylight can reach tens of thousands of lux, the unit of illumination, while typical office lighting provides a few hundred.[^3] When eight people spent a week camping with only sunlight and a campfire, their melatonin began to rise about two hours earlier, in step with sunset.[^5]

> [!TIP] Bright days, dim evenings, dark nights
> An international panel of light and sleep scientists recommends bright light throughout the day, ideally daylight, dim light for the three hours before bed, and a bedroom that is as dark as possible.[^6] In practice: get outside in the morning, sit near a window, and switch to low, warm lamps after dinner.

## Larks, owls and teenagers

People differ in when their clocks are set, a trait called chronotype. Morning "larks" and evening "owls" are not showing virtue or laziness. Chronotype is partly inherited and it changes across life.

The clearest shift comes at puberty, when the clock moves later by up to two hours. A teenager who cannot fall asleep before 11 p.m. and cannot wake at 6:30 a.m. is following biology. This is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m.[^7]

### Social jet lag

When your body clock and your alarm clock disagree, you get social jet lag: sleeping at very different times on work days and on free days, as if you flew across a couple of time zones every weekend.

It is remarkably common. In a database of about 65,000 mostly European adults, roughly two-thirds had at least an hour of social jet lag and a third had two hours or more. Those with more of it were more likely to be overweight.[^8] That is an association, not proof of cause, but it suggests that when you sleep matters as well as [how much](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need).

## When the clock and the world collide

### Shift work

Night work asks you to be alert when your clock says sleep, and to sleep when it says wake. Many shift workers never fully adapt, and some develop shift work disorder, with insomnia and heavy sleepiness. Long-term circadian disruption is linked with higher risks of obesity, diabetes, heart problems and mood disorders.[^9]

In 2019 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified night shift work as "probably carcinogenic to humans", based on limited evidence in people and stronger evidence from laboratory studies.[^10] "Probably" is doing real work in that phrase: the human evidence is not conclusive.

If you work nights, the usual advice is to protect a regular block of daytime sleep in a dark, quiet room, and to talk to a doctor if you cannot stay awake at work or sleep afterwards.[^9] A [planned nap](/articles/how-to-nap) before a shift can help too.

### Jet lag

After a long flight, your clock stays on home time and catches up gradually. Symptoms usually improve within a few days.[^12] Flying west is generally easier than flying east, which fits a clock that naturally runs long.

What helps:

- **Shift before you go.** Move your sleep routine toward the new time zone for a few days before you travel.[^12]
- **Use daylight.** Get outside during the day at your destination and sleep when it is night there.[^12]
- **Consider melatonin.** A Cochrane review concluded that melatonin taken close to bedtime at the destination reduces jet lag on flights across five or more time zones, especially eastward.[^11] The NHS takes a more cautious line and does not recommend it, citing too little evidence.[^12] If you use it, follow the label and check with a pharmacist first if you take other medicines.

## How to work with your clock

The research above boils down to a handful of habits:[^6][^9]

- **Fix your wake-up time.** It anchors everything else. More on this in our list of [sleep habits that help](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work).
- **Get light early.** Daylight soon after waking is the strongest "it is morning" signal you can send.
- **Dim the evening.** Lower the lights and put screens away before bed.
- **Limit weekend drift.** A long lie-in gives you a dose of social jet lag on Monday.
- **Shift gradually.** To move your schedule earlier, bring bedtime and wake-up time forward in small steps, with bright light on waking.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if your sleep times are so late, so early or so irregular that you cannot meet work, school or family commitments, and this has gone on for months. Circadian rhythm disorders are diagnosable and treatable, usually with carefully timed light, melatonin and schedule changes.[^9] If the problem is lying awake at a normal bedtime, read about [insomnia](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works) instead.

## The bottom line

Your body clock runs a little slow and relies on light to stay on time. Give it bright mornings, dim evenings, dark nights and a consistent wake-up time, and most of the rest follows. If your schedule forces you to live against your clock, protect your sleep where you can and get help if you are not coping.
