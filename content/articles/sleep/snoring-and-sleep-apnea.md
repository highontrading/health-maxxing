Snoring is the soundtrack of millions of bedrooms and the cause of countless elbow jabs. Most of the time it is only noise. Sometimes it is the audible part of obstructive sleep apnea, a condition in which breathing stops again and again during sleep, and which often goes undiagnosed for years.

This guide explains how to tell the two apart, which signs deserve a doctor's attention, how testing works, and what the treatments, old and new, can do.

## Snoring versus sleep apnea

Snoring happens when your tongue, mouth, throat or airways vibrate as you breathe during sleep. It is more likely if you are overweight, smoke, drink a lot of alcohol or sleep on your back.[^3]

In obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the upper airway does more than vibrate. It narrows or closes, over and over. Each pause lasts 10 seconds or longer, oxygen levels dip, and your brain rouses you just enough to restart breathing, usually without your knowing.[^6]

Doctors grade OSA by the number of these events per hour of sleep, a figure called the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI). Five to 14 an hour is mild, 15 to 29 is moderate, and 30 or more is severe.[^6] A rarer form, central sleep apnea, happens when the brain briefly stops sending the signal to breathe.[^1]

> [!STAT] 936 million
> The estimated number of adults aged 30 to 69 worldwide with obstructive sleep apnea. About 425 million of them have a moderate or severe form.[^4]

## Signs you should not ignore

People with OSA are often the last to know, because the main events happen while they are asleep. A bed partner usually notices first. The NHS lists these symptoms:[^2]

**During sleep**

- breathing that stops and starts
- gasping, snorting or choking noises
- loud snoring
- waking up a lot

**During the day**

- feeling very tired
- finding it hard to concentrate
- mood swings
- a headache when you wake up

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you snore and someone has noticed pauses in your breathing, gasping or choking, or if you are regularly sleepy in the daytime. Do not drive when you feel sleepy, and get help promptly if you have ever nodded off at the wheel.

## Who is at risk

OSA is more common in people who are overweight, have a large neck, are older, have a family history of the condition, smoke or drink alcohol, or have large tonsils or adenoids.[^2] Men are affected more often than women.

Doctors often use a quick eight-item checklist called STOP-Bang. Count one point for each "yes":[^5]

| Letter | Question |
| --- | --- |
| S | Do you **snore** loudly? |
| T | Do you often feel **tired** or sleepy in the daytime? |
| O | Has anyone **observed** you stop breathing in your sleep? |
| P | Do you have high blood **pressure**? |
| B | Is your **BMI** over 35? |
| A | Is your **age** over 50? |
| N | Is your **neck** more than 40 cm (about 16 inches) around? |
| G | Is your **gender** male? |

A score of 0 to 2 suggests a low risk of moderate to severe OSA, 3 to 4 an intermediate risk, and 5 to 8 a high risk.[^5] You can work out your body mass index with our [BMI calculator](/tools/bmi).

A checklist is a prompt to get tested, not a diagnosis. And if you have no symptoms at all, routine screening is not currently advised: the US Preventive Services Task Force concluded in 2022 that there is too little evidence to judge its benefits and harms.[^8]

## Why untreated apnea matters

Broken sleep and repeated drops in oxygen put a strain on the body. Untreated OSA is linked with high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, low mood and a higher chance of serious accidents caused by tiredness.[^2]

The link with heart disease is close. According to the American Heart Association, OSA is found in as many as 40% to 80% of people with conditions such as high blood pressure, heart failure, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation and stroke, and it is often missed.[^6] If your [blood pressure](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) is hard to control and you snore, tell your doctor both facts.

## Getting tested

The diagnosis is made with a sleep study. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) recommends either an overnight study in a sleep laboratory, called polysomnography, or a home sleep apnea test for otherwise uncomplicated adults whose symptoms point to moderate or severe OSA. If a home test comes back negative or unclear, a laboratory study should follow.[^7]

A home test usually means wearing a small device overnight that records your breathing and oxygen levels.[^2]

## Treatments that work

### CPAP

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the standard treatment for moderate to severe OSA. A bedside machine blows air through a mask at just enough pressure to hold your airway open.[^1]

The AASM recommends it for adults with OSA and excessive sleepiness, and suggests it for those with impaired quality of life or high blood pressure. In the trials behind that guideline, it reduced breathing pauses and sleepiness and, modestly, blood pressure.[^9]

It is worth being honest about what CPAP has not been shown to do. In a trial of 2,717 adults with OSA and existing cardiovascular disease, CPAP did not reduce heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths over almost four years, although it did improve sleepiness, mood and quality of life. Participants used it for an average of only 3.3 hours a night, which may be part of the explanation.[^10]

### Oral appliances

A mandibular advancement device looks like a gum shield and holds the lower jaw forward to keep the airway open while you sleep. It is used less often than CPAP and may not work as well, but it is an alternative for people who cannot get on with a mask.[^2]

### Weight loss and a new medication

Excess weight is one of the main drivers of OSA, and milder cases can sometimes be managed with lifestyle changes such as losing weight, stopping smoking and drinking less alcohol.[^2]

That is the logic behind the first medication for the condition. In two year-long trials in adults with obesity and moderate to severe OSA, tirzepatide cut breathing events by about 25 to 29 an hour, compared with about 5 an hour with a placebo. Participants lost roughly 18% to 20% of their body weight.[^11] In December 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration approved tirzepatide (sold as Zepbound) for this group, alongside a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity.[^12]

It is a prescription medicine with side effects, mostly digestive, and it was tested only in people whose apnea came with obesity.[^11] Read more in our guide to [GLP-1 weight-loss drugs](/articles/glp-1-weight-loss-drugs-explained).

### Position and other options

Some people have apnea mainly when they lie on their back. Sleeping on your side can help, with a special pillow or bed wedge to keep you there.[^2] For selected people who cannot tolerate CPAP, surgery or an implanted device that stimulates the nerve controlling the tongue may be offered.[^1]

## If it is only snoring

Simple snoring does not need medical treatment, but your household may disagree. The NHS suggests losing weight if you are overweight, sleeping on your side, cutting down on alcohol and stopping smoking. It also advises against sleeping pills, which can make snoring worse.[^3] Alcohol relaxes the throat muscles, as we explain in [caffeine, alcohol and your sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep).

> [!TIP] The tennis ball trick
> To stay off your back, tape or sew a tennis ball to the back of your sleepwear. It is an old trick, and the NHS still suggests it.[^3]

## The bottom line

Snoring on its own is a nuisance. Snoring with breathing pauses, gasping or daytime sleepiness is a reason to ask for a sleep study, which can often be done at home. Sleep apnea is common and treatable, and treating it usually improves how you feel during the day. Our [sleep apnea reference page](/conditions/sleep-apnea) has a quick summary.
