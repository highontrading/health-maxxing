Sleep is the one health habit you can do lying down, and plenty of us still do not get enough of it. In the United States, about 3 in 10 adults sleep less than the recommended minimum.[^4]

Part of the problem is that "enough" sounds like a matter of opinion. It is not. Expert panels have sifted thousands of studies and agreed on ranges for every age. This guide lays them out, explains what falling short does, and answers the questions people really ask: whether weekend lie-ins count, whether quality beats quantity, and whether you might be one of the lucky few who need less.

## The numbers, by age

For adults, the headline figure comes from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the Sleep Research Society. Their 15-person panel reviewed more than 5,000 scientific papers and concluded that adults should sleep 7 or more hours a night, on a regular basis, for the best health.[^1]

> [!STAT] 7+ hours
> The nightly minimum for adults aged 18 to 60, according to the AASM and Sleep Research Society consensus.[^1]

Children and teenagers need considerably more. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) gathers the expert recommendations into one table:[^2]

| Age | Sleep per 24 hours |
| --- | --- |
| Newborns (0 to 3 months) | 14 to 17 hours |
| Infants (4 to 12 months) | 12 to 16 hours, including naps |
| Toddlers (1 to 2 years) | 11 to 14 hours, including naps |
| Preschoolers (3 to 5 years) | 10 to 13 hours, including naps |
| School age (6 to 12 years) | 9 to 12 hours |
| Teens (13 to 17 years) | 8 to 10 hours |
| Adults (18 to 60 years) | 7 or more hours |
| Adults (61 to 64 years) | 7 to 9 hours |
| Adults (65 and older) | 7 to 8 hours |

The National Sleep Foundation (NSF) ran its own expert review and landed in almost the same place: 7 to 9 hours for adults up to age 64, 7 to 8 hours from 65, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers. Its ranges for some children are slightly narrower, such as 9 to 11 hours for school-age kids.[^3]

The small differences between panels matter less than the pattern. Sleep need falls steadily through childhood and settles at around 7 to 9 hours for most adults. The NSF adds that a few people do fine slightly outside the ranges, but that straying far from them is rare.[^3]

## What happens when you fall short

Plenty of people are finding out. In 2024, 30.5% of US adults reported sleeping less than 7 hours in a typical 24 hours. The rate peaked at 34.5% among people aged 50 to 64.[^4]

The AASM panel linked regularly sleeping less than 7 hours with weight gain and obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and stroke, depression and a higher risk of death. It also tied short sleep to weaker immune function, more pain, worse performance, more errors and more accidents.[^1]

How big is the effect? A meta-analysis pooling 16 studies and almost 1.4 million people found that short sleepers were 12% more likely to die during follow-up than people who slept a middling amount.[^6] That is a modest increase, and these were observational studies, so they cannot prove that the short sleep itself did the damage. Short nights often travel with long working hours, stress and illness.

The evidence on alertness is more direct, because it comes from experiments.

> [!WARNING]
> Being awake for 17 hours impairs you about as much as a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%. After 24 hours awake it is closer to 0.10%, which is over the 0.08% legal limit for driving in the United States.[^7] If you are fighting to keep your eyes open at the wheel, pull over somewhere safe.

## Quality and regularity count too

Seven hours of broken sleep is not the same as seven solid ones. An NSF panel agreed on four markers of good-quality sleep: falling asleep within about 30 minutes, waking for more than 5 minutes no more than once a night, spending 20 minutes or less awake after first dropping off, and being asleep for at least 85% of your time in bed.[^8]

> [!TIP] Time in bed is not time asleep
> Nobody is asleep for 100% of the time they spend in bed. Even at a healthy 85%, 7 hours of sleep takes a little over 8 hours in bed. If your "7 hours" runs from lights-out to alarm, you are probably getting closer to 6.

Timing matters as well. Researchers tracked about 61,000 UK adults with wrist-worn movement sensors and scored how regular their sleep and wake times were. Compared with the least regular fifth, everyone else had a 20% to 48% lower risk of dying during follow-up, and regularity predicted mortality better than sleep duration did.[^9] This is an association, not proof of cause, but it fits what we know about the [body clock](/articles/circadian-rhythm-and-light): it likes a schedule.

## Can you catch up at the weekend?

Partly, and the evidence pulls in two directions.

In a controlled lab study, healthy young adults limited to 5 hours a night ate more after dinner and gained weight. A second group was allowed to sleep as long as they liked over a weekend before returning to short nights. They fared no better: once the short nights resumed, their sensitivity to insulin, the hormone that controls blood sugar, fell by 9% to 27%, and their body clocks had drifted later.[^10]

On the other hand, a study of nearly 44,000 Swedish adults found that people under 65 who slept 5 hours or less every night had a 65% higher death rate than the comparison group, while people who were short on weekdays but slept long at weekends had no raised risk.[^11] Sleep was self-reported and recorded only once, so treat this as a hint rather than a verdict.

A fair reading: a lie-in after a rough week beats no recovery at all, but it is a patch, not a plan.

## Could you be a natural short sleeper?

Probably not. Familial natural short sleep is real: people who have it sleep only 4 to 6 hours a night all their lives and feel properly rested. But it is rare, it runs in families, and researchers have traced it to uncommon variants in specific genes such as DEC2 and ADRB1.[^12]

A likelier explanation for "I'm fine on five hours" is that short sleepers are poor judges of their own state. People who are sleep deficient often do not realize it and believe they are functioning well.[^5]

At the other end, regularly sleeping more than 9 hours can be appropriate for young adults, people repaying a sleep debt and people who are ill.[^1] In the pooled data, long sleepers had a 30% higher risk of death, but long sleep is thought to be more a marker of underlying illness than a cause of it.[^6]

## Signs you are not getting enough

Daytime sleepiness is the best everyday gauge. You may be short on sleep if you often feel you could doze off while:[^5]

- sitting and reading or watching TV
- sitting still in a meeting, classroom or movie theater
- riding in a car for an hour without stopping
- sitting and talking to someone
- sitting quietly after lunch
- waiting in traffic for a few minutes

Other clues include waking unrefreshed, slower reactions, more mistakes, and trouble with memory, decisions or keeping your emotions in check.[^5]

## How to find your number

Start with your wake-up time, which is usually the fixed point, and count back far enough to allow at least 7 hours of actual sleep. Our [sleep cycle calculator](/tools/sleep-cycles) does the arithmetic. Hold that schedule for two weeks, weekends included, then check yourself against the signs above. Still dozy? Move bedtime 15 to 30 minutes earlier and repeat.

If the hours are there but the sleep is not, the fix is different. Better [sleep habits](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) help with mild problems, and persistent trouble falling or staying asleep has [treatments that work](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works).

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor if you often feel sleepy during the day or wake unrefreshed despite spending enough time in bed, if you have nodded off while driving, or if trouble sleeping has dragged on for weeks.[^5] Loud snoring with pauses in breathing or gasping should be checked for [sleep apnea](/articles/snoring-and-sleep-apnea).

## The bottom line

If you are an adult, plan for at least 7 hours of sleep a night, which usually means about 8 hours in bed, at roughly the same times every day. Children and teenagers need more, so protect their bedtimes too. Judge the result by how alert you feel during the day, and if enough time in bed still leaves you drained, get it checked.
