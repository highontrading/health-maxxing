Two of the world's favorite drugs bookend the day. Caffeine gets us going in the morning and alcohol helps us wind down at night. Both affect sleep more than most people realize, partly because the damage is done while you are unconscious.

This explainer covers how each one works, what sleep-laboratory studies show, and the cut-off times that let you keep your coffee and your glass of wine without paying for them at 3 a.m. Nicotine, the stimulant people forget, gets a section too.

## How caffeine keeps you awake

From the moment you wake up, a chemical called adenosine builds up in your brain. The more of it there is, the sleepier you feel, which researchers call sleep pressure. Caffeine works by blocking the receptors that adenosine acts on.[^5] It does not remove your tiredness. It stops you from feeling it for a while.

Caffeine is absorbed quickly and reaches its peak level in your blood within about an hour.[^12] Getting rid of it takes much longer.

## How long caffeine lasts

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) puts caffeine's half-life, the time your body takes to clear half of a dose, at four to six hours.[^1] Run the numbers on a large coffee with 200 mg of caffeine at 3 p.m. Around 100 mg is still circulating at 8 p.m., and around 50 mg at 1 a.m.

Those are averages. Smoking speeds up caffeine clearance considerably. Pregnancy and oral contraceptives slow it down, and inherited differences in the liver enzyme that breaks caffeine down add more variation.[^3] That is why one person sleeps soundly after an espresso at dinner and another is still wired from a cup of tea at 4 p.m.

## What the sleep studies show

In a well-known experiment, 12 healthy sleepers took 400 mg of caffeine, roughly the amount in four cups of coffee, either at bedtime, three hours before or six hours before. All three timings disrupted sleep. Even the dose taken six hours before bed reduced total sleep by more than an hour.[^4]

> [!STAT] 1 hour+
> The sleep lost when 400 mg of caffeine was taken six hours before bedtime in a sleep-laboratory study.[^4]

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled the available trials. On average, caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 minutes, added 9 minutes to the time needed to fall asleep, and shifted sleep away from the deep stages toward lighter ones.[^5]

The authors also calculated cut-off times. To avoid losing sleep, a regular cup of coffee (about 107 mg of caffeine) should be finished at least 8.8 hours before bedtime, and a typical pre-workout supplement (about 217 mg) at least 13.2 hours before.[^5]

## How much is too much?

For healthy adults, the FDA cites 400 mg a day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects, and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) reaches the same figure. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, EFSA advises no more than 200 mg a day.[^1][^2]

For sleep, the more relevant number is smaller. EFSA notes that a single dose of 100 mg can delay or shorten sleep in some adults, particularly close to bedtime.[^2]

Here is roughly what common drinks contain, according to the FDA:[^1]

| Drink | Typical caffeine |
| --- | --- |
| Brewed coffee, 240 mL (8 fl oz) | 80 to 100 mg |
| Black or green tea, 240 mL (8 fl oz) | 30 to 50 mg |
| Cola, 355 mL (12 fl oz) | 30 to 40 mg |
| Energy drink, 240 mL (8 fl oz) | 40 to 250 mg |
| Decaf coffee, 240 mL (8 fl oz) | 2 to 15 mg |

Coffee has real upsides as well, which we cover in [caffeine: benefits and limits](/articles/caffeine-benefits-and-limits).

## Alcohol: the sedative that spoils the second half

Alcohol is a sedative, so the nightcap's reputation is not entirely unearned. In a review of laboratory studies, alcohol at every dose shortened the time it took to fall asleep and deepened sleep in the first half of the night.[^6]

Then it wears off. As your body clears the alcohol, sleep in the second half of the night becomes lighter and more broken. REM sleep, the stage when most vivid dreaming happens, starts later at all doses and is reduced across the whole night at moderate and high ones.[^6] A 2025 meta-analysis of sleep-laboratory studies went further and concluded that REM sleep is disrupted even at low doses, and more so as the dose rises.[^7]

Alcohol also relaxes the muscles that hold the upper airway open. In a meta-analysis, people who drank more had a 25% higher risk of sleep apnea.[^8] If you snore, a few drinks will usually make it louder. See [snoring and sleep apnea](/articles/snoring-and-sleep-apnea) if that sounds familiar.

> [!WARNING]
> Using alcohol as a nightly sleep aid tends to backfire. You fall asleep sooner but sleep worse, and you can drift into needing a drink to sleep at all. For the wider health picture, see [alcohol and your health](/articles/alcohol-and-your-health).

## Nicotine: the overlooked stimulant

Nicotine is a stimulant too. Smokers tend to take longer to fall asleep and to sleep more lightly, and overnight withdrawal adds its own disruption.[^10]

In a study that tracked 785 adults night by night, using nicotine or alcohol within four hours of bedtime was linked with more broken sleep. Among people with insomnia, evening nicotine was associated with about 40 minutes less sleep.[^9]

Sleep can be unsettled for the first few weeks after quitting, because withdrawal itself disturbs sleep.[^10] That is a short-term cost of a change that pays off many times over. Our guide to [quitting smoking and vaping](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) can help.

## Practical cut-offs

- **Coffee and tea.** Have your last cup about nine hours before bed, which is early afternoon for most people. Six hours is the bare minimum.[^5][^4]
- **Big doses.** Keep energy drinks and pre-workout supplements to the morning.[^5]
- **After the cut-off.** Switch to decaf or a caffeine-free drink.[^1]
- **Alcohol.** Less is better for sleep, and earlier is better than later. Finishing your last drink at least four hours before bed is a reasonable rule, though the evidence for an exact cut-off is thin.[^9]
- **Nicotine.** Avoid it in the evening, or better, quit.[^11]

> [!TIP] Run a two-week test
> If you suspect caffeine but are not sure, move your last cup to noon for two weeks and change nothing else. Compare how long you take to fall asleep and how you feel in the morning. Your own data beats any average.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor if you need alcohol to fall asleep on most nights, if you find you cannot cut down, or if you still sleep badly after two or three weeks of sensible cut-offs. A racing heart, jitters or anxiety after modest amounts of caffeine are also worth mentioning.[^1]

## The bottom line

Enjoy your coffee, but finish it by early afternoon, and treat a big energy drink as a morning-only product. Alcohol is a poor sleep aid: keep it modest and early in the evening.
