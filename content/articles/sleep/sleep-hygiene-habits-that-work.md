"Sleep hygiene" is an unglamorous name for the daily habits and bedroom conditions that make sleep easier. Most of us can recite the rules by now: no late coffee, no phone in bed, keep the room cool.

What is less well known is how uneven the evidence behind them is. Some habits are supported by good trials, some by plausible biology, and a few mostly by tradition.[^3] This list covers the twelve worth your effort, with an honest note on each. It ends with the limits, because good habits improve ordinary bad sleep but do not cure chronic insomnia on their own.[^4]

## Anchor your body clock

### 1. Get up at the same time every day

If you change only one thing, change this. A fixed wake-up time, weekends and vacations included, sits at the top of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) list of healthy sleep habits.[^1] If a lie-in is non-negotiable, the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) suggests keeping the difference to about an hour.[^2] Then count back from your wake-up time to a bedtime that allows [enough sleep for your age](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need).

The supporting evidence is observational but striking. Among about 61,000 UK adults who wore movement trackers, everyone outside the least regular fifth of sleepers had a 20% to 48% lower risk of death during follow-up.[^5]

### 2. Get bright light early in the day

Light is the main signal that sets your [body clock](/articles/circadian-rhythm-and-light). An international panel of light and sleep scientists recommends bright light throughout the day, ideally daylight, to support alertness by day and sleep at night.[^6] A morning walk, breakfast by a window or a commute on foot all count.

### 3. Dim the evening

The same panel advises keeping light low for the three hours before bed and the bedroom as dark as possible overnight.[^6]

Screens add stimulation to the light. A National Sleep Foundation expert panel agreed in 2024 that screen use, and especially engaging content before bed, harms sleep in children and teenagers. It did not reach the same agreement for adults.[^7] The AASM's middle path is reasonable: devices off at least 30 minutes before bedtime.[^1]

## Watch what you drink and eat

### 4. Set a caffeine cut-off

Caffeine lingers longer than most people assume. A 2023 meta-analysis found that it cut total sleep by about 45 minutes and reduced deep sleep. The authors estimated that a standard cup of coffee (about 107 mg of caffeine) needs to be finished at least 8.8 hours before bedtime to avoid shortening sleep.[^8] For an 11 p.m. bedtime, that is roughly 2 p.m.

> [!STAT] 8.8 hours
> The gap needed between an ordinary cup of coffee and bedtime to avoid losing sleep, according to a 2023 meta-analysis.[^8]

### 5. Do not use alcohol as a sleep aid

A nightcap does help you fall asleep faster. The bill arrives later: a review of laboratory studies found that alcohol at any dose disrupts the second half of the night, and that moderate and higher doses reduce REM sleep.[^9] Our guide to [caffeine, alcohol and sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep) has the details.

### 6. Keep late meals light

This one rests more on common sense than on trials. Both the AASM and the NHLBI advise against a large meal in the last couple of hours before bed, and both say a light snack is fine if you are hungry.[^1][^2]

## Set up your day and your evening

### 7. Exercise, at almost any time

People who are regularly active tend to sleep better, and the old rule against evening workouts is mostly wrong.[^3] A meta-analysis of 23 studies found that evening exercise did not harm sleep and slightly increased deep sleep. The exception was vigorous exercise that ended less than an hour before bedtime, which could make it harder to fall asleep.[^10]

### 8. Build a wind-down routine

Give yourself a buffer of low-stimulation time before bed. The NHLBI suggests using the last hour for quiet time, away from intense exercise and bright screens, and mentions a hot bath or relaxation techniques as options.[^2] What you do matters less than doing it consistently, so that the routine itself becomes a cue for sleep.

### 9. Make the bedroom cool, dark and quiet

The AASM recommends a quiet, relaxing bedroom kept at a comfortable, cool temperature.[^1] No single number has been proven best, but the usual advice is about 18 °C (65 °F), give or take a couple of degrees.[^11] Blackout curtains or an eye mask handle the light. Earplugs or steady background sound can help with noise.

## Teach your brain that bed means sleep

The last three habits come from stimulus control, a core part of the therapy that sleep specialists use for insomnia.[^4] The idea is simple: the more time you spend awake in bed, the more your brain links bed with being awake.

### 10. Keep the bed for sleep and sex

Working, scrolling and watching TV in bed all weaken the link between bed and sleep. The AASM advice is to use your bed only for sleep and sex, and not to go to bed until you feel sleepy.[^1]

### 11. If you cannot sleep, get up

Lying there trying harder does not work. If you are not asleep after about 20 minutes, get out of bed and do something quiet in low light, without electronics, and go back when you feel sleepy.[^1] The same applies if you wake at 3 a.m.

### 12. Park your worries on paper

In a sleep laboratory experiment with 57 young adults, people who spent five minutes at bedtime writing a to-do list for the coming days fell asleep about nine minutes faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already finished.[^12] It is one small study, but the method is free and takes five minutes.

> [!TIP] Start with two
> Trying all twelve at once is a recipe for giving up. Begin with a fixed wake-up time and a caffeine cut-off, hold them for two weeks, then add the next habit.

## When habits are not enough

Sleep hygiene works best as prevention and as a fix for sleep that is merely mediocre. It is much less impressive as a treatment for chronic insomnia, meaning trouble sleeping on at least three nights a week for three months or more.

The AASM guideline is blunt about this: it advises clinicians not to use sleep hygiene as the only treatment for chronic insomnia. The treatment it recommends instead is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).[^4] We explain how that works in [what actually helps insomnia](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if poor sleep has lasted more than a few weeks despite good habits, if it is affecting your mood, work or driving, or if you rely on alcohol or sleeping pills to get to sleep. Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, or an irresistible urge to move your legs at night, point to sleep disorders that habits will not fix.

## The bottom line

Fix your wake-up time, get daylight early, set a caffeine cut-off in the early afternoon and stop treating alcohol as a sleep aid. Keep the bed for sleep, and get out of it when sleep will not come. If you have done all that for a few weeks and still sleep badly, the next step is proper treatment, not a thirteenth tip.
