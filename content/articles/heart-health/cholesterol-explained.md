A cholesterol test hands you four numbers and, usually, very little help deciding which one deserves your attention. Plenty of people fret about their total, or feel reassured by a high "good" cholesterol. Both instincts can mislead.

This explainer covers what each number on a lipid panel means, which ones actually predict heart attacks and strokes, what moves them, and what large trials say about statins and muscle aches.

## What cholesterol is, and how it travels

Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance found in every cell. You need it to build cell membranes and make hormones, and your liver produces all that your body requires.[^1]

Because fat and blood do not mix, cholesterol travels inside protein-wrapped parcels called lipoproteins:

- **LDL (low-density lipoprotein)** delivers cholesterol to your tissues. When more is circulating than cells can use, LDL particles get trapped in artery walls and build into plaques. That process, atherosclerosis, narrows arteries and sets up heart attacks and strokes.[^3]
- **HDL (high-density lipoprotein)** carries cholesterol back to the liver for removal.[^1]
- **Triglycerides** are not cholesterol at all. They are the fat your body uses to store spare energy, and they are measured on the same test.[^1]

## Reading a lipid panel

US labs report in mg/dL, and most of the world uses mmol/L. These are the general reference points for healthy adults:[^1][^2]

| Measure | Desirable level | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total cholesterol | Below 200 mg/dL (5.2 mmol/L) | The least informative number on its own |
| LDL cholesterol | Below 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) | Lower goals apply if your risk is high |
| HDL cholesterol | 60 mg/dL (1.6 mmol/L) or more | Below 40 (1.0) in men or 50 (1.3) in women counts as low |
| Triglycerides | Below 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) | Pushed up by alcohol, sugar and excess weight |
| Non-HDL cholesterol | Below 130 mg/dL (3.4 mmol/L) | Total minus HDL, so no extra test is needed |

Treat these as landmarks, not personal targets: the right LDL level for you depends on your overall cardiovascular risk.

> [!NOTE] You probably do not need to fast
> A joint European consensus statement found that eating before the test changes results only slightly: triglycerides rise by about 26 mg/dL (0.3 mmol/L) on average, and LDL falls by about 8 mg/dL (0.2 mmol/L). It recommends non-fasting tests as routine, with a fasting repeat if triglycerides come back above 440 mg/dL (5 mmol/L).[^9] Follow your own lab's instructions.

## Why LDL matters most

LDL has the strongest claim to being a cause of disease and not just a bystander. A European Atherosclerosis Society panel reviewed more than 200 studies involving over 2 million people, spanning genetics, long-term cohorts and randomized trials. Its conclusion: LDL causes atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and the damage depends on both how high LDL is and how many years your arteries are exposed to it.[^3]

Treatment trials agree. Pooling 26 randomized statin trials with about 170,000 participants, the Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' Collaboration found that each 1 mmol/L (about 39 mg/dL) reduction in LDL cut major vascular events, meaning heart attacks, strokes and procedures to reopen arteries, by about 22%.[^4]

> [!STAT] 22%
> Fewer major vascular events for every 1 mmol/L (about 39 mg/dL) drop in LDL cholesterol across 26 randomized trials.[^4]

Two relatives of LDL are worth knowing. **Non-HDL cholesterol** captures every type of particle that can lodge in an artery wall, not only LDL. **Apolipoprotein B (ApoB)** goes a step further and counts those particles directly, because each one carries a single ApoB molecule.[^3] The 2026 US guideline uses ApoB selectively, for example in people with high triglycerides or diabetes, where LDL alone can understate risk.[^5]

How low should LDL go? European guidelines aim for below 116 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) in people at low risk, tightening step by step to below 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L) for those at very high risk, such as people who have already had a heart attack.[^6] The 2026 US guideline has returned to explicit goals as well, with under 55 mg/dL for the highest-risk group.[^5]

## HDL: a marker, not a target

HDL earned the "good cholesterol" label because people with higher levels tend to have fewer heart attacks. Raising it, though, has not been shown to help. In a large genetic study, people who inherited variants that raise HDL had no lower risk of heart attack than those who did not, while variants that raise LDL clearly increased risk.[^7] European guidelines set no HDL target for the same reason.[^6]

So read a low HDL as a signal that often travels with high triglycerides, inactivity and smoking. And do not let a high HDL talk you out of taking a high LDL seriously.

## Lp(a): the test to have once

Lipoprotein(a), written Lp(a), is an LDL-like particle with an extra protein attached. Your level is more than 90% determined by your genes, stays fairly constant through adult life and barely responds to diet or exercise. Roughly one in five people has a high level, which raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and narrowing of the aortic valve even when LDL looks fine.[^8]

Because it changes so little, one measurement is usually enough. European experts recommend testing Lp(a) at least once in every adult, and the 2026 US guideline now says the same.[^8][^5] A result above about 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L) is considered high.[^8] Statins do not lower Lp(a), so a high result is mainly a reason to be stricter about LDL, blood pressure and the other risks you can change.[^5][^8]

## What moves your numbers

Habits shift the three main numbers in different ways:[^6][^2]

- **For LDL:** replace saturated and trans fats (fatty meat, butter, pastries) with unsaturated ones (olive oil, nuts, oily fish), and eat more fiber, especially the soluble kind in oats and beans.
- **For triglycerides:** cut back on alcohol, sugary drinks and refined carbohydrates, lose excess weight and move more.
- **For HDL:** regular physical activity and not smoking both nudge it up.

Our guides to [dietary fats](/articles/dietary-fats-decoded) and [fiber](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient) cover the food side in detail.

Genes set the starting point, though. An LDL of 190 mg/dL (4.9 mmol/L) or more in an adult raises the possibility of familial hypercholesterolemia, an inherited condition that affects roughly 1 in 250 people and rarely responds to lifestyle changes alone.[^6]

## Statins: what the trials show

When lifestyle is not enough, or risk is high from the start, statins are the first medicine doctors use, with others such as ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors added if needed.[^5][^6]

How much you gain depends on your starting risk. A Lancet review estimated that lowering LDL by 2 mmol/L with a statin for five years would prevent major vascular events in about 1,000 of every 10,000 people who already have cardiovascular disease, and in about 500 of every 10,000 at raised risk who do not. Against that, it estimated about 5 cases of myopathy (muscle damage confirmed on blood tests), 50 to 100 new diagnoses of diabetes and 5 to 10 bleeding strokes.[^12]

Muscle aches are the side effect people fear most, and here the blinded trials are reassuring. Across 19 placebo-controlled trials with nearly 124,000 participants, 27.1% of people taking a statin reported muscle pain or weakness, compared with 26.6% of those taking a placebo. Statins caused about 11 extra reports per 1,000 people in the first year and no measurable excess after that. More than 90% of muscle symptoms reported on a statin were not caused by it.[^10]

A small British trial showed what is going on. Sixty people who had quit statins because of side effects took a year of pill bottles in random order: some months a statin, some a placebo, some nothing. Of the symptom burden they felt on the statin, 90% also appeared on the placebo, and half of them restarted a statin afterwards.[^11] This is the nocebo effect: real symptoms brought on by expecting harm.

None of this means genuine statin intolerance does not exist. It means that if you ache on a statin, the next step is a conversation about the dose, a different statin or another medicine, not quietly stopping.

> [!DOCTOR]
> High cholesterol has no symptoms, so the only way to know is a blood test. Ask for one if you have never been tested, and sooner if a parent, brother or sister had heart disease early or has very high cholesterol.[^2] If you take a statin, tell your doctor about muscle pain or weakness you cannot explain, and get urgent advice if it is severe or your urine turns dark.[^12] Chest pain or signs of a stroke need your local emergency number: see [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs).

## The bottom line

Look past total cholesterol. LDL, or better still non-HDL or ApoB, is what drives risk. HDL is a signal, not a shield, and Lp(a) deserves one test in your lifetime. Food, movement and not smoking shift the numbers, and when they are not enough, statins have strong evidence behind them and fewer true side effects than their reputation suggests. Ask for your numbers, then ask what they mean for your [overall risk](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors).
