Heart disease has a reputation for striking out of nowhere. It almost never does. The artery damage behind most heart attacks and strokes builds quietly over decades, driven by a short and well-studied list of causes.

That is good news, because most of the list can be measured at a routine checkup and changed. This explainer covers what drives cardiovascular risk, which parts are fixed, how doctors turn it all into a single number, and what to do with yours.

## The scale of the problem

Cardiovascular diseases, mainly heart attacks and strokes, are the leading cause of death worldwide. The WHO attributes roughly 18 to 20 million deaths a year to them, about a third of all deaths, and says most cardiovascular disease can be prevented by tackling risk factors such as tobacco use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and harmful use of alcohol.[^1]

> [!STAT] 1 in 3
> Roughly the share of all deaths worldwide that are caused by cardiovascular disease.[^1]

## Nine factors, most of the risk

The clearest picture of what drives heart attacks comes from INTERHEART, a study that compared about 15,000 people who had just had a first heart attack with about 15,000 similar people who had not, in 52 countries. Nine factors accounted for about 90% of the risk in men and 94% in women:[^2]

- abnormal blood lipids (cholesterol-carrying particles)
- smoking
- high blood pressure
- diabetes
- abdominal obesity
- psychosocial stress
- eating few fruits and vegetables
- physical inactivity
- alcohol intake, where the relationship was less straightforward

The pattern held in men and women, young and old, in every region studied. Abnormal lipids and smoking were the two strongest, together accounting for about two-thirds of the risk.[^2]

> [!NOTE] What "90%" does and does not mean
> INTERHEART was a case-control study, so it shows association, not proof. A later analysis of 112 forward-looking cohort studies with 1.5 million people was more conservative: five factors (body-mass index, systolic blood pressure, non-HDL cholesterol, smoking and diabetes) accounted for 57% of cardiovascular disease in women and 53% in men.[^3] Either way, at least half of the risk sits in things you can change.

Risks also multiply. In INTERHEART, people who smoked and also had high blood pressure and diabetes had about 13 times the odds of a heart attack, far more than the three risks added together.[^2] That is why doctors look at the whole picture, not one number.

## The eight things to work on

The American Heart Association condenses prevention into Life's Essential 8: four behaviors and four measurements.[^4]

| Factor | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Diet | A DASH-style or Mediterranean-style pattern built on plants, whole grains and fish |
| Physical activity | At least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week |
| Nicotine | None, including vaping |
| Sleep | 7 to 9 hours a night |
| Weight | Body-mass index below 25 |
| Blood lipids | Non-HDL cholesterol below 130 mg/dL (3.4 mmol/L) |
| Blood sugar | Fasting glucose below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), or HbA1c below 5.7% |
| Blood pressure | Below 120/80 mm Hg |

Three of these need a test to see. Our guides to [blood pressure](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained), [cholesterol](/articles/cholesterol-explained) and [blood sugar](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explain each one.

## What you cannot change

Some risk is built in.[^5]

- **Age.** Risk climbs as you get older, from around 45 in men and 55 in women.
- **Sex.** Men tend to develop heart disease earlier. Women's risk rises after menopause.
- **Family history.** Your risk is higher if your father or a brother was diagnosed with heart disease before 55, or your mother or a sister before 65.

US guidelines add other "risk enhancers" that standard calculations can miss, including chronic kidney disease, inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and lupus, South Asian ancestry and a high level of lipoprotein(a), an inherited cholesterol particle.[^6]

You cannot edit this list, but it is useful. A strong family history is a reason to start checking early and to treat the modifiable risks less casually.

## Risks specific to women

Pregnancy acts as an early stress test for the heart and blood vessels. Women who had preeclampsia or high blood pressure in pregnancy, gestational diabetes or a preterm delivery have a higher risk of cardiovascular disease later in life, and the American Heart Association recommends that these histories be part of every woman's risk assessment.[^7] Menopause before 40 is also counted as a risk enhancer.[^6]

If any of these apply to you, mention them. Clinicians do not always ask about pregnancies that happened decades ago.

## Putting a number on it

Doctors combine your age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking and diabetes status into an estimate of your chance of a cardiovascular event over the next 10 years. Different regions use different tools:

| Tool | Used in | Ages | What it estimates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PREVENT | United States | 30 to 79 | 10-year and 30-year risk of heart attack, stroke or heart failure[^8] |
| SCORE2 | Europe | 40 to 69, with a version for older adults | 10-year risk of fatal or non-fatal heart attack or stroke[^9] |
| QRISK3 | United Kingdom | 25 to 84 | 10-year risk of heart attack or stroke[^10] |

The cut-offs for action differ too. In England, NICE recommends offering a statin when QRISK3 puts 10-year risk at 10% or more.[^10] The 2025 US blood pressure guideline uses a PREVENT risk of 7.5% or more to decide who with stage 1 hypertension should start medication.[^11]

A calculator gives the average outcome for people like you, not a prophecy, and none of them apply if you have already had a heart attack or stroke. When the decision is borderline, US guidelines suggest weighing the risk enhancers above and, sometimes, a coronary artery calcium scan.[^6]

## What to do with your number

Whatever the result, the foundation is the same: do not smoke, move regularly, eat mostly plants and whole foods, sleep enough and keep your weight in check.[^6] From there:

- **Low risk:** keep the habits and recheck your numbers every few years.
- **Intermediate risk:** this is where a conversation matters most. Treating blood pressure or cholesterol now may be worthwhile, particularly with a family history or other enhancers.
- **High risk, or existing heart disease:** medication alongside lifestyle change is usually recommended, and the benefit is largest here.

One thing not to do is start daily aspirin on your own. The US Preventive Services Task Force advises against starting it for prevention at 60 or older, and calls it an individual decision for people aged 40 to 59 at higher risk, because of bleeding.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ask for a cardiovascular risk assessment if you are over 40 and have never had one, or earlier if heart disease runs in your family.[^6] Do not wait for symptoms: high blood pressure, high cholesterol and early diabetes usually cause none. Chest pain or pressure, sudden breathlessness, or sudden weakness, face drooping or trouble speaking need your local emergency number. See [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs).

## The bottom line

Most heart disease traces back to a handful of measurable causes: lipids, blood pressure, smoking, blood sugar, weight, inactivity and diet. Get the three numbers you cannot feel (blood pressure, cholesterol and glucose) checked, ask what your 10-year risk is, and tell your clinician about family history and pregnancy complications. Then start with the factor that is furthest from where it should be.
