Most diets are sold on promises. DASH is unusual because it was built to be tested: researchers designed an eating pattern, fed it to hundreds of people under controlled conditions, and measured what happened to their blood pressure.

It worked, and the follow-up trial showed that eating less salt on top worked better still. This guide covers what DASH is, what the trials found, how much salt is too much, and the practical business of finding the sodium in your food.

## What DASH actually is

DASH stands for Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension. It is not a brand or a meal-replacement plan. It is a pattern: plenty of vegetables, fruit and low-fat dairy, with whole grains, fish, poultry, beans and nuts, and less red meat, sugar and saturated fat.[^1][^6]

The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) turns that into daily servings. For someone eating about 2,000 kcal a day:[^6]

| Food group | How much | One serving is about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grains, mostly whole | 6 to 8 a day | 1 slice of bread, or half a cup of cooked rice or pasta |
| Vegetables | 4 to 5 a day | 1 cup of raw leafy greens, or half a cup cooked |
| Fruit | 4 to 5 a day | 1 medium fruit |
| Low-fat or fat-free dairy | 2 to 3 a day | 1 cup of milk or yogurt |
| Lean meat, poultry, fish | 170 g (6 oz) or less a day | A piece the size of a deck of cards is roughly 85 g (3 oz) |
| Nuts, seeds, beans, lentils | 4 to 5 a week | A small handful of nuts, or half a cup of cooked beans |
| Fats and oils | 2 to 3 a day | 1 teaspoon of oil |
| Sweets | 5 or fewer a week | 1 tablespoon of sugar or jam |

If that looks like a lot of plants, it is: eight to ten servings of fruit and vegetables a day. That is also why the pattern is rich in potassium and fiber.

## What the trials found

The original DASH trial gave 459 adults one of three diets for eight weeks. Salt intake and body weight were held steady, so any change could be pinned on the eating pattern itself. Compared with a typical American diet, DASH lowered systolic pressure by 5.5 mm Hg and diastolic pressure by 3.0 mm Hg. Among the 133 participants who had hypertension, the drop was 11.4 and 5.5 mm Hg, and it appeared within two weeks.[^1]

> [!STAT] 11.4 mm Hg
> The average fall in systolic blood pressure on the DASH diet among trial participants who had hypertension, with no change in salt or weight.[^1]

The follow-up trial, DASH-Sodium, tested the diet and salt together. Its 412 participants ate either DASH or a typical diet at three sodium levels for 30 days each. Less sodium meant lower pressure on both diets, in people with and without hypertension. Going from the highest to the lowest sodium level cut systolic pressure by 6.7 mm Hg on the typical diet and by 3.0 mm Hg on DASH.[^2]

The best result came from combining the two. DASH at the lowest sodium level beat the typical diet at the highest level by 7.1 mm Hg in people without hypertension and by 11.5 mm Hg in people with it.[^2]

Two honest caveats. These were feeding studies, in which participants were handed every meal, so expect smaller effects when you are the one shopping and cooking. And they measured blood pressure over weeks, not heart attacks over years.

## How much salt is too much?

The WHO recommends that adults eat less than 2,000 mg of sodium a day, which is less than 5 g of salt, or just under a teaspoon. The world average is more than double that: about 4,310 mg of sodium, or 10.8 g of salt, a day.[^3]

The American Heart Association sets its limit at 2,300 mg of sodium a day, with an ideal of no more than 1,500 mg for most adults, and says Americans average more than 3,300 mg.[^4] The 2025 US blood pressure guideline uses the same two figures.[^5]

> [!NOTE] Salt or sodium?
> Salt is sodium chloride, and it is the sodium that raises blood pressure. Labels may list either one. To convert sodium to salt, multiply by 2.5, so 2,000 mg of sodium equals 5 g of salt. A teaspoon of table salt holds about 2,300 mg of sodium.[^4]

## Where the salt hides

In the US, more than 70% of the sodium people eat comes from packaged, prepared and restaurant food, not from the shaker.[^4] Bread, processed meats, pizza, soups, sandwiches, sauces and savory snacks are typical sources, and many of them do not taste especially salty.[^8] In other parts of the world, more of it is added at home, in cooking or through condiments such as soy sauce, fish sauce and stock cubes.[^3]

That makes the label your main tool. On US Nutrition Facts panels:[^8]

- 5% of the Daily Value or less per serving is low in sodium, and 20% or more is high.
- "Low sodium" means 140 mg or less per serving.
- "Reduced sodium" only means at least 25% less than the regular version, which can still be a lot.

A few habits do most of the work: compare brands, rinse canned beans and vegetables, cook from scratch more often, and season with herbs, spices, garlic, citrus or vinegar.[^8] Our guide to [reading a nutrition label](/articles/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label) goes further.

## Potassium and salt substitutes

Potassium is sodium's counterweight, and DASH supplies a lot of it. The 2017 US blood pressure guideline suggested 3,500 to 5,000 mg a day, preferably from food.[^10] Beans, lentils, potatoes, leafy greens, bananas, dried apricots and yogurt are all good sources.[^9]

Salt substitutes take the idea further by swapping some of the sodium chloride for potassium chloride. A trial in rural China followed about 21,000 people in 600 villages, all of whom had already had a stroke or were 60 or older with high blood pressure. Half the villages used a substitute that was 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride. Over nearly five years, that group had 14% fewer strokes, 13% fewer major cardiovascular events and 12% fewer deaths.[^7]

The 2025 US guideline now recommends potassium-based salt substitutes for adults with or without high blood pressure, especially when most of your salt is added at home.[^5]

> [!WARNING]
> Extra potassium can be dangerous if your kidneys cannot clear it. If you have [chronic kidney disease](/conditions/chronic-kidney-disease), or take medicines that raise potassium (some blood pressure and heart medicines do), ask your doctor before using a salt substitute, taking potassium supplements or starting DASH. The salt-substitute trial excluded people in these groups.[^5][^7]

## A sample day

One way the servings can fit together:

- **Breakfast:** oatmeal made with low-fat milk, topped with a sliced banana and a small handful of unsalted walnuts.
- **Lunch:** a whole-grain wrap filled with grilled chicken or chickpeas and plenty of salad, dressed with olive oil and lemon, plus an orange.
- **Snack:** plain low-fat yogurt with berries.
- **Dinner:** baked salmon or a lentil stew, brown rice, and two vegetables such as broccoli and roasted carrots, seasoned with garlic and herbs.
- **Later:** a piece of fruit.

You do not have to switch overnight. Adding a serving of vegetables at lunch and dinner and swapping one salty staple a week is a realistic start. DASH overlaps heavily with the [Mediterranean diet](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide), so borrow freely.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Food changes work alongside prescribed blood pressure medicine, not instead of it, so do not stop tablets without talking to your doctor. Book an appointment if your home readings stay at 130/80 mm Hg or higher. A reading above 180/120 with chest pain, breathlessness, weakness, or vision or speech changes needs your local emergency number. See [blood pressure numbers, explained](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained).[^5]

## The bottom line

Eat more vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains and low-fat dairy, and less processed and restaurant food. That one shift delivers most of DASH and removes most of the hidden salt at the same time. Check labels for sodium, aim for under 5 g of salt a day, and consider a potassium-enriched salt if your kidneys are healthy and your medicines allow it. In trials, the two together lowered systolic pressure by about 11 mm Hg in people with hypertension.
