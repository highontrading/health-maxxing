Your watch now greets you each morning with a resting heart rate, a heart rate variability score and possibly a verdict on how "ready" you are. Two of those are real physiological measurements. The third is an algorithm's opinion of them.

This explainer covers what resting heart rate and HRV measure, what counts as normal, what pushes them up and down, how far to trust a wrist sensor, and which readings deserve a doctor's attention.

## Resting heart rate: what counts as normal

Resting heart rate is the number of times your heart beats per minute while you are awake, calm and still. For adults, the normal range is 60 to 100 beats per minute (bpm).[^1][^2] Fit people often sit lower, because a stronger heart moves more blood with each beat. An athlete's resting rate can be as low as 40 bpm.[^2]

"Normal" covers a lot of ground. In an analysis of wearable data from 92,457 US adults, the average resting rate was 65 bpm, but individual averages ranged from 40 to 109 bpm. Each person's own rate was far steadier than the spread between people.[^4]

Within the normal range, lower tends to be better. A meta-analysis of 46 studies found that each extra 10 bpm of resting heart rate was associated with a 9% higher risk of death from any cause and an 8% higher risk of cardiovascular death.[^3] These were observational studies, so a higher resting rate may be a marker of low fitness, illness or stress more than a cause of harm in itself.

> [!STAT] 60 to 100 bpm
> The normal resting heart rate range for adults. Well-trained people are often below it.[^1][^2]

## How to measure it yourself

You do not need a device. The American Heart Association suggests checking first thing in the morning, before you get out of bed or have coffee. Place the tips of your first two fingers on the inside of your wrist, on the thumb side, count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number.[^2]

Wearables do this automatically, usually from the quietest stretches of your day or night, so their figure can differ a little from a manual count. Pick one method and stick with it.

## What a trend is telling you

A single reading means little. What matters is your baseline and how you depart from it.

A resting rate that drifts down over months of regular exercise is a sign that your heart is getting more efficient.[^2] A rate that sits several beats above your usual for days is a prompt to ask what changed. Stress, medicines, body position, air temperature and how fit you are all shift it.[^1] So does an infection: in a study of about 47,000 Fitbit users, the share of people whose resting rate ran above their own norm in a given week tracked official flu activity closely enough to improve real-time flu surveillance.[^9]

## HRV: the gaps between beats

Your heart does not tick like a metronome. At 60 bpm the gap between beats is not exactly one second each time. It might be 0.95 seconds, then 1.08, then 1.01. Heart rate variability (HRV) measures that beat-to-beat variation, in milliseconds.[^5]

The variation comes from your autonomic nervous system, which runs your body's background functions. Its "rest and digest" branch slows the heart and adds variability. Its "fight or flight" branch speeds the heart up and flattens the variability. At rest, a higher HRV generally reflects a body that is recovered and adaptable, and a lower HRV reflects strain. A common measure is RMSSD, which captures the fast, beat-to-beat changes driven by the rest-and-digest branch.[^5]

Low HRV has been linked to worse outcomes. In a meta-analysis of eight studies covering about 22,000 people without known heart disease, those with low HRV had a 32% to 45% higher risk of a first cardiovascular event.[^7]

The catch is that the range between healthy people is enormous. An analysis of 8 million Fitbit users showed HRV falling steadily with age and shifting with sex, time of day and activity level.[^6] That is why there is no single "good" HRV score, and why comparing yours with a friend's tells you nothing.

> [!TIP] Compare you with you
> Use your average over a week against your own baseline from the past month or two. Ignore single nights, and ignore other people's numbers.

## What moves both numbers

The two measures usually move in opposite directions:[^5][^6][^8][^9]

| Factor | Resting heart rate | HRV |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Better aerobic fitness | Lower | Often higher |
| Short or poor sleep | Higher | Lower |
| Alcohol | Higher | Lower |
| Illness or fever | Higher | Lower |
| Mental stress | Higher | Lower |
| Hard training without enough recovery | Higher | Lower |

Alcohol is the clearest example. In a study of 4,098 Finnish employees wearing HRV monitors, drinking reduced an HRV-based measure of recovery during the first three hours of sleep, and the effect grew with the dose: about 9 percentage points after light drinking, 24 after moderate drinking and 39 after heavy drinking.[^8] If your numbers look rough the morning after a few drinks, the sensor is not broken. See [caffeine, alcohol and sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep).

## How far to trust your wearable

Wrist devices measure your pulse optically, by shining light into the skin and tracking changes in blood flow. At rest this works well for heart rate. It gets shakier when you move: in a study of six devices worn by 53 people, errors were about 30% higher during activity than at rest. The same study found no significant difference in accuracy across skin tones.[^10]

HRV is a harder measurement, because it depends on timing each beat to within milliseconds. Readings taken during sleep or stillness are the most trustworthy. Different metrics and recording lengths are also not interchangeable, so a number from one brand cannot be compared with a number from another.[^5]

"Readiness", "recovery" and "body battery" scores are proprietary blends of these inputs. Treat them as a prompt to check in with how you actually feel, not as a diagnosis. For training decisions, see [rest, recovery and overtraining](/articles/rest-recovery-and-overtraining).

## When a number needs a doctor

Most wobbles are lifestyle noise. A few patterns are worth acting on.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Tell your doctor if your resting heart rate is regularly above 100 bpm, if it is very low and you are not athletic, or if you have unexplained episodes of a fast heartbeat, especially with weakness, dizziness or fainting.[^1] Call your local emergency number if a racing or irregular heartbeat comes with chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness.

Many watches also flag a possible irregular rhythm, meaning [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), which raises the risk of stroke. In the Apple Heart Study of about 419,000 people, 0.52% received an irregular pulse notification. Of those who then wore an ECG patch, 34% turned out to have atrial fibrillation, and when an alert fired while the patch was on, it matched atrial fibrillation 84% of the time.[^11]

So an alert is not a diagnosis, but it is a good reason to book a proper ECG. It is not an emergency if you feel well. The reverse does not hold: no alert does not mean no problem, and the US Preventive Services Task Force says there is not yet enough evidence to recommend for or against screening people who have no symptoms.[^12]

## The bottom line

Resting heart rate and HRV are most useful as personal trend lines, not as scores to win. Learn your baseline, measure under the same conditions, and pay attention to shifts that last days, not hours. Use them as a nudge toward better sleep, less alcohol and sensible training. See a doctor about a persistently fast or very slow pulse, symptoms such as dizziness or fainting, or an irregular rhythm alert.
