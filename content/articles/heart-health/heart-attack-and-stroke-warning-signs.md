Heart attacks and strokes share one cruel feature: the treatments that work best only work early. Every minute spent wondering whether it is "bad enough" to call is heart muscle or brain tissue you do not get back.

This guide covers what the warning signs look like, including the less obvious ones, what to do in the first minutes, and how a cardiac arrest differs from both.

> [!DOCTOR] Is this happening right now?
> Stop reading and call your local emergency number. Do not wait to see whether the symptoms pass, and do not drive yourself to hospital.

## Heart attack: what it feels like

A heart attack happens when a blocked artery cuts off the blood supply to part of the heart muscle.[^11] The American Heart Association lists these warning signs:[^1]

- **Chest discomfort.** Pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain in the center of the chest that lasts more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back.
- **Discomfort elsewhere in the upper body.** One or both arms, the back, neck, jaw or stomach.
- **Shortness of breath,** with or without chest discomfort.
- **Other signs.** Breaking out in a cold sweat, nausea or lightheadedness.

It rarely looks like the movies. Some heart attacks are sudden and intense, but most start slowly, with mild pain or discomfort.[^1]

### The quieter versions

Chest pain is the most common symptom in women as well as men. Women, though, are more likely to have other symptoms too, particularly shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting, and back or jaw pain.[^1][^3]

Some people have very little chest pain. The NHS notes that the pain can be mild and mistaken for indigestion, and that there may be none at all, especially in women, older people and people with diabetes.[^2] US guidelines tell clinicians to think of a heart attack in anyone aged 75 or over with breathlessness, fainting, sudden confusion or an unexplained fall.[^3]

You do not need to be sure before you call. Uncertainty is normal, and sorting it out is the emergency team's job.

## What to do for a suspected heart attack

1. **Call your local emergency number straight away.** Ambulance crews can start treatment as soon as they arrive, and they are trained to revive someone whose heart stops.[^1]
2. **Sit down and rest** while you wait, to avoid putting extra strain on the heart.[^2]
3. **Take aspirin only if it is safe for you.** The call handler may tell you to chew an adult aspirin, and the NHS advises doing so if one is to hand and you are not allergic.[^2] Skip it if you are allergic or have been told not to take it, and never delay the call to go looking for one.[^4]
4. **Be ready to start CPR** if the person collapses and stops breathing normally. The steps are below.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not drive yourself unless there is truly no other option, and do not take a tablet and then wait to see if it helps. Calling comes first.[^1][^4]

## Stroke: think FAST

A stroke happens when the blood supply to part of the brain is cut off, usually by a clot and sometimes by a burst blood vessel.[^4] The American Stroke Association's FAST test picks up the most common signs:[^5]

| Letter | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| **F**ace | Does one side of the face droop when the person smiles? |
| **A**rm | Does one arm drift down or feel weak when both are raised? |
| **S**peech | Is their speech slurred, strange or hard to understand? |
| **T**ime | If you see any of these, call your local emergency number now |

Some hospitals teach BE-FAST, which adds **B**alance (sudden loss of balance or coordination) and **E**yes (sudden loss of vision or double vision). The word that ties every version together is sudden: sudden numbness or weakness, especially on one side of the body, sudden confusion, sudden trouble seeing or walking, or a sudden severe headache with no known cause.[^5]

> Brain cells lost every minute that a typical large-vessel ischemic stroke goes untreated.[^8]

The main treatments are tied to the clock. Clot-dissolving medicine works best the sooner it is given and is generally only an option within about 4.5 hours of the first symptoms.[^6] A procedure to pull the clot out, called thrombectomy, can help selected people for longer, in some cases up to 24 hours, but earlier is still better.[^7]

So do two things while you wait for help. Note the time the symptoms started, or when the person was last seen well, because the hospital team will ask. And do not give aspirin: some strokes are bleeds, aspirin could make a bleed worse, and only a brain scan can tell the two types apart.[^4]

## A TIA is a warning, not a lucky escape

A transient ischemic attack (TIA), often called a mini-stroke, causes the same symptoms as a stroke, but they clear up, usually within minutes to hours.[^10] It is tempting to shrug it off and carry on with your day.

Please do not. In a study of about 1,700 people diagnosed with a TIA in Californian emergency departments, roughly 1 in 10 went on to have a stroke within 90 days, and half of those strokes came in the first two days.[^9]

While it is happening, you cannot tell a TIA from a full stroke. Call your local emergency number even if the symptoms have already faded. An urgent assessment can find the cause, such as a narrowed artery or an irregular heart rhythm like [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), and treat it before a larger stroke follows.[^10]

## Cardiac arrest is not a heart attack

People use the terms interchangeably, but they are different emergencies. A heart attack is a circulation problem: an artery is blocked, the heart usually keeps beating and the person is usually awake. A cardiac arrest is an electrical problem: the heart suddenly stops pumping, and the person collapses, does not respond and stops breathing normally or only gasps. A heart attack can trigger a cardiac arrest, and without help a cardiac arrest is fatal within minutes.[^11]

If someone collapses and is not breathing normally:[^11][^12]

1. **Call your local emergency number,** or get someone else to, and send for a defibrillator (AED) if one is nearby.
2. **Start chest compressions.** Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest with your other hand on top, and push hard and fast, at 100 to 120 compressions a minute.
3. **Use the AED as soon as it arrives.** Switch it on and follow its spoken instructions.
4. **Keep going** until help takes over.

You do not need to give rescue breaths. The American Heart Association recommends hands-only CPR for bystanders who see a teenager or adult collapse, and immediate CPR can double or triple the chance of survival.[^12]

> [!TIP] Learn it before you need it
> Find out where the nearest defibrillator is at work, at the gym and near home, and consider a CPR class. Our [first aid basics](/articles/first-aid-basics) guide is a good place to start.

## The bottom line

Chest pressure that will not go away, unexplained breathlessness, a drooping face, a weak arm, slurred speech: call your local emergency number first and think second. Note the time, take aspirin for a suspected heart attack only if it is safe for you, and start chest compressions if someone collapses and stops breathing normally. A false alarm costs you an afternoon. Waiting can cost heart muscle, brain or a life.

Most of these emergencies are preventable, so once the crisis is over, it is worth knowing [what drives your risk](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors).
