Fish oil is one of the best-selling supplements in the world, bought largely on the idea that it protects the heart. The idea is not silly. People who eat fish regularly do have less heart disease.

The trouble started when researchers put the capsules to the test. This explainer sorts out what holds up: fish on your plate, supplements from a bottle, and the prescription products that are a different thing altogether.

## Three omega-3s, two sources

Omega-3s are a family of polyunsaturated fats (our guide to [dietary fats](/articles/dietary-fats-decoded) covers the wider family). Three matter in human nutrition:[^1]

- **ALA** (alpha-linolenic acid) comes from plants such as flaxseed, chia, walnuts and canola oil. It is essential, meaning you have to get it from food.
- **EPA and DHA** come mainly from fish and other seafood. Your body can make them from ALA, but only in small amounts: reported conversion rates are below 15%.

There is an official adequate intake for ALA (1.6 g a day for men, 1.1 g for women) but none for EPA and DHA.[^1]

## Eating fish: the best-supported advice

The American Heart Association recommends two servings of fish a week, particularly oily fish. A serving is about 85 g (3 oz) cooked.[^2] Its 2018 science advisory concluded that one to two seafood meals a week are associated with a lower risk of coronary heart disease, heart failure, ischemic stroke and sudden cardiac death, especially when the fish replaces less healthy food.[^3]

That last clause matters. This evidence is observational, and part of the benefit probably comes from what fish pushes off the plate. It is one reason fish sits at the center of the [Mediterranean diet](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide).

Roughly what a serving provides:[^1]

| Food | Omega-3 type | Approximate amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Farmed Atlantic salmon, 85 g cooked | EPA and DHA | 1.8 g |
| Herring, 85 g cooked | EPA and DHA | 1.7 g |
| Canned sardines, 85 g | EPA and DHA | 1.2 g |
| Mackerel, 85 g cooked | EPA and DHA | 1.0 g |
| Canned light tuna, 85 g | EPA and DHA | 0.2 g |
| Chia seeds, 28 g (1 oz) | ALA | 5.1 g |
| Walnuts, 28 g (1 oz) | ALA | 2.6 g |
| Flaxseed, 1 tablespoon | ALA | 2.4 g |

> [!NOTE] Pregnancy, children and mercury
> Fish is recommended in pregnancy, but some large predatory species carry too much mercury. The US FDA advises people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, and young children, to choose lower-mercury fish such as salmon, sardines and trout, and to avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, tilefish and bigeye tuna.[^11]

## Supplements: mostly a letdown

A typical fish oil capsule holds about 1,000 mg of oil, of which roughly 300 mg is EPA and DHA.[^1] Whether swallowing them prevents heart attacks has now been tested in very large randomized trials.

- **VITAL** gave about 26,000 generally healthy older US adults 1 g of fish oil a day or a placebo for around five years. Major cardiovascular events were not significantly reduced. Heart attacks alone were 28% less common, but that was a secondary result the investigators treated with caution.[^4]
- **ASCEND** tested the same approach in about 15,500 people with diabetes for more than seven years and found no significant difference in serious vascular events.[^5]
- **A Cochrane review** pooling 86 trials and more than 160,000 participants concluded that increasing EPA and DHA has little or no effect on deaths or cardiovascular events overall. It may slightly reduce coronary heart disease deaths and events, though that evidence is of low certainty.[^6]

So for people without heart disease, the honest summary is that standard fish oil capsules do little that anyone can measure. That puts them in the same category as most pills covered in [who actually needs supplements](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them).

## The exception: prescription-strength EPA

One trial broke the pattern. REDUCE-IT enrolled about 8,200 people who were already taking statins, still had raised triglycerides, and had either cardiovascular disease or diabetes with other risk factors. They took icosapent ethyl, a purified, high-dose EPA medicine, or a placebo for nearly five years. Major cardiovascular events occurred in 17.2% of the treatment group and 22.0% of the placebo group, a 25% relative reduction.[^7]

> [!STAT] 25%
> The relative reduction in major cardiovascular events with prescription icosapent ethyl in REDUCE-IT, among statin-treated people with raised triglycerides.[^7]

Then came STRENGTH. It tested a different high-dose product combining EPA and DHA in about 13,000 similar patients and was stopped early because it was not working: event rates were 12.0% and 12.2%.[^8]

Why the difference? Nobody is certain. One explanation is that purified EPA does something the EPA and DHA mix does not. Another is the placebo. REDUCE-IT used mineral oil, which may have slightly worsened cholesterol and inflammation markers in the comparison group and flattered the drug, while STRENGTH used corn oil.[^8] The debate is unresolved, although the US FDA went on to approve icosapent ethyl for cardiovascular risk reduction in the kind of patients studied.[^1]

Either way, this result belongs to a specific medicine in a specific high-risk group. It says nothing about supermarket capsules.

## The atrial fibrillation signal

Omega-3 supplements are not risk-free. A meta-analysis of seven large trials with about 81,000 participants found that marine omega-3 supplements raised the risk of [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), an irregular heart rhythm that increases stroke risk, by 25% overall. The risk was 49% higher in trials using more than 1 g a day and 12% higher at 1 g a day or less.[^9] REDUCE-IT also recorded more hospital admissions for atrial fibrillation in the treatment group, 3.1% compared with 2.1%.[^7]

> [!WARNING]
> Fish oil can also cause fishy aftertaste, heartburn, nausea and loose stools, and high doses may affect bleeding, which matters if you take anticoagulants. The FDA considers supplements providing up to 5 g a day of EPA and DHA combined to be safe when used as recommended, but more is not better.[^1] Tell your doctor and pharmacist about any supplement you take.

## Who might be prescribed omega-3

Prescription omega-3 products are licensed medicines, not stronger supplements. Doctors use them in two situations:

- **Very high triglycerides.** At prescription doses, omega-3s lower triglycerides by 20% to 30% in most people, and by 30% or more when levels are very high. The American Heart Association notes that supplements are not reviewed or approved by the FDA in the same way and should not stand in for prescription products.[^10]
- **Cardiovascular risk reduction** in people on statins with raised triglycerides and established cardiovascular disease, or diabetes plus other risk factors, where icosapent ethyl may be added.[^7]

Do not try to recreate a prescription with handfuls of capsules. Triglycerides are one line on a lipid panel, and our [cholesterol explainer](/articles/cholesterol-explained) covers the rest.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor before taking omega-3 supplements if you have a heart rhythm problem, take blood thinners or are due for surgery. Get checked if you notice palpitations, a fluttering or irregular pulse, unusual breathlessness or dizziness while taking them. High triglycerides need a proper assessment, not self-treatment.

## The bottom line

Eat fish, ideally oily fish, about twice a week, and get plant omega-3 from seeds and nuts. If you are generally healthy, the best trials offer no good reason to take fish oil capsules for your heart, and at higher intakes they raise the risk of atrial fibrillation. If your triglycerides are high or you already have heart disease, prescription omega-3 is a conversation to have with your doctor, not a decision to make in the supplement aisle.
