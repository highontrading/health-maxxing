Blood pressure is one of the few health numbers that almost everyone has had measured and almost no one has had explained. You get two numbers, a nod or a frown, and you are out the door.

That is a shame, because raised blood pressure is common, usually silent and very treatable. The WHO estimates that 1.4 billion adults aged 30 to 79 had hypertension in 2024, and about 44% of them did not know it.[^4] This guide covers what the numbers mean, where the cut-offs sit (they differ by country), how to measure properly at home, and what brings the numbers down.

## What the two numbers mean

A reading such as 128/82 mm Hg has two parts. The first, systolic pressure, is the force of blood against your artery walls when your heart beats. The second, diastolic pressure, is the force between beats, while the heart refills.[^2] The unit, millimeters of mercury, is a leftover from the mercury columns once used to measure it.

Either number can be too high, and a diagnosis can rest on either one. For people over 50, systolic pressure tends to be the better guide to risk.[^2]

## The categories, and why they depend on where you live

The 2025 guideline from the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology kept the US categories introduced in 2017:[^1][^2]

| Category | Systolic | | Diastolic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Below 120 | and | Below 80 |
| Elevated | 120 to 129 | and | Below 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130 to 139 | or | 80 to 89 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | 140 or higher | or | 90 or higher |

A reading above 180 systolic or 120 diastolic is in a class of its own. Without symptoms, the guideline now calls it severe hypertension (the older term was hypertensive urgency), and it needs a prompt call to your doctor. With symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, numbness, weakness, vision changes or trouble speaking, it is a hypertensive emergency.[^1][^2]

Elsewhere the line is drawn higher. The WHO defines hypertension as 140/90 mm Hg or above.[^4] The UK's NICE uses 140/90 in the clinic, confirmed by a home or ambulatory average of 135/85 or more.[^5] The European Society of Cardiology also keeps hypertension at 140/90, but in 2024 it added an "elevated" band covering 120 to 139 systolic or 70 to 89 diastolic, and it treats only readings below 120/70 as non-elevated.[^3]

These bodies are reading the same evidence. They disagree mostly about labels: the European "elevated" band exists precisely because cardiovascular risk starts to rise before 140/90.[^3] Our [blood pressure checker](/tools/blood-pressure) shows where a reading falls.

## One reading is not a diagnosis

Blood pressure shifts from minute to minute with stress, caffeine, a full bladder or a dash for the bus. That is why guidelines ask for repeated measurements, and why the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends confirming a high clinic reading with measurements taken outside the clinic before anyone starts treatment.[^10]

Two patterns explain the caution:

- **White-coat hypertension** means high readings in the clinic and normal ones at home. US guideline authors estimate it averages about 13% of people with high office readings, and reaches 35% in some groups.[^9]
- **Masked hypertension** is the reverse: fine in the clinic, high in daily life. It is thought to affect 15% to 30% of adults whose office readings look normal, it is linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, and it is easy to miss.[^7]

The fix for both is the same. Measure at home, or wear a 24-hour ambulatory monitor that takes readings while you go about your day and while you sleep.

## How to get a reading you can trust at home

The American Heart Association's checklist is short:[^6]

- **Use the right device.** Choose an automatic upper-arm cuff that has been independently validated, in a size that fits your arm. Wrist and finger monitors are less reliable.
- **Prepare.** No caffeine, smoking or exercise for 30 minutes beforehand, and empty your bladder.
- **Sit properly.** Back supported, feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed. Rest quietly for five minutes without talking or scrolling.
- **Position the cuff.** Place it on bare skin just above the bend of your elbow, with your arm resting on a table at heart level.
- **Repeat.** Take two readings one minute apart, at the same times each day, and record them all.

Arm position is not a fussy detail. In a randomized crossover trial of 133 adults, resting the arm in the lap overestimated systolic pressure by 3.9 mm Hg compared with supporting it on a desk. Letting it hang at the side overestimated it by 6.5 mm Hg.[^8]

> [!STAT] 6.5 mm Hg
> How much an unsupported arm hanging at your side can inflate a systolic reading. That is enough to push a borderline result into the next category.[^8]

A diagnosis rests on the average of readings taken over several days, not on any single one. Home numbers also run a little lower than clinic numbers: NICE treats a home average of 135/85 as the equivalent of 140/90 in the clinic.[^5]

Skip the smartwatch for now. The 2025 US guideline advises against relying on cuffless devices until they prove more accurate.[^1]

## What lowers it, and by how much

Lifestyle changes come first at every level, and they remain the foundation if you take medication.[^1] The 2017 US guideline estimated what each one does to systolic pressure in people who already have hypertension:[^9]

| Change | Typical drop in systolic pressure |
| --- | --- |
| DASH-style eating pattern | About 11 mm Hg |
| Aerobic exercise, 90 to 150 minutes a week | 5 to 8 mm Hg |
| Less sodium | 5 to 6 mm Hg |
| More potassium from food | 4 to 5 mm Hg |
| Weight loss | About 1 mm Hg per kg (2.2 lb) lost |
| Less alcohol, if you drink more than 1 or 2 drinks a day | About 4 mm Hg |

These are averages from trials, and the effects overlap rather than adding up neatly. The 2025 update adds specifics: keep sodium under 2,300 mg a day and ideally nearer 1,500 mg, aim to lose at least 5% of your body weight if you are overweight, and limit or avoid alcohol. It also suggests potassium-enriched salt substitutes, with a caution for people who have kidney disease or take medicines that raise potassium.[^1]

> [!TIP] Read the label before you hide the salt shaker
> Most of the sodium in a typical US diet comes from packaged and restaurant food, not from salt added at the table, so the 2025 guideline points people to food labels first.[^1]

Our guide to [the DASH diet and salt](/articles/dash-diet-and-salt) covers the eating side, and [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) covers the movement.

## When medication comes in

Under the 2025 US guideline, medication alongside lifestyle change is recommended straight away for stage 2 hypertension, often as two medicines combined in one pill. For stage 1, medication is recommended if you already have cardiovascular disease, diabetes or chronic kidney disease, or if your 10-year cardiovascular risk is 7.5% or higher on the PREVENT calculator. If your risk is lower, the advice is three to six months of lifestyle change first, then medication if your pressure is still 130/80 or above.[^1]

The US goal for most adults on treatment is below 130/80.[^1] European guidelines aim for a treated systolic pressure of 120 to 129 where that is well tolerated, with more leeway for frail or older people.[^3]

Which medicine to use, and when, is a decision to make with your doctor based on your readings and your [overall heart risk](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors). Treatment is usually long term. Good numbers on medication mean it is working, not that it is no longer needed.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Book an appointment if your home readings are consistently 130/80 mm Hg or higher. If a reading is above 180/120, rest for five minutes and measure again. If it is still that high and you feel well, contact your doctor promptly. If it comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, a change in vision or difficulty speaking, call your local emergency number without waiting to see if it settles.[^2]

## The bottom line

Know your numbers, and make sure they are real: a validated upper-arm cuff, a supported arm, five quiet minutes and an average taken over several days. If that average is 130/80 or higher, talk to a clinician about your overall risk and start with the changes that have measurable effects, such as a DASH-style diet, less salt, more movement and less alcohol. If medication is recommended, it works alongside those habits, not instead of them.
