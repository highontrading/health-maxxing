"I'm allergic to dairy" and "milk doesn't agree with me" sound like the same complaint. They are very different problems. One is an immune reaction that can, at its worst, close an airway. The other is a digestive issue that is unpleasant but not dangerous. A third condition, celiac disease, is neither.

Mixing them up matters in both directions. People with true allergies sometimes underestimate the risk, and many more people give up foods they could happily eat. In a US survey of more than 40,000 adults, about 19% believed they had a food allergy, while about 11% reported symptoms convincing for one.[^4]

> [!STAT] 19% vs 11%
> US adults who believe they have a food allergy, compared with those whose reported reactions are convincing for one.[^4]

## The core difference

A **food allergy** involves the immune system. In the classic type, the body makes IgE antibodies against a food protein. When you eat that food again, immune cells release histamine and other chemicals, and symptoms appear within minutes to a couple of hours.[^1]

A **food intolerance** does not involve the immune system in that way. Usually your gut cannot fully digest or absorb something, or you are sensitive to a natural chemical or additive in the food.[^2]

| | Food allergy | Food intolerance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is reacting | Immune system | Mostly the digestive system |
| Onset | Minutes to 2 hours | Usually a few hours |
| Amount needed | A trace can be enough | Depends on the dose |
| Typical symptoms | Hives, swelling, vomiting, wheeze | Bloating, gas, abdominal pain, diarrhea |
| Worst case | Anaphylaxis, which can be fatal | Miserable, but not life-threatening |

Sources for the table: the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and the NHS.[^1][^2][^3]

## Food allergy: small list, big consequences

A handful of foods cause most serious reactions. US law names nine major allergens that must be declared on labels: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and, since 2023, sesame.[^5] Other countries use somewhat different lists, so check local labeling rules when you travel.

Symptoms can include an itchy mouth, hives, swelling of the lips, face or throat, vomiting, abdominal pain, wheezing and dizziness.[^3] The most severe reaction is [anaphylaxis](/conditions/anaphylaxis), which affects breathing or circulation and needs immediate treatment with epinephrine (adrenaline).[^1]

Allergies are not only a childhood problem. In the same US survey, about half of adults with a food allergy said at least one had started in adulthood, and shellfish was the most common culprit.[^4]

### How allergy is diagnosed

Diagnosis starts with the story: what you ate, how quickly symptoms began and what they were. Skin-prick tests and blood tests for food-specific IgE can support it, but a positive test alone only shows sensitization, which is not the same as a clinical allergy. When doubt remains, the definitive test is an oral food challenge under medical supervision.[^1]

## Food intolerance: common, dose-dependent, manageable

### Lactose

Lactose, the sugar in milk, needs the enzyme lactase to be absorbed. Most of the world's adults make less of it after childhood. A pooled analysis estimated that about 68% of people worldwide have lactose malabsorption, ranging from under 30% in northern and western Europe to more than 60% in Asia and the Middle East.[^6]

Malabsorption is not the same as intolerance, which means actually getting symptoms. Research suggests many people with lactose malabsorption can have about 12 g of lactose, the amount in a cup (240 mL) of milk, with no or only mild symptoms, especially with a meal.[^7] Hard cheeses and yogurt are often tolerated better than milk, and lactose-free dairy and lactase tablets are other options. If you do cut down on dairy, make sure you get calcium and vitamin D elsewhere.[^7]

### FODMAPs and other culprits

Lactose is one of a wider family of poorly absorbed carbohydrates known as FODMAPs, found in foods such as wheat, onions, garlic, beans and some fruits. They are a frequent trigger of bloating and pain in people with irritable bowel syndrome; see [IBS and the low-FODMAP diet](/articles/ibs-and-the-low-fodmap-diet). Some people also react to caffeine, alcohol, histamine in aged and fermented foods, or sulfites.[^2]

## Celiac disease is neither

[Celiac disease](/conditions/celiac-disease) is an autoimmune condition. In people with certain genes, gluten (a protein in wheat, barley and rye) provokes the immune system to attack the lining of the small intestine.[^9] It affects roughly 1% of people worldwide, and many of them have not been diagnosed.[^8][^9]

Symptoms range from diarrhea, bloating and weight loss to iron-deficiency anemia, fatigue and an itchy skin rash, and some people have no gut symptoms at all.[^9] Diagnosis is by blood tests for specific antibodies, usually followed by a biopsy of the small intestine. The only treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet.[^9]

> [!WARNING]
> Get tested for celiac disease before you cut out gluten. The tests depend on gluten being in your diet, and going gluten-free first can hide the diagnosis.[^9]

### What about gluten sensitivity?

Some people test negative for celiac disease and wheat allergy but still feel unwell after eating wheat. This is called non-celiac gluten (or wheat) sensitivity, and there is no test for it. Gluten may not even be the cause. In a blinded trial of 59 people who believed they were gluten sensitive, fructans, a FODMAP found in wheat, triggered more symptoms than gluten itself.[^10]

## Tests that are not worth your money

Online "food intolerance" panels usually measure IgG antibodies to dozens of foods. A European allergy task force concluded that food-specific IgG4 indicates repeated exposure to a food, and probably tolerance, not intolerance, and that testing for it is not recommended.[^11] The NHS likewise says commercial intolerance tests are not based on scientific evidence.[^2]

The cost is not only financial. Long lists of "reactive" foods lead people to cut out nutritious foods for no reason, which is a particular worry for children.

## What actually works: eliminate, then reintroduce

For a suspected intolerance, the reliable method is unglamorous:[^2]

1. Keep a food and symptom diary for a couple of weeks.
2. Remove the one suspected food for a few weeks.
3. Reintroduce it and see whether symptoms return.

Do this with a doctor or dietitian if you can, and always if the diet is for a child. Do not test a suspected allergy this way at home. If a food has ever caused hives, swelling or breathing trouble, reintroduction belongs in a specialist clinic.[^1]

> [!NOTE]
> Avoidance is not always the best prevention. In a landmark trial in infants at high risk of peanut allergy, introducing peanut early and eating it regularly cut the rate of peanut allergy at age five by about 80% compared with avoiding it.[^12] Guidance for babies now favors early introduction; ask your pediatrician how to do it safely.

## When to get help

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number immediately for signs of anaphylaxis: difficulty breathing or swallowing, swelling of the tongue or throat, wheezing, a weak or rapid pulse, or feeling faint. Use an epinephrine auto-injector straight away if one is available.[^1][^3] See a doctor soon after any suspected allergic reaction, and for ongoing gut symptoms, especially with weight loss, anemia or blood in the stool.

## The bottom line

Fast reactions with hives, swelling or breathing symptoms point to allergy and need proper testing and an emergency plan. Slower, dose-dependent gut symptoms point to intolerance, which you can pin down with a careful elimination and reintroduction. Rule out celiac disease before giving up gluten, and skip the IgG kits.
