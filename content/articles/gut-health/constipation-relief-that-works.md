Constipation is one of the most common digestive complaints: about 16 in 100 adults have symptoms, rising to about 33 in 100 among people aged 60 and over.[^1] It also attracts some of the vaguest advice in medicine. "More fiber, more water" is a start, but some fibers work better than others, some fruits have been tested head to head against supplements, and laxatives are not all equal.

This guide ranks the options by how good the evidence is, then puts them in a sensible order to try.

## What counts as constipation

You do not need to go every day. Constipation means some combination of:[^1][^2]

- fewer than three bowel movements a week
- stools that are hard, dry or lumpy
- stools that are difficult or painful to pass
- a feeling that you have not fully emptied your bowels

The Bristol stool chart, developed by researchers who showed that stool form tracks how long food takes to pass through the gut, sorts stools into seven types.[^3] Types 1 and 2 (separate hard lumps, or a lumpy sausage) point to constipation. Types 3 and 4 (a sausage with cracks, or smooth and soft) are the target. Types 5 to 7 are increasingly loose.

> [!STAT] 16 in 100
> Adults who have symptoms of constipation. Among people aged 60 and over it is about 33 in 100.[^1]

## The evidence at a glance

The clearest ranking comes from the 2023 joint guideline of the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) and the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) on chronic constipation.[^4] Food and habit changes were outside its scope, so those rows rest on smaller studies and public health advice.

| Option | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Polyethylene glycol (PEG, macrogol) | Strong recommendation in the AGA-ACG guideline |
| Bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate | Strong recommendation for short-term or rescue use |
| Psyllium (fiber supplement) | Conditional recommendation; the best-supported fiber |
| Senna, magnesium oxide, lactulose | Conditional recommendations |
| Kiwifruit, prunes | Small randomized trials; similar to psyllium |
| Toilet routine, posture, movement, fluids | Sensible and low risk; lightly studied |

Strongest evidence is not the same as first choice. Most people should start at the gentle end, with food and habits, and move up only if needed.

## Start with fiber, kiwifruit or prunes

Adults need roughly 22 to 34 g of fiber a day depending on age and sex, and most of us fall short.[^8] Vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds all count; see our [fiber guide](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient) for the best sources.

If you add a supplement, choose psyllium (also sold as ispaghula husk). The AGA-ACG guideline suggests fiber supplements for chronic constipation and notes that, among them, only psyllium has reasonably good trial evidence. It also stresses drinking enough fluid with it, and warns that gas is a common side effect.[^4] Add fiber a little at a time so your gut can adjust.[^8]

Two fruits have been tested against psyllium:

- **Kiwifruit.** In a US trial of about 80 adults with chronic constipation, two green kiwifruit a day, about 100 g of prunes a day, or 12 g of psyllium a day all increased bowel movements over four weeks. Kiwifruit caused the fewest side effects.[^5] A larger trial in New Zealand, Italy and Japan found that two green kiwifruit a day increased complete bowel movements and eased abdominal discomfort in people with constipation.[^6]
- **Prunes.** In a crossover trial of 40 adults, 50 g of prunes twice a day (about 10 to 12 prunes in total) improved stool frequency and consistency more than psyllium did.[^7]

These trials were small and several had fruit industry funding, so treat the results as encouraging, not final. But two kiwifruit or a handful of prunes is a cheap, safe experiment.

## Habits that cost nothing

**Use the morning reflex.** Eating stimulates the colon. US guidance suggests trying to have a bowel movement at the same time each day, for instance 15 to 45 minutes after breakfast.[^9]

**Do not ignore the urge.** Go when you need to, and give yourself time.[^2]

**Fix your position.** The NHS advises resting your feet on a low stool so that your knees are above your hips.[^2] This puts your body closer to a squat.

**Move.** Regular physical activity may help relieve symptoms.[^9] A daily walk counts.

**Drink enough.** Water and other fluids help fiber work better and keep stools softer.[^8] Our guide to [how much water you need](/articles/how-much-water-do-you-need) can help you judge.

> [!TIP] Give each change a fair trial
> Make one change at a time and stick with it for two to four weeks before judging it. The fruit and fiber trials ran for three to four weeks.[^5][^7]

## Over-the-counter laxatives

If food and habits are not enough, laxatives are a reasonable next step. Use them as directed on the label.

- **Osmotic laxatives** draw water into the bowel. PEG (called macrogol in the UK) is the standout: the AGA-ACG guideline strongly recommends it and notes that the benefit lasts over at least six months of use.[^4] Lactulose and magnesium oxide have conditional recommendations, and magnesium should be avoided by people with kidney disease.[^4] Osmotic laxatives usually take two or three days to work.[^10]
- **Stimulant laxatives** such as senna and bisacodyl make the bowel muscles contract and usually work within 6 to 12 hours.[^10] The guideline strongly recommends bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate, but for short-term use (four weeks or less) or as occasional rescue, because cramping and diarrhea are common and long-term data are limited.[^4]
- **Bulk-forming laxatives** are fiber supplements such as psyllium and take two or three days to work.[^10]

How long to continue is where advice differs. The NHS tells the public to use laxatives only occasionally, for up to a week at a time, and to see a doctor if constipation has not improved.[^10] The US guideline, written for doctors treating chronic constipation, supports longer use of PEG.[^4] The practical message is the same: if you need a laxative regularly, get a medical opinion on the cause and the plan.

There are further options if these fail. Prescription medicines exist for constipation that does not respond, and some people have a pelvic floor coordination problem that improves with biofeedback therapy.[^4][^9]

## Check your medicine cabinet

Many common medicines slow the bowel. The list includes opioid painkillers, iron supplements, antacids containing aluminum or calcium, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, anticholinergic drugs and some antidepressants.[^11]

> [!WARNING]
> Do not stop a prescribed medicine because you suspect it is causing constipation. Ask your doctor or pharmacist; there is often an alternative, or a laxative can be added.

## When constipation needs a doctor

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly if constipation comes with blood in your stool or bleeding from your rectum, constant abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, being unable to pass gas, or weight loss you cannot explain.[^11] Also book a visit if your bowel habits change suddenly and stay changed, if you are regularly constipated, or if self-care has not helped.[^2]

A lasting, unexplained change in bowel habits, particularly in middle age or later, should be checked to rule out conditions such as [colorectal cancer](/conditions/colorectal-cancer). Constipation in babies and children is managed differently, so ask a pediatrician or pharmacist before giving a child a laxative.

## The bottom line

Start with psyllium, kiwifruit or prunes, a regular morning toilet routine, a footstool and a daily walk. If that is not enough after a few weeks, PEG is the best-supported over-the-counter laxative, with senna or bisacodyl for short-term backup. And if constipation is new, persistent or comes with bleeding, pain or weight loss, see a doctor before reaching for anything else.
