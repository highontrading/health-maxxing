You can now buy probiotics as capsules, gummies, shots, sodas and chocolate bars, usually with a promise about "gut health" on the front. The enthusiasm has run well ahead of the evidence. But the evidence is not empty: a few uses are reasonably well supported, many are not, and the cheapest options, ordinary fermented foods and fiber-rich plants, hold up better than you might expect.

This guide untangles the three terms, shows where trials support probiotics and where guidelines say no, and explains how to choose a product if you still want to try one.

## Three words that get mixed up

| Term | What it means | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probiotic | Live microorganisms that, in adequate amounts, give a health benefit | Named strains in supplements and some yogurts |
| Prebiotic | A substance your resident microbes selectively use, with a health benefit | Inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS) |
| Fermented food | Food made through desired microbial growth and enzyme activity | Yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, kombucha |

These definitions come from expert panels convened by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics, and the fine print matters.[^1][^2][^3] A probiotic has to be alive, present in a sufficient dose and shown in studies to do something useful.[^1] A fermented food has to pass none of those tests. Some are full of live microbes. Others, such as sourdough bread, soy sauce or pasteurized sauerkraut, have none left by the time you eat them.[^3]

## Benefits belong to strains, not to "probiotics"

"Probiotic" is a category, like "medicine". What one strain does in a trial tells you little about another, and recommendations need to be specific to the strain and the condition.[^10]

A good label names the genus, species and strain, for example *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG, and gives the dose in colony-forming units (CFU), a count of live cells. Many supplements provide 1 to 10 billion CFU per dose. A higher count is not automatically better.[^10]

## Where the evidence is decent

### Diarrhea during a course of antibiotics

Antibiotics disturb gut bacteria, and diarrhea is a common result. A Cochrane review of 33 trials in more than 6,000 children found that about 8% of those given probiotics developed antibiotic-associated diarrhea, compared with about 19% of those given a placebo or no treatment. The reviewers rated the evidence as moderate certainty, and the best-studied options were *Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus* GG and the yeast *Saccharomyces boulardii*.[^5]

> [!STAT] 8% vs 19%
> Children who developed antibiotic-associated diarrhea with and without probiotics, in a Cochrane review of 33 trials.[^5]

In adults, a pooled analysis of dozens of trials found the risk was roughly a third lower with probiotics.[^6]

### C. difficile

*Clostridioides difficile* (C. diff) is a more serious infection that can take hold after antibiotics. A Cochrane review found that probiotics given alongside antibiotics lowered the risk of C. diff diarrhea by about 60%, with moderate-certainty evidence. The benefit was concentrated in people at high risk to begin with.[^7]

On that basis the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) made a conditional, meaning weak, recommendation in 2020 for certain strains and combinations in people taking antibiotics. It added that people at low risk, such as otherwise healthy outpatients, could reasonably choose to skip them.[^4]

## Where guidelines say hold off

The same AGA guideline reviewed probiotics for a range of digestive conditions and was unimpressed:[^4]

- **Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, irritable bowel syndrome and treating C. diff infection:** use probiotics only as part of a clinical trial.
- **Children with acute infectious diarrhea (in the US and Canada):** the guideline suggests against them.
- **Two narrow exceptions** received conditional support: pouchitis, a complication of bowel surgery, and prevention of a serious bowel condition in premature, low-birth-weight infants. Both are decisions for specialists, not for the supplement aisle.

Guidance on a time-limited trial for IBS differs a little between countries; our [IBS guide](/articles/ibs-and-the-low-fodmap-diet) has the details.

For healthy people hoping for general benefits, the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health is blunt: researchers still do not know which probiotics help, at what dose or for whom, and the US Food and Drug Administration has not approved any probiotic to prevent or treat a health problem.[^9]

## Fermented foods: food first

Fermented foods have less clinical trial evidence than supplements, but what there is looks interesting. In a randomized trial at Stanford, 36 healthy adults ate either a high-fiber diet or a diet rich in fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, kombucha and similar) for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group ended up with a more diverse [gut microbiome](/articles/gut-microbiome-explained) and lower blood levels of 19 inflammatory proteins.[^8] It was one small study that measured lab markers, not illness, so treat it as promising, not proven.

If live microbes are the goal, the type of product matters:[^3]

- **Usually contain live microbes:** yogurt and kefir with live cultures, refrigerated unpasteurized sauerkraut and kimchi, miso, natto.
- **Usually do not:** sourdough bread (baking kills them), shelf-stable pasteurized sauerkraut, soy sauce, most beer and wine. Pickles made with vinegar are not fermented at all.

> [!TIP] Check two things on the label
> Look for "live" or "active" cultures, then check the salt and added sugar. Kimchi, sauerkraut and miso can be salty, and some kombuchas and flavored yogurts are closer to soft drinks and desserts.

## Prebiotics: feeding the microbes you already have

Prebiotics are mostly specific types of fiber. The best established are inulin, FOS and GOS, which your gut bacteria ferment.[^2] You do not need a powder to get them. Onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, chicory root, wheat and bananas contain inulin-type fibers, and beans, lentils and chickpeas supply GOS.[^2]

The usual trade-off is gas, especially if you increase your intake quickly.[^2] These are also among the fermentable carbohydrates restricted in a low-FODMAP diet, so people with IBS may tolerate them poorly. For most people, simply eating more varied, [fiber-rich plants](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient) does the job.

## Safety: who should be careful

For healthy people, probiotics have a good safety record, and side effects are usually limited to gas or mild bloating.[^9] The picture changes for vulnerable groups. Serious and sometimes fatal infections have been reported in premature infants, and the risk of harm is higher in people who are severely ill or have weakened immune systems.[^9]

Quality is a second issue. In the US most probiotics are sold as dietary supplements, which do not need to prove that they work before going on sale, and products do not always contain what the label says.[^9][^10]

Finally, taking a probiotic to "restore" your gut after antibiotics may not do what you expect. In one small study, healthy volunteers who took a multi-strain probiotic after antibiotics were slower to regain their original gut bacteria than those who took nothing.[^11]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ask your doctor before using probiotics if you have a weakened immune system or a serious illness, or if the product is for a premature baby. Do not use a supplement in place of medical care: get advice promptly for diarrhea that starts during or after a course of antibiotics, is bloody or severe, lasts more than a few days, or comes with a fever or signs of dehydration.[^12]

## How to choose, if you want to try one

1. **Start with the problem.** Pick a product whose exact strain has been tested for the thing you want help with. For antibiotic-associated diarrhea, the trials mostly used *L. rhamnosus* GG or *S. boulardii* at 5 to 40 billion CFU a day.[^5]
2. **Read the label.** It should list the full strain name, storage instructions and the CFU count guaranteed through the end of shelf life, not at the time of manufacture.[^10]
3. **Set a time limit.** If you notice no difference after about a month, stop. A probiotic that is not helping is just an expensive habit.
4. **Consider food first.** A daily serving of live yogurt or kefir costs less than most capsules and brings protein and calcium with it.

## The bottom line

Probiotics are worth considering for one main job: lowering the risk of diarrhea while you take antibiotics. For most other uses, including general "gut health", guidelines do not recommend them, and people who are seriously ill or immunocompromised should avoid them unless a doctor advises otherwise. For everyone else, fermented foods and a wide range of fiber-rich plants are the sensible, low-cost bet.
