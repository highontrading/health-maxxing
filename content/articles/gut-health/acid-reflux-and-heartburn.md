A burning feeling behind the breastbone after a big dinner is one of the most familiar sensations in medicine. Almost everyone gets heartburn now and then. For a sizable minority it becomes a regular visitor that disrupts sleep, meals and mood.

The good news is that reflux is well studied. We know which habits help, which medicines work and how to use them without staying on them forever. This guide covers all three, plus the symptoms that should send you to a doctor.

## Reflux, heartburn and GERD

A ring of muscle at the bottom of your esophagus (food pipe) normally closes after food passes into the stomach. **Acid reflux** happens when that valve relaxes at the wrong time or is weak, and stomach contents flow back up.[^2] **Heartburn** is the burning that results.

Occasional reflux is normal. **Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)** is the name for reflux that causes troublesome symptoms or complications, such as inflammation of the esophagus.[^1][^3] Our [GERD overview](/conditions/gerd) has the short version.

> [!STAT] About 20%
> The share of people in the United States estimated to have GERD.[^1]

Things that make reflux more likely include excess weight, pregnancy, smoking or exposure to smoke, a hiatal hernia (where part of the stomach slides up through the diaphragm) and some medicines.[^2]

## Symptoms: the obvious and the sneaky

The classic pair is heartburn and regurgitation, a sour or bitter fluid rising into the throat or mouth.[^2]

Reflux can also show up in less obvious ways: a chronic cough, hoarseness, nausea, chest pain, or pain or difficulty when swallowing.[^2] These symptoms have many other causes, so US guidelines advise doctors to look for alternative explanations before blaming reflux when someone has a cough or hoarse voice without heartburn.[^3]

## Lifestyle changes with evidence behind them

Not every piece of traditional reflux advice has been tested. These have the best support.

**Lose weight if you carry excess.** This is the strongest recommendation among lifestyle measures in the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guideline.[^3] In studies, weight loss reduced both acid exposure in the esophagus and symptoms.[^4]

**Leave a gap between dinner and bed.** The ACG suggests avoiding meals within two to three hours of bedtime; the NHS says three to four.[^3][^5] Late meals increase acid exposure when you lie down.[^4]

**Raise the head of your bed.** Lift it by about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 inches) by safely putting blocks under the bedposts. Extra pillows alone do not do the job.[^6] Trials show that head-of-bed elevation reduces acid exposure at night.[^4]

**Stop smoking.** Quitting was followed by fewer reflux symptoms in a large population study, at least among people of normal weight, and both the ACG and the NHS recommend it.[^3][^4][^5] Our guide to [quitting smoking and vaping](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) can help.

**Try your left side.** A study that tracked sleeping position alongside acid levels overnight found less acid exposure when people slept on their left side.[^7]

**Avoid your own triggers, not everyone's.** Coffee, chocolate, alcohol, spicy, fatty and acidic foods are common culprits, but responses vary. The ACG suggests cutting the foods that reliably set off your symptoms, not a blanket ban.[^3]

How much could habits matter? In a study of about 43,000 US women, those who kept to five habits (a healthy weight, never smoking, 30 minutes of daily activity, no more than two cups of coffee, tea or soda, and a healthy diet) were about half as likely to develop weekly reflux symptoms. The researchers estimated these habits could prevent over a third of cases.[^8] It was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof.

> [!TIP] Change one thing at a time
> Keep a simple diary for two weeks: meals, timing, sleep position and symptoms. It will show you which of these levers matters for you.

## Medicines: three tiers

All three types are sold over the counter in many countries. Use them as directed on the label.

| Type | Examples | How it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antacids | Calcium carbonate, alginate products | Fastest relief, short-lived; for occasional heartburn |
| H2 blockers | Famotidine | Reduce acid for several hours; for milder or night-time symptoms |
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | Omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole | Strongest acid reduction; for frequent symptoms |

For typical heartburn and regurgitation without warning signs, the ACG recommends an eight-week trial of a once-daily PPI, taken 30 to 60 minutes before a meal.[^3] If you buy a PPI without a prescription, the NHS advises not taking it for more than two weeks without checking with a doctor.[^9]

### Using PPIs sensibly

PPIs are among the most widely used medicines in the world, and headlines linking them to fractures, kidney disease, dementia and more have worried many people. Those links come from observational studies, which cannot separate the drug from the health of the people who take it. A randomized trial that assigned more than 17,000 people to a PPI or a placebo and followed them for about three years found no increase in these problems, apart from a small rise in gut infections.[^10] The ACG's summary is that for people with GERD, the established benefits outweigh the theoretical risks.[^3]

That is not a reason to take them indefinitely by default. Guidelines recommend:

- trying to stop, or stepping down to the lowest effective dose, once symptoms are controlled[^3]
- regularly reviewing whether a PPI is still needed[^11]
- not stopping without medical advice if you have severe inflammation of the esophagus, Barrett's esophagus or a narrowing of the esophagus, since these need long-term treatment[^11]

Some people get a temporary flare of symptoms after stopping a PPI because acid production rebounds. It is usually short-lived, and it does not necessarily mean you need to restart.[^11]

If symptoms persist despite a proper PPI trial, the next step is investigation, usually an endoscopy, not a higher dose bought over the counter.[^3]

## When to worry

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly if you have difficulty or pain when swallowing, food sticking, persistent vomiting, vomit that contains blood or looks like coffee grounds, black stools or unexplained weight loss.[^2] Also get checked if you have heartburn most days for three weeks or more, or if pharmacy medicines are not helping.[^5]

> [!WARNING]
> Chest pain is not always heartburn. Call your local emergency number for chest pain or pressure, especially with shortness of breath, a cold sweat, nausea, lightheadedness or pain spreading to the arm, back, neck or jaw.[^12] See [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs).

Long-standing reflux can occasionally lead to Barrett's esophagus, a change in the lining that slightly raises cancer risk. If you have had reflux for years, ask your doctor whether an endoscopy makes sense for you.[^3]

## The bottom line

For occasional heartburn, an antacid and a longer gap between dinner and bed are usually enough. For frequent symptoms, combine the proven habits (lose excess weight, raise the bed head, stop smoking) with a time-limited PPI course, then step down. Swallowing problems, bleeding, weight loss or chest pain are not things to self-treat: get them assessed.
