Few parts of the body have been marketed as hard as the gut microbiome. Depending on who is selling, the microbes in your intestines control your mood, your weight, your skin and your energy, and a test kit or a capsule can put them right.

The real science is more interesting and more modest. Your gut microbes do useful work, and some of what you do changes them. But many of the bigger claims rest on mouse experiments and correlations. This explainer covers what the microbiome is, what it does, what shapes it, and where the evidence stops.

## What the microbiome is

Your gut microbiome is the community of microbes living in your digestive tract: mostly bacteria, plus viruses, fungi and other single-celled organisms. The vast majority live in the colon (large intestine).[^1]

You may have heard that bacteria outnumber your own cells ten to one. That long-repeated figure does not survive a careful count. A 2016 recount put the total at about 38 trillion bacteria in a 70 kg (154 lb) adult, alongside about 30 trillion human cells: a ratio of roughly 1.3 to 1.[^1]

> [!STAT] 38 trillion
> The estimated number of bacteria in a 70 kg adult, close to one for each human cell. It is an estimate for a "reference" adult, not an exact count for any one person.[^1]

## What your gut microbes do

The best-understood job is digesting what you cannot. Fiber passes through your small intestine largely untouched. In the colon, bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids, mainly acetate, propionate and butyrate.[^2]

These small molecules do several things:[^2]

- **Fuel.** Butyrate is the main energy source for the cells that line your colon.
- **Barrier.** Short-chain fatty acids help those cells maintain the tight seals and mucus layer that keep bacteria on the right side of the gut wall.
- **Immune calibration.** They signal to immune cells in the gut and encourage the regulatory cells that keep inflammation in check.

This is why fiber comes up in every sensible conversation about gut health: it is the raw material. Our guide to [fiber](/articles/fiber-the-most-underrated-nutrient) covers how much to aim for and where to find it.

## What shapes your microbiome

### Birth and early life

A study of 596 healthy, full-term babies found that almost all of those delivered by caesarean section had little or no *Bacteroides*, a common group of gut bacteria. Their guts were dominated instead by opportunistic species such as *Enterococcus* and *Klebsiella*. About half of the vaginally born babies had *Bacteroides* in abundance. In roughly 60% of the caesarean-born babies who were followed up, the pattern was still there later in infancy.[^3]

The study measured bacteria, not health, so it cannot say whether the difference matters in the long run. It is not a reason to avoid a caesarean that is medically needed.

### Diet

In the American Gut Project, which analyzed stool samples from more than 10,000 volunteers, people who reported eating more than 30 types of plants a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer, whatever label they gave their diet.[^4] This was an observational study, so it shows a link, not cause and effect.

A small randomized trial went a step further. Thirty-six healthy adults followed either a high-fiber diet or a diet rich in fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi and kombucha for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group ended up with more diverse microbiomes and lower blood levels of 19 inflammatory proteins. The high-fiber group did not show the same changes over that period.[^5] It is one small study, but it is an experiment rather than a survey, which is rare in this field. For more, see [probiotics, prebiotics and fermented foods](/articles/probiotics-prebiotics-fermented-foods).

### Antibiotics

When 12 healthy men took a four-day course of three powerful antibiotics, their gut communities returned to near baseline within about six weeks. Six months on, though, nine common species were still undetectable in most of them.[^6] That is an argument for using [antibiotics only when they are needed](/articles/antibiotics-when-they-help), not for refusing them when they are.

## The gut-brain axis: real, but oversold

Your gut and brain are in constant conversation. Signals travel along the vagus nerve, through the immune system, and via molecules that microbes make, including short-chain fatty acids. Most of what we know about how microbes join that conversation comes from animal experiments, and human research is still catching up.[^7][^8]

In people, the best data so far are associations. In a study of more than 1,000 Belgian adults, two groups of bacteria, *Coprococcus* and *Dialister*, were consistently scarcer in people with depression, even after accounting for antidepressant use.[^8] That is intriguing, but it cannot show which came first. Low mood changes how people eat, sleep and move, and any of those could shift gut bacteria.

Treatment trials are thin. A pooled analysis of seven small trials (404 participants) found that probiotics eased depressive symptoms when added to antidepressants, but not when used on their own.[^9] No probiotic is an established treatment for depression. If low mood is affecting your life, well-tested help exists: see [depression signs and treatment](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment).

## Weight: dramatic in mice, modest in people

Animal experiments suggesting that gut bacteria can influence body weight and metabolism raised hopes of a microbial fix for obesity. So researchers tested the idea in people. In one double-blind trial, 24 adults with obesity took weekly capsules of gut bacteria from lean donors, or placebo capsules, for six weeks. The donor bacteria took hold in most recipients. Their weight and insulin sensitivity did not meaningfully change.[^10]

The trial was small, and bigger ones may yet find subtler effects. For now, swapping gut bacteria is not a demonstrated route to weight loss.

## Where microbiome medicine does work

One microbiome treatment is well established. For people with repeated *Clostridioides difficile* (C. diff) infections, a serious gut infection that often follows antibiotic use, fecal microbiota-based therapies given after antibiotic treatment help stop the infection coming back. The American Gastroenterological Association suggests them for most adults with recurrent C. diff. The same guideline advises against fecal transplants for irritable bowel syndrome or inflammatory bowel disease outside clinical trials.[^11]

> [!WARNING]
> These are medical treatments that use carefully screened donor material under specialist care, and the guideline advises against them for people who are severely immunocompromised.[^11] They are not something to try at home.

## Should you buy a microbiome test?

Plenty of companies will sequence a stool sample and send back a score, a list of "good" and "bad" bacteria and, often, a shopping list. In 2025 an international expert panel reviewed the field and concluded there is not enough evidence to recommend microbiome testing in routine clinical practice. They noted that direct-to-consumer tests have multiplied without agreed regulation or proven clinical value, and that dysbiosis, the microbial "imbalance" these tests claim to detect, has no agreed definition.[^12]

> [!NOTE]
> A test can list the microbes found in a stool sample. What nobody can yet do is turn that list into reliable advice about your health or what to eat.[^12]

## What is worth doing

- **Eat a wide variety of plants.** Vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds supply the fiber your microbes ferment, and variety is linked with a more diverse microbiome.[^2][^4]
- **Include fermented foods if you enjoy them.** The trial evidence is early but encouraging.[^5]
- **Take antibiotics when you need them, and only then.**[^6]
- **Skip the test kits and "gut reset" programs.** The science to interpret them does not exist yet.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ongoing digestive symptoms deserve a proper assessment, not a mail-order kit. See a doctor if you have blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, a change in bowel habits lasting more than a few weeks, persistent abdominal pain or bloating, or trouble swallowing.

## The bottom line

Your gut microbes are real partners in digestion and immune regulation, and feeding them well, with plenty of different plants and some fermented foods, is a reasonable bet. Beyond that, stay skeptical. The claims about mood and weight are mostly unproven in people, and home microbiome tests cannot yet tell you anything you can act on.
