"Boosts immunity" may be the most successful three words in the supplement business. They appear on juices, gummies, mushroom powders and teas, usually next to a picture of an orange. The promise is appealing: take this, get sick less.

The trouble starts with the idea itself. This explainer looks at why "boosting" is the wrong way to think about immunity, which habits measurably affect your chances of getting ill, and what trials say about the most popular supplements.

## Why "boost" is the wrong word

Your immune system is not a single thing with a volume dial. It is a network of many cell types and signals that has to respond hard enough to clear an infection and then stand down. Scientists do not know which cells you would want more of, or how many, and so far no product has been shown to enhance immunity in a way that protects healthy people from infection.[^1]

More is not automatically better either. Hay fever is an immune overreaction to pollen, and autoimmune diseases are the immune system attacking the body. What you want is not a boosted system but a normally functioning one. (For the mechanics, see [how your immune system works](/articles/how-your-immune-system-works).)

So the useful question is different: what stops the immune system working as well as it should, and can you fix that?

## What keeps it working

### Sleep

The best evidence here is an experiment, not a survey. Researchers tracked the sleep of 164 healthy adults for a week, then gave them nose drops containing a cold virus and quarantined them. Those who had slept less than six hours a night were more than four times as likely to develop a cold as those who slept more than seven.[^2]

> [!STAT] 4 times
> How much more likely short sleepers (under six hours a night) were to catch a cold than people sleeping more than seven hours, after deliberate exposure to the virus.[^2]

See [how much sleep you need](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need).

### Regular activity

In a study that followed about 1,000 adults through 12 weeks of autumn and winter, those who did aerobic exercise five or more days a week had 43% fewer days with cold symptoms than those who exercised once a week or less.[^3] This was observational, so fitter people may differ in other ways, but it is consistent with the general advice that regular moderate exercise supports immune health.[^1]

### Food, not megadoses

Immune cells need a steady supply of nutrients, and deficiencies in zinc, iron, selenium and vitamins A, B6, C, D and E can all impair immune function.[^5] The remedy for a shortfall is to correct it. There is little evidence that taking extra on top of an adequate diet improves immunity in well-nourished people.[^1][^5]

### Stress, smoking and alcohol

In a classic experiment, 394 healthy volunteers completed stress questionnaires and were then exposed to cold viruses. The more stress they reported, the more likely they were to become infected and to develop a cold.[^4] Not smoking and keeping alcohol moderate are also standard advice for protecting immune function.[^1]

### Vaccines

Vaccination is the one intervention that reliably strengthens immunity, and it does so in the right way: by training a targeted response to a specific germ.[^1] Our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) covers the ones people miss.

## Supplements, one by one

| Supplement | What the best evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Vitamin C | No fewer colds for most people; slightly shorter colds if taken regularly |
| Zinc | May shorten a cold by about two days; low-certainty evidence |
| Vitamin D | Small benefit at best; worth correcting a deficiency |
| Echinacea | No clear benefit for treating colds |
| Elderberry | Preliminary evidence only |

### Vitamin C

A Cochrane review pooled 29 trials with more than 11,000 participants. Taking at least 0.2 g of vitamin C every day did not reduce the number of colds in the general population. It did shorten them a little, by about 8% in adults and 14% in children. Taking it only once symptoms had started showed no consistent benefit.[^6]

One exception: in five trials of people under heavy short-term physical stress, such as marathon runners and soldiers on winter exercises, regular vitamin C roughly halved the risk of a cold.[^6]

### Zinc

A 2024 Cochrane review concluded that zinc probably does little or nothing to prevent colds. Taken once a cold has started, it may shorten the illness by about two days, but the reviewers rated that evidence as low certainty, and side effects such as nausea and an unpleasant taste were more common.[^7]

> [!WARNING]
> Avoid zinc nasal sprays and gels: they have been linked to loss of the sense of smell, in some cases long-lasting. The upper limit for zinc from all sources is 40 mg a day for adults, and long-term high doses can cause copper deficiency.[^8]

### Vitamin D

A 2017 analysis of individual data from about 11,000 trial participants found that vitamin D supplements modestly reduced the risk of respiratory infections, with the clearest benefit in people who were very deficient and took daily or weekly doses.[^9] A larger 2021 update covering more than 40 trials found a smaller overall effect, roughly 8% lower odds, and could not confirm the extra benefit for people with low levels.[^10]

The fair summary: if you are deficient, treat it for the sake of your bones. Do not expect vitamin D to keep colds away, and do not take large doses in the hope that it will.

### Echinacea and elderberry

A Cochrane review of 24 trials found no clear benefit of echinacea for treating colds. Prevention trials hinted at a small effect, but individual studies were not convincing and the products varied widely.[^11]

For elderberry, the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says some preliminary research suggests it may relieve symptoms of flu and other upper respiratory infections, but there is not enough evidence to be confident. Raw or unripe elderberries, and the plant's leaves and stems, contain substances that can cause nausea and vomiting.[^12]

> [!NOTE]
> Supplements are not tested or regulated like medicines, and "supports immune health" on a label is not evidence that a product prevents illness.[^5] If you take regular medication, check with a pharmacist before adding one. ## When to see a doctor

Two or three colds a year is normal for an adult. Frequent colds usually reflect exposure, such as young children at home, not a faulty immune system.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you have infections that are unusually frequent, severe, slow to clear or that keep returning, or if you are losing weight or feel persistently exhausted. These need proper assessment, not a supplement.

## The bottom line

You cannot supercharge a healthy immune system, and you would not want to. You can stop undermining it: sleep at least seven hours, keep active, eat a varied diet, manage stress, do not smoke and keep your vaccines current. Among supplements, zinc and vitamin C may trim a cold slightly; none of them is a substitute for the basics.
