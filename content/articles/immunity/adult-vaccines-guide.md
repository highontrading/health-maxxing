Ask most adults when they last had a vaccine and you will get a shrug, or a story about a school gym. Childhood schedules are well organized. Adult vaccination is not, and it is easy to drift for decades without anyone checking.

That gap matters. The World Health Organization estimates that immunization prevents 3.5 to 5 million deaths a year, and the benefits do not stop at childhood.[^1] This guide covers why adults need vaccines, which ones are commonly recommended, how safety is monitored and how to find out what applies to you.

> [!NOTE]
> Vaccine schedules differ between countries and are revised regularly, and several US recommendations have been under review since 2025. This guide describes long-standing recommendations and names the body behind each one. Always confirm against your own current national schedule, or ask a doctor or pharmacist, before acting on it.[^2][^3][^4]

## Why adults still need vaccines

- **Protection can fade.** Immunity to tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough (pertussis) wanes over the years.
- **New vaccines exist.** Vaccines against HPV, shingles and RSV were not available when many of today's adults were children.
- **Your risks change.** Age weakens [immune responses](/articles/how-your-immune-system-works), and pregnancy, long-term conditions, some jobs and travel all change what you are exposed to and how badly it could affect you.
- **Some viruses keep changing.** Flu vaccines are updated because the viruses evolve, which is why that one is yearly.[^5]

## A closer look

### Every year: flu, and COVID-19 where advised

The WHO recommends yearly flu vaccination for pregnant women, older adults, young children, people with chronic conditions and health workers.[^5] The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has long recommended it for everyone aged 6 months and over, while the NHS offers it free to people aged 65 and over, pregnant women and those in clinical risk groups.[^2][^3] Protection is partial and varies with the season, but vaccination lowers the risk of severe illness.[^5] If you do get ill, see [cold, flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid)

COVID-19 vaccine advice differs widely between countries and has been revised several times. The WHO's priority groups are older adults, people with chronic conditions or weakened immune systems, pregnant women and health workers.[^6] Check what your own program offers each autumn.

### Boosters: tetanus, diphtheria and whooping cough

Here countries diverge. The CDC recommends a tetanus and diphtheria booster every 10 years for adults.[^2] The UK regards the five doses in its childhood program as enough for long-term protection, with extra doses for certain wounds or travel.[^3] Both recommend a whooping cough vaccine in every pregnancy, because the mother's antibodies protect the baby in its first weeks.[^2][^9]

### From midlife: shingles, pneumococcal and RSV

**[Shingles](/conditions/shingles)** is a painful rash caused by the chickenpox virus reawakening, and it becomes more common with age. In the main trial of the current recombinant vaccine, two doses were about 97% effective at preventing shingles in adults aged 50 and over.[^7] The CDC recommends it from age 50, and the NHS is phasing it in from 65; both offer it earlier to people with severely weakened immune systems.[^2][^3]

> [!STAT] About 97%
> Efficacy of the recombinant shingles vaccine against shingles in adults aged 50 and over in its main clinical trial.[^7]

**Pneumococcal** vaccines protect against a bacterium that causes pneumonia, bloodstream infection and meningitis. They are offered to older adults, with the starting age depending on the country, and to younger people with certain medical conditions.[^2][^3]

**RSV** (respiratory syncytial virus) can cause serious chest infections in older adults and in babies. Vaccines became available in 2023. Programs for older adults use different age thresholds, and some countries, including the UK and the US, also offer a vaccine during pregnancy to protect newborns.[^2][^3]

### Catch-up: HPV, hepatitis B and MMR

- **HPV** vaccine prevents the infections that cause most [cervical cancers](/conditions/cervical-cancer) and several other cancers. It works best before exposure to the virus, so programs target adolescents, but the CDC recommends catch-up vaccination through age 26 and says adults aged 27 to 45 can decide with their clinician.[^2]
- **Hepatitis B.** The CDC recommends vaccination for all adults aged 19 to 59 who have not had it, and for older adults with risk factors. Many other countries vaccinate adults based on risk.[^2][^3]
- **MMR** (measles, mumps, rubella). Measles has resurged in many countries as vaccination rates have slipped, and it still kills roughly 100,000 people a year worldwide, mostly young children.[^8] If you are not sure you had two doses or the disease itself, ask about catch-up vaccination.[^2][^3]

## Pregnancy

Vaccines in pregnancy protect two people. Flu and whooping cough vaccines are recommended during pregnancy in many countries, and RSV vaccination is offered in some.[^9] Live vaccines such as MMR are not given during pregnancy, so if you are planning a pregnancy, check your status beforehand.[^9] Advice on COVID-19 vaccination in pregnancy varies by country, so ask your midwife or doctor.

## Travel

Depending on your destination you may need vaccines against hepatitis A, typhoid, yellow fever, rabies or other infections, and some countries require proof of certain vaccinations for entry.[^10] See a travel clinic, doctor or pharmacist at least four to six weeks before you leave, because some vaccines need several doses or time to work.[^10]

## How we know they are safe

Vaccines go through clinical trials before approval, and the scrutiny continues afterward. In the US, the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) collects reports of possible side effects from anyone. A report does not prove that a vaccine caused a problem, but patterns in the reports prompt further investigation.[^11] Other countries run similar systems, such as the UK's Yellow Card scheme.

Most side effects are mild and short-lived: a sore arm, tiredness, headache, muscle aches or a low fever for a day or two. The shingles vaccine is known for causing a rough day or two in some people.[^7]

Serious reactions are rare. An analysis of more than 25 million vaccine doses in the US found 33 cases of anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction, or about 1.3 per million doses.[^12] Clinics are equipped to treat it, which is one reason you may be asked to wait a few minutes after an injection.

## How to check what you need

> [!TIP] A five-minute audit
> Find your records, or ask your family doctor what is on file. Then look up your country's adult schedule, or take the question to a pharmacist. Mention your age, any pregnancy plans, long-term conditions, medicines that affect your immune system, your job and upcoming travel.

In some countries, recommendations from different bodies do not always match. If the advice you hear seems inconsistent, ask your clinician which schedule they follow and why. A routine check-up is a good moment to raise it; see [health screenings by age](/articles/health-screenings-by-age).

## When to seek medical advice

> [!DOCTOR]
> Get emergency help for signs of a severe allergic reaction after a vaccine: difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, a fast heartbeat, widespread hives, or dizziness and weakness. Speak to a doctor before vaccination if you are pregnant, have a weakened immune system or have had a serious reaction to a previous dose, since some vaccines may not be suitable.

## The bottom line

Adult vaccination is easy to forget and simple to fix. Check your records, look up your national schedule and ask about flu, tetanus-containing boosters, shingles, pneumococcal and RSV vaccines as you get older, plus any childhood vaccines you missed. Add pregnancy and travel vaccines when they apply.
