For a condition that sounds mild, hay fever does a lot of damage: weeks of sneezing, a blocked nose, itchy eyes, broken sleep and a foggy head at work or school. It is also extremely common. In 2021, about one in four US adults reported having a seasonal allergy.[^2]

Most people manage it by grabbing a tablet when symptoms get bad. That works for some, but it is not what the evidence supports for anything beyond mild symptoms. This guide ranks the treatments, shows how to use them properly and explains when to get more help.

> [!STAT] 1 in 4
> US adults who had a seasonal allergy in 2021, according to national survey data.[^2]

## What hay fever is

Hay fever, or seasonal [allergic rhinitis](/conditions/allergic-rhinitis), is an immune overreaction. Your [immune system](/articles/how-your-immune-system-works) mistakes harmless pollen or mold spores for a threat and releases histamine and other chemicals that inflame the lining of your nose and eyes.[^3] The result is sneezing, a runny or blocked nose, itchy red watery eyes, an itchy throat and often tiredness.[^1]

Timing depends on what you react to. In temperate climates, tree pollen peaks in spring, grass pollen in late spring and summer, and weed pollen such as ragweed in late summer and autumn.[^3] Seasons are also shifting: an analysis of North American pollen records from 1990 to 2018 found that seasons now start about 20 days earlier and carry about 21% more pollen, with climate change a major driver.[^4]

## Allergy or a cold?

| Clue | Allergy | Cold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Itchy eyes, nose or throat | Common | Rare |
| Fever or aches | Never | Sometimes |
| Mucus | Clear and watery | Often thicker |
| Duration | Weeks, while the pollen lasts | About 7 to 10 days |
| Pattern | Same time every year | Any time, often winter |

The comparison follows US National Institutes of Health guidance.[^5] If you are unsure, see [cold, flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid)

## Treatments, ranked

| Treatment | Best for | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steroid nasal spray | All nasal symptoms, including blockage | Most effective single option; needs daily use |
| Non-sedating antihistamine tablet | Sneezing, itching, runny nose | Works within about an hour; less help for blockage |
| Antihistamine nasal spray | Fast relief, or added to a steroid spray | Can taste bitter |
| Antihistamine eye drops | Itchy, watery eyes | Use alongside other treatments |
| Saline rinse | Extra relief from congestion | Only with safe water |

**Steroid nasal sprays come first.** US ear, nose and throat specialists strongly recommend them for anyone whose symptoms affect their quality of life.[^6] Allergy specialists go further: for moderate to severe seasonal allergies in people aged 12 and over, they recommend starting with a steroid nasal spray alone in preference to adding an antihistamine tablet, because the tablet adds little.[^7] Examples include fluticasone, mometasone and budesonide, and several are sold without a prescription.

**Non-sedating antihistamines** such as cetirizine, loratadine and fexofenadine are recommended when sneezing and itching are the main complaints.[^6] They act quickly and are a good choice for mild or occasional symptoms.

**For stubborn symptoms**, guidelines support combining a steroid spray with an antihistamine nasal spray.[^7]

**Decongestant sprays** clear a blocked nose fast but should only be used for a few days, because longer use causes rebound congestion.[^6]

Use all of these as directed on the label, and check with a pharmacist if you are pregnant, buying for a child or taking other medicines.

## How to use a nasal spray properly

Poor technique is a common reason sprays seem not to work.

1. Gently blow your nose, then shake the bottle.
2. Tilt your head slightly forward, not back.
3. Close one nostril, put the nozzle just inside the other and point it slightly away from the middle of your nose.
4. Spray while breathing in slowly and gently. Do not sniff hard, or the medicine ends up in your throat.
5. Repeat on the other side.

These steps follow NHS instructions.[^8]

> [!TIP] Start before the season
> Steroid sprays take a few days to reach full effect. The NHS suggests starting one to two weeks before your symptoms usually begin and using the spray every day, not only on bad days.[^8]

## Skip the older, sedating antihistamines

First-generation antihistamines, including diphenhydramine and chlorphenamine (chlorpheniramine), are still widely sold for allergies. Guidelines favor the newer, less sedating ones because the older drugs cause drowsiness and can impair driving, work and school performance.[^6] They also have side effects such as a dry mouth and, in older adults, confusion.[^11]

> [!WARNING]
> A sedating antihistamine can slow your reactions even if you do not feel sleepy. Do not drive or operate machinery after taking one, and avoid combining it with alcohol.[^6]

One more medicine deserves caution. Montelukast, a tablet sometimes prescribed for allergies, carries a US Food and Drug Administration warning about mood and behavior changes and is now reserved for people who cannot use other treatments.[^11]

## Saline rinses: helpful, with one firm rule

Rinsing the nose with salt water is cheap and drug-free. A Cochrane review found it may reduce symptom severity, although the evidence was low quality.[^9]

The firm rule is about water. Use distilled or sterile water, or tap water that has been boiled and cooled. Tap water that is safe to drink can still carry organisms that cause rare but dangerous infections when flushed into the nasal passages.[^10] Wash the device and let it air-dry after each use.[^10]

## Keeping pollen off you

Avoidance will not cure hay fever, but it lowers the dose. The NHS suggests:[^1]

- keeping windows and doors shut when the pollen count is high
- showering and changing your clothes after being outdoors
- wearing wraparound sunglasses
- putting a little petroleum jelly around your nostrils to trap pollen
- not drying clothes outside, and not cutting or walking on grass

Pollen counts tend to be highest on warm, dry, windy days, so check the forecast when planning time outdoors.[^3]

## When medicines are not enough

If you have used a steroid spray correctly and consistently and still struggle, ask about allergen immunotherapy. It retrains the immune system with regular, controlled doses of the allergen, given as injections or as tablets that dissolve under the tongue, usually for several years. Guidelines recommend offering it to people who do not respond adequately to medication, and it is the one treatment that acts on the underlying allergy instead of the symptoms.[^6]

## Hay fever and asthma

The nose and lungs share one airway, and allergic rhinitis and [asthma](/conditions/asthma) often occur together. Guidelines tell doctors to check for asthma in people with allergic rhinitis.[^6] Pollen can also trigger asthma attacks.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if your symptoms are not controlled by pharmacy treatments or are disrupting sleep, work or school.[^1] Get medical advice promptly for wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath or a persistent cough in pollen season. Call your local emergency number if someone is struggling to breathe.

## The bottom line

If hay fever bothers you most days in season, make a daily steroid nasal spray your foundation, start it early and use good technique. Add a non-sedating antihistamine or eye drops as needed, rinse only with safe water and leave the sedating antihistamines on the shelf. If that is not enough, or if your chest is involved, see a doctor and ask about immunotherapy.
