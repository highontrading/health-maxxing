Antibiotics are among the most important medicines ever developed. They turned pneumonia, infected wounds and childbirth fever from common killers into treatable problems, and they make surgery, chemotherapy and organ transplants far safer.

They are also among the most misused. Many of us have asked for them for a cough that would have gone on its own, or kept a few tablets "for next time". This explainer covers what antibiotics can and cannot do, which common illnesses do not need them, the real risks of taking them, and how to use them well.

## What antibiotics do, and what they cannot

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. That makes them essential for bacterial infections such as strep throat, urinary tract infections, bacterial pneumonia and sepsis.[^1]

They have no effect on viruses. Colds, flu, COVID-19, most sore throats and most coughs are viral, so an antibiotic will not help you feel better or recover faster.[^1][^2]

## Illnesses that usually do not need them

| Illness | Usual cause | Antibiotics? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cold or runny nose | Virus | No |
| Flu | Virus | No |
| COVID-19 | Virus | No |
| Most sore throats | Virus | No |
| Strep throat | Bacteria | Yes, after a positive test |
| Chest cold (acute bronchitis) | Usually a virus | No |
| Sinus infection | Virus or bacteria | Sometimes |
| Middle ear infection | Virus or bacteria | Sometimes |
| Urinary tract infection | Bacteria | Yes |

This summary follows guidance from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).[^1] Two points surprise people. A chest cold can leave you coughing for up to three weeks and still not need antibiotics. And yellow or green mucus does not mean the infection is bacterial.[^1]

For sinus and ear infections, many of which clear up by themselves, doctors often recommend watchful waiting, or a back-up prescription to fill only if things have not improved after a few days.[^1]

Despite this, overprescribing is common. A study of US clinic and emergency department visits estimated that about 30% of antibiotic prescriptions given outside hospitals were unnecessary, most of them for respiratory illnesses caused by viruses.[^3]

## Why an unnecessary antibiotic is not harmless

### The risk to you

Any antibiotic can cause side effects. The common ones are rash, nausea, diarrhea and yeast infections. Less often they cause severe allergic reactions.[^1]

Antibiotics also disturb the helpful bacteria in your gut. That can allow *Clostridioides difficile* (C. diff) to take over, causing severe diarrhea that can be life-threatening. According to the CDC, people are 7 to 10 times more likely to get C. diff while taking antibiotics and during the month afterward.[^4]

The disruption can linger. When 12 healthy men took a four-day course of three strong antibiotics, their gut bacteria returned to near baseline in about six weeks, but nine common species were still undetectable in most of them six months later.[^5] More in [your gut microbiome, explained](/articles/gut-microbiome-explained), and see our [probiotics guide](/articles/probiotics-prebiotics-fermented-foods) for what the trials say about preventing antibiotic-associated diarrhea.

If the antibiotic was needed, these risks are well worth taking. If it was not, they are all downside.

> [!NOTE]
> Think you are allergic to penicillin? About 10% of people in the US carry that label, but when tested, the great majority turn out to tolerate penicillins.[^6] Being wrongly labeled can mean getting less suitable antibiotics, so ask your doctor whether testing makes sense.

### The risk to everyone

Each time bacteria are exposed to an antibiotic, the ones that happen to survive can multiply and pass on their resistance. The World Health Organization names the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials as the main driver of resistance, which it ranks among the top global public health threats.[^7]

> [!STAT] 1.27 million
> Deaths worldwide directly caused by antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections in 2019. Resistant infections played a part in an estimated 4.95 million deaths.[^8]

The outlook is sobering. An updated analysis forecast that resistant infections could directly cause more than 39 million deaths between 2025 and 2050 if nothing changes.[^9] Resistance does not mean your body becomes resistant. It means the bacteria do, and resistant bacteria spread between people.

## How to take antibiotics properly

- **Take them exactly as prescribed:** the right dose, at the right times, for the length of time your prescriber said.[^1][^2]
- **Never share them or use someone else's.** The drug, dose and duration are chosen for a specific infection.[^1]
- **Do not save leftovers.** Ask a pharmacist how to dispose of unused medicine safely.[^1]
- **Report side effects.** Contact your doctor or pharmacist if you develop a rash, severe diarrhea or other problems.[^1]
- **Ask about interactions** with alcohol or your other medicines; they vary by antibiotic.[^2]

## "Finish the course": what has changed

For decades, patients were told to finish every tablet or risk breeding resistance. The reasoning has been challenged. In 2017 a group of infection specialists argued in the BMJ that the idea is not supported by evidence, and that taking antibiotics for longer than necessary is what increases the risk of resistance.[^10]

Trials have since shown that for many common infections, shorter courses work as well as traditional long ones. The American College of Physicians advised in 2021 that, for example, as little as five days can be enough for community-acquired pneumonia, and that short courses are appropriate for uncomplicated urinary tract infections and skin infections.[^11]

That does not mean you should stop when you feel better. Some infections do need long treatment, and stopping too soon can let them return. The NHS still tells patients to finish the prescribed course unless a healthcare professional says otherwise.[^2] The practical version of the new thinking is this: your prescriber should choose the shortest course that works, and you should take all of it. If you feel well and want to stop early, ask first.

> [!TIP] Three questions to ask
> "Is this likely to be bacterial?" "What happens if we wait a couple of days?" "What is the shortest course that will work?" Doctors generally welcome these questions, and many patients are relieved to learn they do not need a prescription.

## What you can do about resistance

- Do not press for antibiotics when your doctor says you do not need them. Ask instead what will relieve your symptoms.[^1]
- Prevent infections in the first place: wash your hands, handle food safely and keep your [vaccinations](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) up to date.[^7]
- For colds and flu, use the self-care in [cold, flu or COVID?](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid)

## When to seek help

Being cautious with antibiotics does not mean toughing out a serious infection. Bacterial infections can worsen fast, and prompt treatment saves lives.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Get urgent medical help for a high fever with shaking chills, confusion, fast breathing, severe pain, a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when pressed, or if you feel very unwell and are getting worse. While taking antibiotics, seek help straight away for swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing or widespread hives, and contact your doctor about severe or bloody diarrhea, even weeks after the course ends.[^4]

## The bottom line

Antibiotics are powerful against bacteria and useless against viruses. Do not ask for them for colds, flu or most coughs and sore throats. When you do need them, take them exactly as prescribed, never share or save them, and ask your prescriber about the shortest effective course. Using them carefully protects you now and keeps them working for the day you really need them.
