Few numbers cause as much household anxiety as the one on a thermometer. A reading of 39 °C at two in the morning feels like an alarm. Most of the time it is closer to a status report: your immune system has noticed an infection and is responding.

That does not mean fever never matters. In a few situations, especially in very young babies, it needs fast medical attention. This guide explains what counts as a fever, how to measure it, how to look after it at home and how to recognize the cases that cannot wait.

## What counts as a fever

Most health bodies define a fever as a temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher.[^2][^7]

> [!STAT] 38 °C
> The usual threshold for a fever, equal to 100.4 °F.[^2]

"Normal" is a range, not a single number. Body temperature varies from person to person and is usually around 37 °C (98.6 °F).[^1] It tends to be lower in the morning and higher in the late afternoon and evening, and readings differ depending on where you measure.

A fever is a symptom, not a disease. Infections such as [colds, flu and COVID-19](/articles/cold-flu-or-covid) cause most fevers, but they can also follow some vaccines and medicines, and occur with heat illness, autoimmune conditions and some cancers.[^1]

## Why your body turns up the heat

Fever is deliberate. When immune cells detect an infection, they release chemical signals that tell the brain's thermostat, the hypothalamus, to raise its set point. You shiver and feel cold as your body works to reach the new target, then sweat when the fever breaks.[^3]

The heat appears to help. Many bacteria and viruses do less well at higher temperatures, and research shows that fever-range temperatures make several kinds of immune cells work more effectively.[^1][^3] More in [how your immune system works](/articles/how-your-immune-system-works).

This is why the number alone is a poor guide to how sick someone is. How the person looks and behaves usually tells you more, with one big exception: young babies, covered below.

## How to take a temperature

Use a digital thermometer. Feeling a forehead with your hand is unreliable. Advice on the best method differs a little between countries.

| Age | Recommended methods |
| --- | --- |
| Under 3 months | Rectal is most accurate (American Academy of Pediatrics). UK guidance uses an electronic thermometer in the armpit. |
| 3 months to about 4 years | Rectal, forehead or armpit, with ear thermometers from 6 months (AAP). UK guidance: armpit or ear. |
| Older children and adults | Mouth, ear or forehead. |

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) regards rectal readings as the most accurate for babies and toddlers and armpit readings as the least reliable.[^4] The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises against routinely using the mouth or rectum in children under 5.[^5] Follow the approach used where you live and the instructions that come with your thermometer.

> [!TIP] Getting a reliable reading
> For an oral reading, wait a while after eating or drinking anything hot or cold. Use the same method each time so that you are comparing like with like, and tell the doctor which method you used.

## Looking after a fever at home

The aim is comfort. The AAP says the main goal of treating a child's fever should be to improve the child's overall comfort, not to bring the temperature back to normal, and notes there is no evidence that fever itself makes an illness worse or causes lasting neurological harm.[^6] The same logic applies to adults.

**What helps**

- Drink plenty of fluids to avoid dehydration. Pale urine is a good sign.[^2]
- Rest, and stay home until the fever has gone.[^2]
- Dress lightly and keep the room comfortable. Do not bundle up, and do not strip a child down either.[^7]
- If you or your child feel uncomfortable or distressed, paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen can help. Use them as directed on the label, and for children go by the product's age and weight instructions.[^2][^7]

**What to avoid**

- **Cold baths, ice or sponging.** NICE does not recommend tepid sponging for children with fever.[^5]
- **Doubling up.** UK guidance says not to give a child paracetamol and ibuprofen at the same time unless a clinician advises it.[^5][^7]
- **Ibuprofen for young babies.** The NHS says not to give it to babies under 3 months or weighing less than 5 kg, and US pediatric guidance sets the limit at 6 months. Ask a pharmacist or doctor if unsure.[^6][^7]
- **Waking a sleeping child to give medicine.** Sleep is more useful than a lower number.[^6]

> [!WARNING]
> Do not give aspirin to children or teenagers. It has been linked to Reye syndrome, a rare but serious condition affecting the brain and liver, especially during viral illnesses such as flu and chickenpox.[^8]

Fever medicines ease discomfort. They do not treat the cause or shorten the illness, so judge progress by how the person is doing overall.

## Febrile seizures

Some young children have a seizure (fit) when they have a fever. Febrile seizures affect about 2 to 5% of children, usually between 6 months and 5 years of age.[^9] They are frightening to watch, but most last only a few minutes and do not cause lasting harm.[^9]

If it happens, lay the child on their side on a soft surface, do not put anything in their mouth and note the time.[^10] Call your local emergency number if the seizure lasts longer than five minutes, if it is the child's first, or if they have trouble breathing or do not recover quickly.[^10] Fever-reducing medicines do not prevent febrile seizures.[^5]

## When to worry

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department if a fever comes with a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it, pain from bright light, confusion or unusual drowsiness, difficulty breathing, blue, pale or blotchy skin, or a seizure.[^7][^11]

**Babies and children.** Get urgent medical advice if:[^5][^7]

- a baby under 3 months has a temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher
- a baby aged 3 to 6 months has a temperature of 39 °C (102.2 °F) or higher
- the fever has lasted five days or more
- your child shows signs of dehydration, such as few wet diapers, no tears or sunken eyes
- your child is not feeding, is not their usual self, or you are worried

**Adults.** See a doctor promptly if:[^1][^2][^12]

- the fever lasts more than about three days or keeps returning
- you have severe headache, chest pain, persistent vomiting, pain when urinating or a new rash
- you have recently traveled abroad, particularly to a region with malaria, where symptoms can appear weeks or months after you return
- you are pregnant, are having chemotherapy, have a weakened immune system or have a serious long-term condition

One more situation needs fast action. A very high body temperature after heat exposure or hard exertion, with confusion or collapse, suggests heatstroke. Call for emergency help and start cooling the person straight away; our [first aid basics](/articles/first-aid-basics) covers what to do.

For help deciding how urgent something is, see [emergency or not?](/articles/emergency-or-not)

## The bottom line

Treat the person, not the thermometer. For most adults and older children, rest, fluids and a fever medicine if they feel rough is all a fever needs. Act fast for any fever in a baby under 3 months, and for fever with a stiff neck, a non-fading rash, confusion, breathing trouble or a seizure. And see a doctor if a fever lingers beyond a few days or follows travel abroad.
