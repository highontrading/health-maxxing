Right now, without any effort on your part, your body is checking billions of cells for signs of trouble, patrolling your blood and tissues, and filing away the identity of every germ it has ever beaten. You only notice the immune system when it is working hard, as with a fever or a swollen gland, or when it gets something wrong.

This is a tour of how it works: the layers of defense, the main cell types, why inflammation and fever are features, how vaccines use immune memory, and what weakens or misdirects the whole thing.

## Layer one: barriers

The best infection is the one that never gets in. Your skin is a tough physical wall. The linings of your nose, airways and gut are coated in mucus that traps microbes, and stomach acid destroys many of the ones you swallow.[^1] The harmless microbes that live on and in you help too, by taking up space and resources that invaders would need.[^11]

## Layer two: the innate response

When something does get through, the innate immune system responds within minutes to hours. It is the system you are born with, and it reacts in the same general way to anything that looks foreign.[^1]

Its main players are white blood cells:[^1]

- **Neutrophils**, the most numerous, rush to the site and engulf invaders.
- **Macrophages** swallow microbes and debris and send out chemical alarm signals.
- **Natural killer cells** destroy your own cells when they are infected by viruses or turning cancerous.
- **Dendritic cells** collect fragments of the invader and carry them to the lymph nodes, where they brief the next layer of defense.

### Inflammation and fever are tools

The redness, heat, swelling and pain around a cut are not the infection itself. They are your response to it. Blood vessels widen and become leaky so that immune cells and proteins can flood the area.[^1]

Fever works on the same principle at whole-body scale. Immune signals prompt the brain to raise its temperature set point, and research suggests the higher temperature helps immune cells perform better.[^3] Fever is uncomfortable, and at the extremes or in vulnerable people it needs attention, but in itself it is a sign of the system working. See [fever: what it means and when to worry](/articles/fever-when-to-worry).

> [!NOTE]
> Short-lived inflammation heals. An immune response that is too strong, or aimed at the wrong target, causes harm of its own, as in allergies and autoimmune diseases. That is why "more immune activity" is not automatically better.[^2]

## Layer three: the adaptive response

The innate system is fast but general. The adaptive system is the opposite: slow to start, but precise, and it learns.[^1]

Its key cells are lymphocytes:[^1]

- **B cells** make antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that lock on to one specific target (an antigen). Antibodies can neutralize a germ directly or tag it for destruction.
- **Helper T cells** coordinate the response and help B cells make better antibodies.
- **Killer T cells** find and destroy your own cells that have been infected.

Each B and T cell recognizes only one target. When a new germ arrives, the few cells that happen to match it multiply into an army. That takes days, which is why you can feel ill for a while before turning the corner.[^1][^4] Much of this happens in your lymph nodes, the small bean-shaped glands in your neck, armpits and groin, which is why they can swell and feel tender during an infection.[^1]

| | Innate immunity | Adaptive immunity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Minutes to hours | Days on first exposure |
| Target | Anything that looks foreign | One specific germ |
| Memory | Little | Long-lasting |
| Key cells | Neutrophils, macrophages, natural killer cells | B cells, T cells |

## Memory, and how vaccines borrow it

After an infection clears, most of the army stands down, but some B and T cells remain as **memory cells**. If the same germ returns, they respond faster and more forcefully, often before you notice any symptoms.[^1]

Vaccines exploit this. A vaccine contains a weakened or inactivated germ, a piece of one, or instructions that let your cells make a harmless piece. Your immune system responds and builds memory without your having to survive the disease first.[^4]

The results are among the largest in medicine. The World Health Organization estimates that immunization prevents 3.5 to 5 million deaths every year.[^5] A 2024 modeling study estimated that vaccines against 14 diseases have saved about 154 million lives over the past 50 years, most of them children under five.[^6]

> [!STAT] 154 million
> Estimated lives saved by vaccination worldwide over the past 50 years.[^6]

Memory varies by germ. Some vaccines and infections protect for decades, while others need boosters because the memory fades or the germ changes. Our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) covers which ones adults tend to miss.

## When the system misfires

**Allergy.** The immune system treats something harmless, such as pollen or peanut, as a threat. See [seasonal allergies](/articles/seasonal-allergies-relief).

**Autoimmune disease.** The system mistakes part of your own body for an invader. There are more than 80 autoimmune diseases, including type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and multiple sclerosis.[^7] In a UK study of 22 million people, about one in ten had at least one of 19 common autoimmune conditions, and they were more common in women.[^8]

**Immune deficiency.** Defenses can be weakened by rare inherited conditions or, far more often, by something acquired: HIV, cancer treatment, medicines that suppress immunity, or severe malnutrition.[^2]

## What shapes immunity across life

- **Age.** The immune system matures through childhood and becomes less responsive in later life, so older adults get more infections and respond less strongly to vaccines.[^9]
- **Sleep.** In one study, healthy volunteers were given cold virus nose drops after a week of sleep tracking. Those sleeping less than six hours a night were more than four times as likely to catch the cold as those sleeping more than seven.[^10]
- **Nutrition, activity and stress.** Shortages of nutrients such as zinc, iron and vitamins A, B6, C and E can impair immune function, long-term stress appears to dampen it, and regular exercise supports it.[^9]
- **Smoking and heavy drinking** both work against it.[^9]
- **Your gut.** The gut is one of the body's main immune sites, and the microbes living there help train and calibrate the immune system from infancy onward.[^11] More in [your gut microbiome, explained](/articles/gut-microbiome-explained).

None of these is a way to turbocharge immunity. They are the conditions under which it works normally, which is the subject of [can you really boost your immune system?](/articles/can-you-boost-your-immune-system)

## When to see a doctor

A few colds a year is normal immune function, not a weak system.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you get infections that are unusually frequent, severe or slow to clear, or that keep coming back. If you are having chemotherapy or take medicines that suppress your immune system, treat any fever as urgent and contact your care team straight away.

## The bottom line

Your immune system is layered: barriers, a rapid general response, and a slower, specific one with a long memory. Fever and inflammation are usually signs it is doing its job. The most reliable ways to help it are unexciting: stay up to date with vaccines, sleep enough, eat well, keep active and do not smoke.
