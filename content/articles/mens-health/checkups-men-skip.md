Compared with women, men are more likely to smoke and drink, and more likely to put off regular checkups and medical care. That is how the US National Library of Medicine sums it up.[^1] The trouble is that the conditions most likely to shorten a man's life, such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer, stay silent for years and are easy to find with simple tests.

This list goes decade by decade through the checks worth having, with the guideline behind each one. Most come from the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), an independent expert panel. Other countries differ on the details, so treat the ages as a guide and look up your own national program.

## The list at a glance

| Check | When | Who says so |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | From 18; every year from 40 | USPSTF |
| Cholesterol | Every 5 years when younger; every 1 to 2 years from 45 | MedlinePlus |
| HIV and hepatitis C | At least once | USPSTF |
| Diabetes | 35 to 70 if overweight | USPSTF |
| Colorectal cancer | 45 to 75 | USPSTF, American Cancer Society |
| Prostate (PSA) discussion | From 50, or 45 if higher risk | American Cancer Society |
| Lung cancer scan | 50 to 80 with a heavy smoking history | USPSTF |
| Aortic aneurysm scan | Once between 65 and 75 if you ever smoked | USPSTF, NHS |

## In your 20s and 30s

1. **Blood pressure.** High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, so the only way to know is to measure it. The USPSTF recommends screening every adult from 18: every three to five years if your readings are normal and your risk is low, and every year from 40, or sooner if you are at higher risk.[^2] Our guide to [blood pressure numbers](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) explains the results.
2. **Cholesterol.** MedlinePlus advises a test every five years for younger adults and every one to two years for men aged 45 to 65.[^11]
3. **HIV and hepatitis C.** The USPSTF recommends at least one HIV test for everyone aged 15 to 65 and one hepatitis C test for adults aged 18 to 79, with repeat testing if you have ongoing risk.[^3] Ask about tests for other sexually transmitted infections based on your sexual history.
4. **Testicular awareness.** Testicular cancer is the most common cancer in men aged 15 to 34, and it is highly treatable. The USPSTF does not recommend routine screening examinations, partly because most cases are found by men themselves or their partners.[^7] So know what is normal for you, and report any lump, swelling or heaviness promptly.
5. **Mood.** The USPSTF recommends that doctors screen adults for depression, and adults under 65 for anxiety.[^3] If nobody asks, raise it yourself. Our piece on [men's mental health](/articles/mens-mental-health) may help you start.
6. **Vaccines.** The US adult schedule includes a flu vaccine every year, a tetanus booster every 10 years, and the HPV vaccine through age 26 if you missed it.[^12]
7. **Skin, teeth and eyes.** Look over your skin every few months and show a doctor any mole that is new, changing or unlike the others. Keep up dental checks at the interval your dentist advises, and have your eyes tested if your vision changes. See our guides to [mole checks](/articles/skin-cancer-and-mole-checks) and [oral health](/articles/oral-health-basics).

## In your 40s

8. **Blood sugar.** The USPSTF recommends screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes in adults aged 35 to 70 who are overweight or have obesity.[^3]
9. **Heart risk.** From 40, guidelines shift from single numbers to your overall 10-year risk of heart attack and stroke, which combines age, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking and diabetes. The USPSTF uses it to decide who should be offered a statin.[^3] In England, the NHS Health Check offers this assessment every five years to adults aged 40 to 74.[^8] See [know your numbers](/articles/know-your-numbers).
10. **Colorectal cancer screening, from 45.** The USPSTF and the American Cancer Society both recommend starting at 45.[^4][^10] You have a choice of tests, including a stool test every year or a colonoscopy every 10 years.[^4] Ask about starting earlier if a close relative has had bowel cancer.
11. **The prostate conversation, if you are at higher risk.** The American Cancer Society suggests discussing PSA testing at 45 if you are Black or have a father or brother who was diagnosed with prostate cancer before 65.[^10]

> [!STAT] 45
> The age at which US guidelines now recommend starting colorectal cancer screening for people at average risk. Screening continues to 75.[^4]

## In your 50s

12. **The prostate decision.** For men at average risk, the American Cancer Society suggests having the discussion at 50.[^10] The USPSTF describes PSA screening between 55 and 69 as an individual decision, because the benefits and harms are closely balanced.[^5] Our [prostate guide](/articles/prostate-health-guide) lays out the trade-offs.
13. **Lung cancer screening, if you have smoked heavily.** The USPSTF recommends a yearly low-dose CT scan for adults aged 50 to 80 who have a 20 pack-year history and either still smoke or quit within the past 15 years.[^3] A pack-year is a pack a day for a year.
14. **Shingles and pneumococcal vaccines.** In the US, both are recommended from 50.[^12] Other countries start later, so check your own schedule in our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide).

## In your 60s and beyond

15. **An aortic aneurysm scan.** An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the body's main artery that gives no warning before it ruptures. The USPSTF recommends a one-time ultrasound for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked.[^6] The NHS invites all men for the scan in the year they turn 65.[^9]
16. **Everything above, still.** Blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar checks continue, colorectal screening runs to 75, and the vaccine list grows.[^4][^12]
17. **Knowing when to stop.** Screening is not for ever. The USPSTF recommends against PSA screening from 70, and advises a case-by-case decision on colorectal screening from 76 to 85.[^4][^5]

> [!TIP] Make it one appointment
> Many of these checks can be done in a single visit with one blood sample. Book it for the same month every year, such as your birthday month, and it stops being a decision.

## Do not wait for a checkup if

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number for chest pain or pressure, sudden breathlessness, or sudden weakness, numbness or trouble speaking. See a doctor soon about blood in your stool or urine, a lasting change in bowel habits, a lump in a testicle, a mole that is changing, unexplained weight loss, or erection problems, which can be an early sign of artery disease. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line or emergency services straight away.

## The bottom line

In your 20s and 30s, know your blood pressure and cholesterol, get tested once for HIV and hepatitis C, and keep your vaccines current. From 40, add blood sugar and heart-risk checks. From 45, start colorectal screening. From 50, have the prostate conversation, and at 65 have an aneurysm scan if you ever smoked. None of it takes long, and most of it finds problems while they are still easy to fix. For the wider picture, see [health screenings by age](/articles/health-screenings-by-age).
