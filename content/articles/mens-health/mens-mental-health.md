Here is a puzzle. By the World Health Organization's count, depression is about 50% more common among women than among men.[^3] Yet men die by suicide at roughly twice the rate of women.[^2] Put together, those two facts suggest we are missing something about how men are doing.

The likeliest explanation is not that men suffer less. It is that their distress is harder to see, less often named and less often treated. This piece looks at how that happens, what distress can look like in men, what helps, and how to start a conversation with a friend or about yourself.

> [!DOCTOR] If you need help now
> If you are thinking about harming yourself, or you are worried that someone else is in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^11]

## The numbers behind the silence

More than 700,000 people die by suicide every year, according to the World Health Organization.[^1] In the Global Burden of Disease study, the rate among men was more than double the rate among women: 15.6 versus 7.0 deaths per 100,000 people in 2016.[^2]

> [!STAT] About 2x
> Men's suicide rate compared with women's, worldwide.[^2]

Men are also less likely to be in treatment. The US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) notes that men are less likely than women to have received mental health care in the past year.[^4]

Part of the gap may be a measurement problem. Researchers reanalyzed a large US mental health survey using a broader definition of depression, one that counted anger attacks, aggression, substance misuse and risk-taking alongside the classic symptoms. The difference between the sexes disappeared: 30.6% of men and 33.3% of women met the criteria.[^5] That is one study, and the broader scale is not a standard diagnostic tool. But it suggests that the textbook picture of depression misses some men.

## What distress can look like

Sadness and tearfulness are the symptoms most people expect. NIMH lists others that are common in men:[^4]

- anger, irritability or aggressiveness
- noticeable changes in mood, energy or appetite
- trouble sleeping, or sleeping too much
- difficulty concentrating, or feeling restless and on edge
- misuse of alcohol or drugs
- feeling flat, or struggling to feel anything positive
- taking part in high-risk activities
- aches, headaches or digestive problems with no clear cause
- thoughts or behavior that get in the way of work, family or social life

No single item proves anything. The signal is change: a friend who has become short-tempered, is drinking more, has stopped turning up, or is working every hour to avoid going home.

Some stages of life carry extra risk. New fatherhood is one. A meta-analysis of 43 studies found that about 1 in 10 fathers experience depression during their partner's pregnancy or in the first year after the birth, and that the risk is higher when the mother is depressed as well.[^12]

## Why men hold back

A systematic review found that the more strongly men hold to traditional masculine norms, such as self-reliance and keeping emotions in check, the more those norms shape three things: how depression shows itself, whether they seek help, and how they try to manage it.[^6] In practice that can mean not recognizing low mood as a health problem, seeing help as weakness, and coping alone through work or alcohol.

None of this is fixed. Men do engage with treatment when it feels accessible and relevant to them, and services can be designed with that in mind.[^7]

## What helps

The standard treatments for [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment) and anxiety, which are talking therapies and medication, work for men. The hard part is getting through the door, and a few things make that easier.

- **Therapy that fits.** A review of how to engage men in psychological treatment recommends approaches that are collaborative, clearly structured and focused on goals and practical skills, with a plain explanation of what therapy involves.[^7] If the first therapist is not a good fit, trying another is normal.
- **Exercise.** A 2024 analysis of 218 trials found that exercise, especially walking or jogging, yoga and strength training, reduced symptoms of depression to a moderate degree, although many of the trials were of low quality. The authors suggest that exercise be considered alongside therapy and medication.[^8] Our guide to [exercise for mental health](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health) covers how much and what kind.
- **Connection with a purpose.** Many men find it easier to talk side by side than face to face: on a walk, in a car, at a workbench. Sports teams, volunteering and community workshop groups such as Men's Sheds build that in. Rigorous trials of such groups are scarce, but they fit what is known about how men prefer to engage.[^7] See also our piece on [loneliness and social connection](/articles/loneliness-and-social-connection).
- **Less alcohol.** Drinking more is on NIMH's list of warning signs, and it is a common way of coping that makes sleep and mood worse.[^4]
- **Your regular doctor.** A physical complaint such as poor sleep or constant tiredness is a perfectly good way to open the conversation.

## How to check in on a friend

1. **Pick the moment.** Somewhere private and unhurried, ideally while you are doing something else.
2. **Say what you have noticed.** "You have not seemed yourself lately" works better than "What's wrong with you?"
3. **Ask twice.** "I'm fine" is a reflex. "Are you sure? You'd tell me?" often gets a truer answer.
4. **Listen more than you fix.** You do not need solutions. Being heard is the point.
5. **Offer a next step, and help with it.** Look up the doctor's number together, or offer to go along.
6. **Follow up.** A message a few days later shows that you meant it.

> [!TIP] You do not need the right words
> Most people remember that someone asked, not how elegantly they did it. A clumsy question from a friend beats a perfect one that never gets asked.

## If you are worried about suicide

Ask directly. It can feel dangerous to say the word, but a review of the research found no evidence that asking about suicidal thoughts increases them. If anything, talking openly may reduce distress.[^9]

NIMH recommends five steps:[^10]

1. **Ask:** "Are you thinking about suicide?"
2. **Be there:** listen without judgment.
3. **Help keep them safe:** ask whether they have thought about how, and help put distance between them and anything they could use to harm themselves.
4. **Help them connect:** with a crisis line, a doctor or a trusted relative.
5. **Follow up:** stay in touch afterwards.

Get urgent help if someone talks about wanting to die or being a burden, is looking for ways to end their life, is saying goodbye or giving away possessions, or has suddenly withdrawn.[^10] Stay with them, and contact your local emergency number or a crisis line. Find A Helpline lists services in most countries.[^11]

## The bottom line

Men's distress often wears a disguise: irritability, overwork, drinking, withdrawal. If you see it in yourself, treat it like any other health problem and tell a doctor or someone you trust. If you see it in a friend, ask, then ask again. And if there is any thought of suicide, yours or someone else's, contact a crisis line or emergency services now. You will not make things worse by asking.
