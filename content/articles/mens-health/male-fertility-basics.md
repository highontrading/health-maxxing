When a couple has trouble conceiving, attention tends to land on the woman first: her cycle, her age, her tests. Yet a male factor is the sole cause in about 20% of infertile couples and a contributing cause in another 30% to 40%. That adds up to roughly half, which is why current guidelines say both partners should be assessed at the same time.[^2]

Most men learn none of this until they need it. This explainer covers how sperm are made, what a semen analysis measures, what helps and what harms, how to read the headlines about collapsing sperm counts, and when to get tested.

## How sperm are made

Sperm production is a continuous production line in the testicles, driven by hormone signals from the pituitary gland in the brain. Building one sperm takes around 10 weeks, and maturing and transport add a little more, so the sperm in today's sample started out roughly three months ago.[^4]

> [!STAT] ~3 months
> The time it takes to make and mature a sperm. Any change you make, good or bad, needs about that long to show up in a test.[^4]

The testicles also sit outside the body for a reason. Sperm production works best a few degrees below core body temperature, which is why heat keeps coming up in fertility research.[^11]

## What a semen analysis measures

The basic test is a semen analysis: a sample, usually produced by masturbation after a few days without ejaculating, examined under a microscope. The World Health Organization (WHO) publishes reference values based on men whose partners conceived within a year.[^3]

| Measure | Lower reference limit |
| --- | --- |
| Semen volume | 1.4 mL |
| Sperm concentration | 16 million per mL |
| Total sperm count | 39 million per ejaculate |
| Total motility (sperm that move) | 42% |
| Progressive motility (sperm that swim forward) | 30% |
| Normal shape | 4% |

Each limit is the fifth percentile, meaning 95% of those recent fathers scored higher. They are reference points, not pass marks. Men below them can father children, and men above them can still have trouble.[^3]

Results also swing from one sample to the next, so an abnormal result is usually repeated. If it stays abnormal, a specialist looks for a cause with an examination, hormone blood tests and sometimes genetic tests.[^2]

## What affects sperm

- **Testosterone and anabolic steroids.** This is the most overlooked cause. Testosterone from outside the body shuts down the hormone signals that drive the testicles, and sperm production can stop. US guidelines say testosterone should not be prescribed on its own to men who want children now or later.[^2] Our [testosterone explainer](/articles/testosterone-explained) has more.
- **Varicocele.** Enlarged veins in the scrotum are found in roughly 15% of all men and about 40% of men seen for infertility.[^12] Surgical repair is considered when a varicocele can be felt and semen results are abnormal.[^2]
- **Smoking.** A meta-analysis of 20 studies covering nearly 6,000 men linked smoking with lower sperm counts and poorer motility.[^9] Our guide to [quitting smoking](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) can help.
- **Weight.** In a pooled analysis of 21 studies, men with obesity were 28% more likely than men of normal weight to have a low or zero sperm count, and the odds roughly doubled with severe obesity.[^10]
- **Heat.** Among 656 men attending a fertility clinic, those who usually wore boxers had a 25% higher sperm concentration than those who wore tighter underwear.[^11] It was an observational study, so it cannot prove the underwear was responsible. Frequent hot tubs and saunas are also thought to lower counts temporarily.[^12]
- **Alcohol and drugs.** Heavy drinking, cannabis and opioids can all affect sperm production.[^12]
- **Age.** Male fertility has no cliff edge, but guidelines advise telling couples that when the father is 40 or older there is a higher risk of adverse health outcomes for children.[^2]
- **Medical history.** Undescended testicles, infections, cancer treatment and some medicines can all play a part.[^12] If you are about to have chemotherapy or radiotherapy, ask about banking sperm first.

One honest caveat: the same US guideline notes that the data on most lifestyle factors are limited.[^2]

## Are sperm counts really collapsing?

A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 223 studies of more than 57,000 men who had not been selected for fertility problems. It estimated that the average sperm concentration fell by 51.6% between 1973 and 2018, from 101 to 49 million per mL, and that the decline has accelerated since 2000.[^5]

Critics dispute the interpretation more than the arithmetic. One group of researchers argued that the analysis leans on patchy data from different places, eras and laboratory methods, and that it treats men from Western countries in the 1970s as the healthy baseline. They proposed instead that sperm counts vary naturally across a wide range, and that above a certain threshold a higher count does not mean better fertility.[^6] An average of 49 million per mL is still three times the WHO's lower reference limit.[^3]

> [!NOTE]
> The trend deserves research, particularly into environmental causes. But a global average says nothing about you. Your own semen analysis tells you far more.

## Do supplements help?

Fertility supplements for men are a big business with modest evidence behind it. A Cochrane review of 90 trials concluded that antioxidants may improve live birth rates, but the evidence was of low certainty, and the benefit disappeared when the weaker trials were left out.[^7]

In one of the largest and best-designed trials, 2,370 men in couples having fertility treatment took folic acid and zinc or a placebo for six months. The supplement improved neither semen quality nor live birth rates.[^8] The US guideline describes the benefits of supplements as being of questionable clinical value.[^2]

## When to get tested

The WHO defines infertility as no pregnancy after 12 months of regular unprotected sex.[^1] That is the usual point at which to seek help, or six months if the woman is 35 or older.[^12] Go sooner if you have a known risk: a history of undescended testicles, testicular surgery or cancer treatment, past or current use of testosterone or anabolic steroids, a swelling in the scrotum, or problems with erection or ejaculation.

There is a second reason to be assessed. Abnormal semen results are linked with other health problems, and guidelines tell doctors to counsel men about them, so a fertility workup doubles as a health check.[^2] Our list of [checkups men skip](/articles/checkups-men-skip) covers the rest.

Treatment depends on the cause. It ranges from surgery for a varicocele or a blockage, through hormone treatment for certain hormone problems, to assisted reproduction such as IVF, which can work even with very low counts.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Sudden, severe pain in a testicle is an emergency. Go to an emergency department immediately, because a twisted testicle has to be treated within hours. See a doctor promptly about any lump, swelling or feeling of heaviness in the scrotum.

Infertility is hard on both partners, and men often carry it quietly. If it is weighing on you, our piece on [men's mental health](/articles/mens-mental-health) may help.

## The bottom line

If you are trying for a baby, the cheap wins are to stop smoking, steer clear of testosterone and steroids, keep to a healthy weight and give any change three months to show. Do not spend heavily on supplements. If nothing has happened after a year, or after six months if your partner is 35 or older, both of you should be tested. A semen analysis is the simplest test either of you will have.
