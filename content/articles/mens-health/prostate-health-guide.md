The prostate is a gland about the size of a walnut that sits just below the bladder and wraps around the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body. Its job is to make some of the fluid in semen.[^1] For the first few decades of adult life, most men never think about it. Then it starts to grow.

Three different conditions affect the prostate, and they are easy to confuse: benign enlargement, inflammation and cancer. This guide explains each one, then tackles the question men ask most: should I have a PSA test?

| | Benign enlargement (BPH) | Prostatitis | Prostate cancer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Non-cancerous growth | Inflammation, sometimes from infection | Malignant cells in the gland |
| Who gets it | Increasingly common after 50 | Any age; common under 50 | Mostly after 50 |
| Typical signs | Weak stream, frequent and night-time urination | Pelvic or genital pain; fever if bacterial | Usually none early on |

## Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

BPH is a non-cancerous enlargement of the prostate. It is not cancer and does not turn into it. It is also close to universal with age.[^1]

> [!STAT] Up to 90%
> The share of men over 80 who have benign prostate enlargement. About half of men aged 51 to 60 already have it.[^1]

As the gland grows it squeezes the urethra, and the bladder has to work harder to push urine past it. Typical symptoms are:[^1]

- a weak or stop-start stream, and trouble getting started
- dribbling at the end
- needing to go often or urgently, especially at night
- a feeling that the bladder has not emptied

If symptoms are mild, adjusting a few habits is a reasonable first plan:[^1]

- drink less in the evening, and cut back on caffeine and alcohol
- ask whether any of your medicines, such as decongestants and some antihistamines, could be making things worse
- train your bladder to hold on a little longer, and learn [pelvic floor exercises](/articles/pelvic-floor-health)
- avoid constipation

### Medicines

If symptoms bother you, several medicines work:[^1][^2]

- **Alpha blockers** relax the muscle in the prostate and at the neck of the bladder so urine flows more easily.
- **5-alpha-reductase inhibitors**, such as finasteride and dutasteride, shrink the gland by blocking the hormone that drives its growth. They take months to work and suit larger prostates. They can cause sexual side effects, and they lower PSA readings, so tell your doctor you take one before any PSA test.[^7]
- **Tadalafil**, better known as an erection medicine, is also used for BPH symptoms.
- **Combinations.** In a trial of about 3,000 men followed for four and a half years, an alpha blocker plus finasteride cut the risk of BPH getting worse by 66%, compared with 39% and 34% for each drug alone.[^3]

One thing that does not work is saw palmetto. High-quality trials found it no better than placebo for urinary symptoms.[^4]

### Procedures

When medicines are not enough, a procedure can remove or shrink the tissue that is blocking the urethra. Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) has long been the standard. Newer options include laser surgery, steam treatment and small implants that hold the urethra open, each with its own trade-offs in recovery time and sexual side effects.[^2]

## Prostatitis

Prostatitis means inflammation of the prostate, and it is the most common urinary tract problem in men under 50.[^5]

The most frequent form, called chronic prostatitis or chronic pelvic pain syndrome, is not caused by bacteria. It brings pain in the pelvis, genitals or lower back that lasts for months, sometimes with urinary symptoms or painful ejaculation, and treatment is tailored to the symptoms.[^5]

Acute bacterial prostatitis is a different animal. It comes on suddenly with fever, chills, pain and difficulty urinating, and it needs immediate medical care and antibiotics.[^5]

## Prostate cancer and the PSA question

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men. The risk rises with age, and it is higher in Black men and in men whose father or brother had the disease. Early prostate cancer usually causes no symptoms, which is why the argument is about testing.[^9]

The PSA test measures prostate-specific antigen, a protein made by the prostate, in a blood sample. No level cleanly separates normal from abnormal. PSA rises with cancer, but also with BPH, prostatitis and urinary infections, and only about one in four men who have a biopsy because of a raised PSA turn out to have cancer.[^7]

Screening saves some lives and harms some men. The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) estimates that for every 1,000 men aged 55 to 69 offered PSA screening over about 13 years, roughly 1.3 deaths from prostate cancer are prevented and about 3 cases of cancer spreading are avoided. Against that, many men have false alarms and biopsies, and an estimated 20% to 50% of cancers found by screening would never have caused harm. Treating them can leave lasting problems: after surgery, about 1 in 5 men have long-term urinary leakage and about 2 in 3 have long-term erection problems.[^6]

So the guidelines frame it as a personal decision:

- **USPSTF (2018):** men aged 55 to 69 should decide individually after discussing the benefits and harms with a clinician. It recommends against PSA screening from age 70.[^6]
- **American Cancer Society:** have the conversation at 50 if you are at average risk, at 45 if you are Black or have a father or brother who was diagnosed before 65, and at 40 if several close relatives were diagnosed young.[^8]
- **UK:** there is no national screening program for men at average risk, but men aged 50 and over can ask their GP for a PSA test after talking through the pros and cons.[^9]

## What has improved: MRI and active surveillance

Two changes have shifted the balance since the older screening trials.

**MRI before biopsy.** In the PRECISION trial, men with a suspected cancer had either a standard biopsy or an MRI scan first, with a biopsy only if the scan looked suspicious. The MRI route found more clinically significant cancers (38% versus 26%) and fewer insignificant ones (9% versus 22%), and 28% of the men avoided a biopsy altogether.[^10] The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence now recommends MRI as the first investigation for suspected localized prostate cancer.[^12]

**Active surveillance.** Low-risk cancers can be monitored with PSA tests, scans and repeat biopsies, and treated only if they progress.[^12] In the UK's ProtecT trial, 1,643 men with localized cancer found by PSA testing were assigned to active monitoring, surgery or radiotherapy. After 15 years, about 3% had died of prostate cancer, with no significant difference between the groups, although spread of the cancer was more common with monitoring (9.4%, against about 5% with surgery or radiotherapy).[^11]

> [!NOTE]
> A raised PSA is the start of a conversation, not a verdict. Ask what else could explain it, whether the test should be repeated, and whether an MRI is the next step before any biopsy.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor about urinary symptoms that bother you, blood in your urine or semen, or pain when you urinate or ejaculate. Go to an emergency department if you cannot pass urine at all, or if you have fever and chills together with pelvic pain or difficulty urinating.[^1][^5] Unexplained bone pain or weight loss alongside urinary symptoms also needs prompt assessment.

## The bottom line

Most prostate symptoms come from [benign enlargement](/conditions/benign-prostatic-hyperplasia), which is common, not dangerous in itself and very treatable. Whether to have a PSA test is a judgment call: it can catch a dangerous [prostate cancer](/conditions/prostate-cancer) early, and it can also lead to treatment you never needed. Have the conversation at 50, or at 45 if you are at higher risk, and know that MRI and active surveillance have made the path after a raised PSA considerably safer.
