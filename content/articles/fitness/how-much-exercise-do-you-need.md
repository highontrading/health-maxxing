Ask ten people how much exercise you need and you will get ten answers, most of them involving guilt. The actual answer is refreshingly specific, backed by a mountain of evidence, and smaller than most people fear.

It comes from the World Health Organization, which reviewed the research in 2020 and published one set of targets for the whole planet.[^1] This guide translates those targets into a normal week, explains what counts, and shows why doing something imperfectly beats planning something perfect.

## The numbers, in one place

For adults aged 18 to 64, the WHO recommends:[^1]

- **150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week**, or
- **75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity a week**, or a mix of the two, and
- **muscle-strengthening activity for all major muscle groups on two or more days a week.**

> [!STAT] 150 min
> The weekly minimum of moderate activity for adults. That is about 22 minutes a day, or 30 minutes on five days.[^1]

Other age groups get their own version:[^2]

| Who | Aerobic target | Also |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children and teens (5 to 17) | An average of 60 minutes a day, moderate to vigorous | Vigorous and bone-strengthening activity 3 days a week |
| Adults (18 to 64) | 150 to 300 min moderate or 75 to 150 min vigorous a week | Strength work 2+ days a week |
| Older adults (65+) | Same as adults | Balance and strength training 3+ days a week |
| Pregnant and postpartum women | At least 150 min moderate a week | Strength work and gentle stretching |

Going beyond 300 minutes brings additional benefit, and the guideline adds one more line that is easy to miss: sit less, and replace sitting with movement of any intensity.[^1]

## What "moderate" and "vigorous" feel like

You do not need a heart-rate monitor. The talk test works well.[^5]

- **Moderate:** your breathing and heart rate are clearly up, but you can hold a conversation. You could talk; you could not sing. Brisk walking, easy cycling, doubles tennis, water aerobics and pushing a lawn mower all qualify.
- **Vigorous:** you can say a few words, then you need a breath. Running, swimming laps, fast cycling, singles tennis, a hard hike uphill.

As a rule of thumb, one minute of vigorous activity is worth about two minutes of moderate activity, which is why the vigorous target is half the size.[^5] If you want numbers, our [heart rate zone calculator](/tools/heart-rate-zones) will estimate your ranges.

> [!TIP] Count what you already do
> Walking fast to the station, carrying shopping up stairs, cycling to work and digging the garden all count. Before you add anything, tally a typical week. Many people are closer than they think.

## How much difference does it make?

A lot, and the biggest jump comes at the very start.

A pooled analysis of more than 660,000 adults compared people's leisure-time activity with how long they lived. Compared with people who did nothing, those who did some activity but less than the recommended minimum had a 20% lower risk of death during follow-up. People who met the minimum had a 31% lower risk, and the benefit topped out at around 39% for those doing three to five times the minimum. Even at ten times the minimum, there was no sign of harm.[^4]

This was an observational study, so it cannot prove that the exercise alone caused the difference. But the pattern is consistent across dozens of studies and populations, and it underpins the WHO's simple summary: some physical activity is better than none, and more is better.[^2]

> [!NOTE]
> About 31% of adults worldwide, roughly 1.8 billion people, did not reach the recommended level of activity in 2022, and the share has been rising since 2000.[^3] If you reach 150 minutes a week, you are doing better than a large part of the planet.

## Short bursts count now

Older advice said activity only counted in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The WHO removed that rule in 2020 after reviewing evidence that activity of any length contributes to health.[^2]

Research using wrist-worn trackers has since made the point vividly. Among about 25,000 UK adults who did no structured exercise, those who logged roughly three short bursts of vigorous everyday activity a day, each lasting a minute or two (think running for a bus or climbing stairs fast), had a 38% to 40% lower risk of death from any cause over about seven years than those who logged none.[^7] Again, this is an association, not proof, but it is encouraging if a gym schedule is not realistic for you right now.

The same logic applies to timing. People who squeeze their weekly total into one or two sessions, the so-called weekend warriors, had about a 30% lower risk of death than inactive adults in a large British study, close to the benefit seen in people who exercised more often.[^8]

## Do not skip the strength part

The two-days-a-week strength target is the half of the guideline people forget. It matters for more than muscle size: strength training supports bone density, blood sugar control, balance and the ability to keep doing ordinary things as you age.

A meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies found that muscle-strengthening activity was associated with a 10% to 17% lower risk of death from any cause, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes, with the greatest benefit at around 30 to 60 minutes a week.[^6]

Strength work means anything that makes your muscles work against resistance until they are properly tired: weights, resistance bands, bodyweight moves such as squats and push-ups, heavy gardening, or carrying a loaded backpack uphill. If you are new to it, start with our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) and the [exercise library](/exercises).

## What a real week looks like

There are many ways to reach the target. Three examples:

**The walker.** Brisk 30-minute walk on five days (150 minutes), plus two 20-minute bodyweight sessions at home.

**The busy one.** Two 25-minute runs and one 30-minute spin class (80 vigorous minutes), plus two short strength sessions tacked on to the runs.

**The mixer.** Cycle commute three days a week (20 minutes each way, 120 minutes), a 40-minute weekend hike, and two gym sessions with weights.

If your current total is close to zero, do not jump straight to 150. Add 10 minutes a day for a couple of weeks, then build. If you track steps, see [how many steps a day you really need](/articles/how-many-steps-a-day).

> [!DOCTOR]
> Most people can start gentle activity without a medical check. See a doctor first if you have chest pain, breathlessness that seems out of proportion, dizziness or fainting with exertion, or a heart, kidney or metabolic condition such as diabetes. Stop and get urgent help if you develop chest pain or pressure, or feel faint, during exercise.

## The bottom line

Aim for 150 minutes of moderate movement a week and two sessions that challenge your muscles. Count everything, including the short and unglamorous bits. If you cannot reach the target yet, do what you can: the first few minutes are the most valuable ones you will ever add.
