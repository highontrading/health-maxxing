Two terms have escaped the sports science lab and landed in everyday conversation: VO2 max and zone 2. One is a measurement, the other a training intensity, and both come wrapped in big promises about living longer.

Some of those promises hold up well. Others have run ahead of the evidence. This piece explains what each term means, what the research does and does not show, and how to build a week of cardio that covers both.

## What VO2 max measures

VO2 max is the largest amount of oxygen your body can take in and use during all-out exercise, usually expressed in milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute (mL/kg/min). It reflects the whole supply chain: lungs, heart, blood vessels and the muscles that burn the oxygen.

Doctors call the same quality cardiorespiratory fitness and often report it in METs. One MET is the energy you use sitting still, about 3.5 mL/kg/min.[^3]

A true measurement needs a lab, a treadmill or bike, and a mask that analyzes your breath while the effort ramps up until you cannot continue. Most people will only ever see an estimate, which is fine for the purposes below.

## Why fitness predicts how long you live

The link between fitness and survival is one of the most consistent findings in exercise research.

In a study of 122,007 adults who had treadmill tests at a large US hospital system, the least fit quarter died at about five times the rate of the "elite" group, roughly the fittest 2% for their age and sex, over a median of about eight years. There was no point at which more fitness stopped helping, and being unfit carried a risk comparable to or greater than smoking, diabetes or coronary artery disease.[^1]

A larger study of 750,302 US veterans aged 30 to 95 found the same pattern across ages, sexes and racial groups: the least fit fifth had about four times the risk of death of the fittest 2%.[^2]

The American Heart Association reviewed this body of evidence in 2016 and concluded that each 1-MET increase in fitness is associated with a 10% to 25% improvement in survival. It argued that fitness should be treated as a clinical vital sign, alongside blood pressure.[^3]

These are observational studies. Fitness is partly inherited, and illness lowers it, so not all of that gap is caused by exercise. What makes the finding useful is that fitness is trainable, at any age.

## What zone 2 means

Training zones slice effort into bands. In the five-zone model built into most sports watches, zone 2 is roughly 60% to 70% of your maximum heart rate: easy, steady effort that you could keep up for a long time. Exercise scientists define it by physiology instead, as effort below the first lactate threshold, the point at which lactate starts to accumulate in the blood.[^4]

In practice, use the talk test. In zone 2 you can speak in full sentences but would not want to sing. That overlaps with what health bodies call moderate intensity, which the American Heart Association puts at about 50% to 70% of maximum heart rate.[^10]

The usual formula for maximum heart rate, 220 minus your age, is only an estimate, so treat any zone calculated from it as approximate.[^10] Our [heart rate zone calculator](/tools/heart-rate-zones) gives you starting ranges. Let the talk test overrule the watch.

## Is zone 2 special? The honest answer

Online, zone 2 is often presented as uniquely good for your mitochondria, the parts of your cells that turn fuel into energy, and for burning fat. A 2025 narrative review looked for evidence that zone 2 beats other intensities on those measures in the general population and did not find it. The authors concluded that higher intensities appear to produce equal or greater improvements, particularly for people with limited time to train.[^4]

Head-to-head trials point the same way for VO2 max. A meta-analysis of controlled trials found that steady endurance training and high-intensity intervals both raised VO2 max substantially in healthy young to middle-aged adults, with intervals ahead by about 1 mL/kg/min.[^5] In a classic Norwegian study of active young men, eight weeks of 4-minute intervals raised VO2 max by about 7%, while the same amount of work done as long, slow running at 70% of maximum heart rate produced no significant change.[^6]

So why do elite endurance athletes do about 80% of their sessions at low intensity?[^7] Largely because they train so much. Easy sessions let them pile up volume without breaking down. Someone who exercises three hours a week is in a different situation.

What about living longer? The best trial so far, Generation 100, assigned 1,567 Norwegians aged 70 to 77 to five years of twice-weekly intervals, twice-weekly moderate sessions, or simply following national activity advice. Deaths did not differ significantly between the groups: 3.0% with intervals, 5.9% with moderate training and 4.7% in the comparison group.[^8]

None of this makes zone 2 a waste of time. Easy cardio is low risk and pleasant, and you can do a lot of it without feeling wrecked. It is the simplest way to reach the 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week that the World Health Organization recommends.[^9] It is just not magic.

## A week that trains both

| Session | What to do | How it should feel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 to 3 easy sessions | 30 to 60 minutes of brisk walking, cycling, jogging or swimming | Full sentences, steady breathing |
| 1 interval session | 10 minutes easy, then 4 x 4 minutes hard with 3 minutes easy between, then cool down | A few words at a time |
| 2 strength sessions | Full-body resistance training | See our [beginner plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) |

The 4 x 4 format comes from the Norwegian research: four 4-minute efforts at 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate, separated by 3 minutes of easy movement.[^6] The last minute of each effort should feel very hard but controlled.

If you are new to exercise, spend the first four to six weeks on easy sessions only, then add intervals gradually. If time is tight, remember that the WHO counts vigorous activity at double value: 75 to 150 vigorous minutes a week meets the same target as 150 to 300 moderate ones.[^9] For more on the trade-offs, see [HIIT vs. steady-state cardio](/articles/hiit-vs-steady-state-cardio).

> [!TIP] No heart rate monitor?
> Easy means you can chat. Hard means you can get out a short phrase and then need a breath. That is accurate enough for almost everyone.

## How your watch estimates VO2 max

Wearables estimate VO2 max from the relationship between your heart rate and your speed during walks, runs or rides, and some estimate it from measurements taken at rest. A meta-analysis by the INTERLIVE research network found that exercise-based estimates were accurate on average but could be off by around 10 mL/kg/min in either direction for an individual. Estimates made at rest tended to read about 2 mL/kg/min too high, with even wider scatter.[^11]

The practical reading: do not compare your number with a friend's or with a chart. Use the same device and watch the trend over months. If you need a real measurement, ask your doctor about a cardiopulmonary exercise test.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Check with a doctor before starting hard intervals if you have heart disease, chest pain, unexplained breathlessness, or fainting or dizziness with effort, or if you have been inactive and have diabetes, high blood pressure or kidney disease. Stop and call your local emergency number if you develop chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness or faintness during exercise.

## The bottom line

Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of how long you live, and it is trainable. Build a base of easy, conversational cardio, add one harder interval session a week once you are ready, and keep up your strength work. Judge progress by the trend over months, not by a single number on your watch.
