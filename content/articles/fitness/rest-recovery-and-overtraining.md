The workout is the request. Recovery is when your body answers it. Muscles, tendons, heart and nervous system all adapt in the hours and days after training, not during it, which means a rest day is not time off from getting fitter. It is when most of the getting fitter happens.

This guide covers what recovery actually requires, which popular recovery tools have evidence behind them, and how to tell normal tiredness from the kind that needs attention.

## How getting fitter works

Training is a stress. It leaves you temporarily weaker and more tired than when you started. Given rest and fuel, your body repairs itself and overshoots slightly, so you come back a little fitter than before. Coaches call this supercompensation.

Repeat the cycle with enough recovery and you improve. Stack stress on stress without it and performance stalls, then falls. The joint consensus statement on overtraining from the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) puts it plainly: successful training must involve overload, but must avoid combining too much overload with too little recovery.[^1]

## Sleep is the main recovery tool

Before you spend anything on recovery products, check one number. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend 7 or more hours of sleep a night for adults, and note that more than 9 hours can be appropriate for people recovering from a sleep debt or illness.[^2]

Hard training does not shrink that requirement. If your sessions are going badly and you are sleeping six hours, fix the sleep first. Our guides to [how much sleep you need](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need) and [sleep habits that work](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) can help.

## Eat enough, and enough protein

Muscle repair needs raw material. The International Society of Sports Nutrition considers 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day sufficient for most people who exercise.[^3] A meta-analysis of 49 trials found that extra protein increased the strength and muscle gained from resistance training, with no further benefit above a total intake of about 1.6 g per kilogram a day.[^4]

For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, that range works out to roughly 100 to 140 g a day. Our [protein calculator](/tools/protein) will do the sum for your weight.

### When you do not eat enough: REDs

Total energy matters as much as protein. In 2023 the International Olympic Committee updated its consensus statement on relative energy deficiency in sport, now abbreviated REDs (you may also see the older form, RED-S). It describes a syndrome of impaired health and performance caused by problematic low energy availability: eating too little, on purpose or by accident, to cover both training and the body's basic functions.[^5]

REDs affects women and men, and not only elite athletes. Its effects include disrupted reproductive hormones, weaker bones, lowered immunity, and worse mood and performance.[^5] Warning signs include:

- Periods that become irregular or stop, or a falling sex drive
- Stress fractures or repeated injuries
- Frequent colds and infections
- Fatigue and declining performance despite consistent training

If these sound familiar, see a doctor. Food and exercise can also become entangled with [eating disorders](/conditions/eating-disorders), which need specialist support.

## Soreness is not a scorecard

Delayed onset muscle soreness, or DOMS, is the ache and stiffness that follows unfamiliar exercise, especially movements where muscles lengthen under load, such as running downhill or lowering into a squat. It builds over the first day, peaks between 24 and 72 hours, and is gone within five to seven days.[^6]

DOMS is most common when you start a new activity or come back after a break.[^6] In other words, it tells you the work was unfamiliar, not that the session was good. Chasing soreness is a poor way to judge training.

Light movement is the most effective way to ease it, although the relief is temporary.[^6] An easy walk or a gentle spin on a bike beats sitting still.

## Overreaching versus overtraining

Feeling flat after a hard week is normal. Sports medicine describes a spectrum:[^1]

| Stage | What it looks like | Time to recover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional overreaching | A short dip in performance after a hard block, often followed by improvement | Days to weeks |
| Non-functional overreaching | A longer drop in performance with fatigue and mood or sleep changes | Weeks to months |
| Overtraining syndrome | Prolonged underperformance with ongoing symptoms | Months or longer |

Overtraining syndrome is a diagnosis of exclusion. Doctors first rule out other explanations, such as anemia, thyroid problems, infections and low energy intake.[^1] The research on it comes largely from competitive athletes. For most people, the problem is more ordinary: the training is reasonable, but sleep, food or life stress has slipped.

The warning signs to watch for are a drop in performance that lasts for weeks despite rest, persistent fatigue, irritability or low mood, disturbed sleep and more frequent illness.[^1]

## Evidence check: ice baths, massage guns and foam rollers

| Tool | What studies show | Worth it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ice baths | A Cochrane review of 17 small trials found less soreness than with passive rest, based on low-quality evidence.[^7] In a 12-week trial, cold water immersion after each strength session blunted gains in muscle size and strength[^8] | Useful between events; skip it after lifting if muscle is the goal |
| Massage | In a meta-analysis of recovery methods, massage was the most effective for reducing soreness and perceived fatigue[^9] | Yes, if you enjoy it |
| Foam rolling | A meta-analysis of 21 studies found small short-term gains in flexibility and slightly less perceived soreness[^10] | Cheap and modestly helpful |
| Massage guns | A review of a small number of studies found short-term gains in flexibility and reduced experience of muscle pain[^12] | Fine for how it feels; do not expect faster recovery |

> [!NOTE]
> None of these tools comes close to the basics. If you are short on sleep or food, no amount of rolling, icing or percussing will make up the difference. For more on cold exposure, see [heat, cold and sauna](/articles/heat-cold-and-sauna).

## How to schedule rest

- **Give muscles 48 hours.** The ACSM recommends at least 48 hours between resistance sessions for the same muscle group.[^11]
- **Alternate hard and easy.** Follow a demanding day with an easy one, and keep at least one or two days a week for rest or gentle movement.
- **Rest does not mean motionless.** Walking, easy cycling or a short mobility session all count as recovery.
- **Scale back when life is heavy.** After a bad night, an illness or a stressful week, shorten the session or lower the intensity instead of forcing the plan.
- **Take an easier week when fatigue builds.** If your numbers slide for more than a week or two, cut your training volume for several days and see whether they rebound.

> [!TIP] A 30-second morning check
> Ask yourself three things: Did I sleep reasonably? Is my mood normal? Do my legs feel ready? If two of the three answers are no, make it an easy day. This is a practical rule of thumb, not a validated test, but it stops most people from digging a deeper hole.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if fatigue or falling performance lasts more than two to three weeks despite rest, if your periods stop for three months or more, or if you have bone pain that could be a stress fracture. Get urgent help for chest pain, fainting or a racing heartbeat at rest. Very dark, tea-colored urine with severe muscle pain or weakness after extreme exertion is an emergency, because it can signal dangerous muscle breakdown.

## The bottom line

Recovery is not optional and it is mostly free: sleep at least seven hours, eat enough food and protein, and leave 48 hours before hitting the same muscles hard again. Treat soreness as information, not a goal. Use ice baths, rollers and massage if you like them, and see a doctor if tiredness or poor performance persists for weeks.
