Strength training has an image problem. It looks complicated, the gym floor seems to have its own dress code, and every corner of the internet insists that its program is the only one that works. The research says nearly the opposite: almost any sensible plan works, and the biggest payoff comes from simply starting.[^1]

This guide gives you one plan built on five basic movements. It takes two or three sessions a week, works in a gym or a living room, and comes with a simple rule for choosing weights.

## Why lifting is worth your time

The World Health Organization asks every adult to do muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week.[^3] That is a health target, and it reflects what stronger muscles do for the rest of you.

An American Heart Association scientific statement sums up the evidence: adults who do resistance training have about a 15% lower risk of death from any cause than those who do none, and the greatest reduction in risk appears at roughly 30 to 60 minutes a week.[^2] Those figures come from observational studies, so they show a link, not proof. The plausible reasons are well documented, though: the same statement concludes that resistance training improves blood pressure, blood sugar control, blood lipids and body composition.[^2]

Bones respond too. Bone is living tissue that gets stronger when it is loaded, and resistance exercise is one of the activity types the US National Institutes of Health recommends for bone health.[^8]

> [!STAT] 30–60 min
> The weekly amount of resistance training linked with the largest reduction in the risk of death from any cause, according to the American Heart Association.[^2]

The most encouraging news for beginners comes from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), which in 2026 published its first update on resistance training since 2009, drawing on 137 systematic reviews. Its message: the largest gain comes from moving from no resistance training to some, and details such as machines versus free weights or elaborate programming did not consistently change results for the average healthy adult.[^1][^9]

## Five movements that cover your whole body

Forget training individual muscles. Think in movements, because a handful of them reach every area the guidelines list: legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms.[^4]

| Movement | Main muscles | Beginner version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | Thighs, glutes | Goblet squat, or sit-to-stand from a chair |
| Hinge | Glutes, hamstrings, lower back | Romanian deadlift or glute bridge |
| Push | Chest, shoulders, triceps | Push-up or overhead press |
| Pull | Upper back, biceps | Dumbbell row, band row or lat pulldown |
| Carry | Grip, core, shoulders | Farmer's carry: walk holding a weight in each hand |

You will find step-by-step instructions for these in our [exercise library](/exercises).

## The plan: two workouts, alternated

Train two or three days a week with at least one rest day in between. That frequency has been the ACSM recommendation for novices for years, and the 2026 update keeps the same starting point: all major muscle groups at least twice a week.[^5][^9]

| Workout A | Workout B | Sets and reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goblet squat | Split squat or step-up | 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 |
| Push-up | Overhead press | 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 |
| One-arm dumbbell row | Lat pulldown or band pull-down | 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 |
| Romanian deadlift | Glute bridge or hip thrust | 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 |
| Farmer's carry | Plank | 2 to 3 rounds of 20 to 40 seconds |

A three-day week looks like A on Monday, B on Wednesday and A on Friday, with the following week starting on B. If two days suits your life better, do A and B once each.

For the first two weeks, do just one or two sets of each exercise. That is not a soft option: the American Heart Association describes a single set of 8 to 12 repetitions, across 8 to 10 exercises that cover the major muscle groups, done twice a week, as highly effective.[^2] Then build to two or three sets, the range the ACSM suggests per exercise for building strength, resting a minute or two between sets.[^9][^5]

The 8 to 12 repetition range is the classic novice recommendation, not a magic number.[^5] It is simply heavy enough to be challenging and light enough to let you practice good technique.

## How heavy? Leave two or three reps in reserve

Pick a weight that lets you finish each set knowing you could have done two or three more good repetitions, and no more than that. Coaches call this "reps in reserve," an effort scale that researchers formalized in 2016 so lifters could rate a set by how many repetitions they had left.[^6]

You do not need to grind until you fail. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found no clear muscle-growth advantage for sets taken to the point where you cannot complete another repetition, compared with stopping short.[^7] The NHS states the target in plain words: work until you need a short rest before you could repeat the exercise.[^4]

When you can do 12 repetitions on every set and still have two in reserve, add the smallest weight increment available and drop back to 8. That steady climb is called [progressive overload](/articles/progressive-overload), and it is the engine of the whole plan.

> [!TIP] Not sure where to start?
> Begin lighter than you think you need. If the last few reps of a set do not slow down or feel clearly harder, go up a little next time. Write down what you lifted, because you will not remember.

## Warm up, then lift well

Spend five to ten minutes getting warm. The NHS routine of marching on the spot, heel digs, knee lifts, shoulder rolls and knee bends takes about six minutes.[^10] Then do one lighter practice set of your first exercise.

Three habits matter more than any clever cue:

- **Control the weight in both directions.** If you have to swing or bounce it, it is too heavy.
- **Use the fullest range of motion you can manage comfortably**, and let that range grow over time.
- **Keep breathing.** Breathe out as you lift or push and in as you lower. The American Heart Association stresses proper breathing technique for people with high blood pressure in particular.[^2]

## No gym? No problem

The ACSM guidance supports elastic bands, bodyweight exercises and home training as effective options.[^9] Each movement has a home version:

- **Squat:** sit-to-stand from a chair, then squats hugging a loaded backpack.
- **Hinge:** glute bridges, then single-leg bridges.
- **Push:** push-ups with your hands on a kitchen counter, then a sturdy chair, then the floor.
- **Pull:** rows with a resistance band or a loaded backpack.
- **Carry:** full shopping bags or water containers, one in each hand.

Bodyweight exercises get harder by changing the angle or using one limb at a time, so the reps-in-reserve rule still applies.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Check with a doctor before starting if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure that is not controlled, a hernia, recent surgery or a recent injury. If you have osteoporosis or low bone density, get individual advice first: experts recommend protecting the spine by avoiding exercises that flex, bend or twist it.[^8] Stop and seek urgent help if you get chest pain or pressure, or feel faint, while exercising. Sharp or worsening joint pain is a reason to stop that exercise and have it assessed.

## The bottom line

Two or three full-body sessions a week, five movements, one or two sets to begin with and a weight that leaves a couple of reps in reserve: that is enough to get stronger and to meet the strength half of the [exercise guidelines](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need). Add a little load when the reps get easy, [eat enough protein](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need), and take your [rest days](/articles/rest-recovery-and-overtraining). Consistency will do more for you than any perfect program.
