Most supplements promise a lot and deliver a shrug. Creatine is the exception. It has been tested in hundreds of trials over three decades, it reliably does what the label says, and it costs very little.

It is also surrounded by myths, from ruined kidneys to receding hairlines. This piece covers how creatine works, what you can realistically expect, how to take it, and where the evidence is still thin.

## What creatine is and how it works

Creatine is a compound your body makes from amino acids, mainly in the liver and kidneys. You produce about 1 g a day and get a similar amount from food if you eat meat or fish. Around 95% of it is stored in your muscles.[^1]

There, in the form of phosphocreatine, it helps recycle ATP, the molecule that powers muscle contraction. This system matters most during short, intense efforts such as a heavy set of squats or a sprint, when your muscles burn through ATP faster than other energy pathways can replace it.[^1]

Supplementing tops up the tank. Muscle creatine stores rise by about 20% to 40%, which lets you squeeze out an extra repetition or hold your power a little longer.[^1] Over months, those extra reps add up. People who eat little or no meat tend to start with lower stores and may respond more strongly.[^2]

## What it does: strength, muscle and performance

The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) calls creatine monohydrate the most effective nutritional supplement available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training. Performance in short, repeated, high-intensity efforts typically improves by about 10% to 20%.[^1]

The effect on muscle is real but modest. A meta-analysis of 22 trials in 721 older adults found that those who took creatine while doing resistance training gained about 1.4 kg (3 lb) more lean tissue than those who trained with a placebo, and improved more in upper- and lower-body strength.[^4]

> [!STAT] 1.4 kg
> The extra lean tissue gained by older adults who took creatine during resistance training, compared with training plus placebo, in a meta-analysis of 22 trials.[^4]

Two limits are worth knowing. Creatine amplifies training; it does not replace it. And it is of little value for endurance sports, where the extra body weight may even be a disadvantage.[^3]

## How to take it

The guideline dose is simple: 3 to 5 g of creatine monohydrate a day.[^1]

| Approach | Daily amount | Muscles fully stocked in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| With loading | About 20 g (0.3 g per kg of body weight), split into four doses, for 5 to 7 days, then 3 to 5 g | About a week |
| Without loading | 3 to 5 g | About four weeks |

Both routes end in the same place.[^1] Loading simply gets you there sooner, at the cost of a higher chance of stomach upset. Unless you have a competition next week, skipping it is the easier choice.

A few practical points:

- **Choose monohydrate.** It is the form used in almost all the research, and pricier alternatives have not been shown to work better.[^1][^2]
- **Take it daily**, at whatever time you will remember, including on rest days.
- **Check the label.** In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration does not approve supplements for safety or effectiveness before they are sold.[^8] A product tested by an independent certification program is a safer bet.

## Water weight and other side effects

The only side effect that shows up consistently in studies is weight gain.[^1] Expect a kilogram or two (2 to 4 lb) in the first week or two, mostly water drawn into muscle cells.[^1][^3] This is not fat, and it is part of how creatine works.

Large single doses can upset your stomach, which is another reason to split a loading dose or skip loading entirely.

Two popular worries have little behind them. Claims that creatine causes cramping or dehydration have not been supported by research. The hair-loss scare traces to one small 2009 study of rugby players, which found a rise in DHT, a hormone linked to [male pattern hair loss](/articles/male-pattern-hair-loss). That result has not been replicated, and no study has shown that creatine causes hair loss.[^2]

Two other misconceptions are easy to clear up. Creatine is not an anabolic steroid: it is a compound found in ordinary food, and it works through energy supply, not hormones. And although much of the early research was done in men, the trials available suggest it works for women too.[^2]

## Is it safe for your kidneys?

In healthy people, the evidence is reassuring. The ISSN reports no harm in studies using up to 30 g a day for as long as five years.[^1] A 2019 meta-analysis concluded that creatine did not damage kidney function at the doses and durations studied.[^5]

The confusion comes from a similar-sounding word. Creatine breaks down into creatinine, the waste product that blood tests use to estimate how well your kidneys filter. Taking creatine can nudge blood creatinine upward without any change in the kidneys themselves, which can make a routine result look worse than it is.[^2]

> [!WARNING] Tell whoever orders your blood tests
> If you take creatine, say so before a kidney function test. It helps your doctor read the result correctly and decide whether a repeat or a different test is needed. See [understanding blood tests](/articles/understanding-blood-tests).

The reassuring studies were done mostly in people with healthy kidneys. If you have [chronic kidney disease](/conditions/chronic-kidney-disease) or reduced kidney function, the safety of creatine is not established.

## Brain and aging: promising, not proven

Your brain uses creatine too, which has prompted a wave of interest in whether supplements sharpen thinking.

A 2018 systematic review of six trials involving 281 healthy people found signs that creatine may improve short-term memory and reasoning, with inconsistent results for other mental abilities.[^6] A 2023 meta-analysis found improved memory performance, with the clearest effect in adults aged 66 to 76.[^7]

Those results are interesting, not conclusive. The trials were small and short, used different doses and measured different things. There is no evidence that creatine prevents dementia.

For older adults, the best-supported use remains physical: more lean tissue and strength, and the evidence there is for creatine combined with resistance training, not creatine on its own.[^4] If that is your goal, a [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) matters more than the powder.

## Who should check with a doctor first

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor before taking creatine if you have kidney disease or reduced kidney function, have only one kidney, or take medicines that can affect the kidneys. Do the same if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, because safety data are lacking. Performance supplements are not recommended for children and teenagers.[^3] Stop and seek advice if you notice swelling in your legs or face, a marked drop in how much you urinate, or persistent nausea.

## The bottom line

If you lift weights or do high-intensity sport and want a supplement with real evidence, creatine monohydrate at 3 to 5 g a day is it. Expect a small edge in strength and muscle and a kilogram or two of water weight. It is not a substitute for training, [enough protein](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need) or sleep, and anyone with kidney problems should get medical advice first.
