Ten thousand steps a day is the most famous number in fitness. It is on your phone, your watch and possibly your conscience. It also began life as a marketing slogan, not a medical finding.

Researchers have now tested the question properly, using step counters on hundreds of thousands of people. The answer is good news: the benefits start well below 10,000, and for older adults the curve flattens before you get there.

## Where 10,000 came from

The target is generally traced to 1965, when the Japanese company Yamasa Clock sold a pedometer called Manpo-kei, which translates as "10,000 steps meter." The name was catchy, the number was round, and it spread around the world. It did not come from a study.[^2]

## What the research found

The first big test came in 2019. Researchers gave step counters to 16,741 US women with an average age of 72, then followed them for about four years. Women who averaged about 4,400 steps a day had a 41% lower death rate than those who averaged about 2,700. The benefit kept growing up to roughly 7,500 steps a day and then leveled off.[^2]

Since then, several larger analyses have filled in the picture.

| Study | Who was studied | Main finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US adults, 2020 | 4,840 people aged 40 and over | 8,000 steps a day was linked with a 51% lower risk of death than 4,000[^3] |
| Pooled analysis, 2022 | 47,471 adults in 15 cohorts | Benefit plateaus at 6,000 to 8,000 steps for ages 60 and over, 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults[^1] |
| Pooled analysis, 2023 | About 227,000 people in 17 studies | Each extra 1,000 steps a day was linked with a 15% lower risk of death; benefits started at about 4,000 steps[^4] |
| Dose-response review, 2025 | Adults in dozens of cohorts | 7,000 steps a day was linked with a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000[^5] |

The 2025 review also looked beyond mortality. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 steps was associated with a 38% lower risk of dementia and with lower risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, depressive symptoms and falls.[^5]

> [!STAT] 7,000
> Daily steps associated with a 47% lower risk of death, compared with 2,000 steps, in a 2025 review of step-count studies.[^5]

The pooled analyses add useful detail. In the 2022 study, people in the more active three-quarters of the sample had a 40% to 53% lower risk of death than the least active quarter.[^1] The 2023 analysis found heart benefits at even lower counts: fewer deaths from cardiovascular disease from about 2,300 steps a day, with each additional 500 steps linked to a 7% lower risk.[^4]

Two patterns show up again and again. First, the steepest gains are at the bottom: going from 3,000 to 5,000 steps does more for you than going from 10,000 to 12,000. Second, the curve flattens at a point that depends on age, which is why a single target for everyone never made much sense.[^1]

> [!NOTE]
> These are observational studies. People who are already unwell tend to walk less, which can make steps look more protective than they really are. Researchers adjust for existing illness, but they cannot remove the problem completely. What makes the finding credible is its consistency across many cohorts and countries.

## Does pace matter?

Cadence, the number of steps you take per minute, is a simple gauge of intensity. About 100 steps a minute corresponds to moderate intensity for most adults.[^6] At that pace, a 30-minute walk is about 3,000 steps.

Surprisingly, speed seems to add little once you account for the total. In both the older women's study and the US adult study, stepping intensity was not clearly linked to mortality after total steps were considered.[^2][^3]

That does not make brisk walking pointless. Health guidelines are written in minutes, not steps: at least 150 minutes a week of moderate-intensity activity. Brisk walking counts toward that target, and a slow shuffle around the kitchen does not.[^7] The fairest summary is that total steps matter most and brisk steps are a bonus.

## How steps fit with the official guidelines

The World Health Organization does not set a step target. Its guidelines ask adults for 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening on two days.[^7]

You can translate between the two systems with the cadence rule. At about 100 steps a minute, 150 minutes of brisk walking is roughly 15,000 brisk steps a week, or a little over 2,000 a day.[^6] So someone who takes 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day, with a couple of thousand of them at a purposeful pace, is probably meeting the aerobic guideline without ever calling it exercise.

The reverse is also true. You can reach 8,000 steps entirely by pottering around, in which case you get the benefits linked with step volume but may still fall short on intensity. A daily brisk walk of 20 to 30 minutes closes that gap.

## So what should you aim for?

| If you are | A reasonable target |
| --- | --- |
| Currently under 4,000 steps a day | Add 1,000 to 2,000; this is where the gains are steepest |
| Aged 60 or over | 6,000 to 8,000 a day |
| Under 60 | 8,000 to 10,000 a day |
| Already there | Carry on if you enjoy it; the extra benefit is smaller, and the pooled data show no sign of harm |

Two caveats. Step counts miss cycling, swimming and strength training, so a low number on a day you rode 20 km (12 miles) is not a failure. And steps do not replace the muscle-strengthening sessions that guidelines also ask for.[^7] Our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) puts the pieces together.

Phones and watches also count a little differently from one another. Pick one device, wear it the same way each day and compare yourself only with your own past weeks.

## Easy ways to add steps

You do not need a daily hike. Small additions spread through the day do the job.

- **Walk for 10 minutes after a meal.** At a brisk pace that is about 1,000 steps each time.[^6]
- **Take phone calls on your feet.** Pacing counts.
- **Move the start line.** Get off the bus one stop early or park at the far end of the lot.
- **Choose stairs** for anything under three floors.
- **Make it social.** A standing walk with a friend is harder to cancel than a solo plan.
- **Break up long sitting.** A two-minute walk every hour adds up, and it helps if you [sit all day](/articles/sitting-all-day-what-to-do).

> [!TIP] Find your baseline first
> Wear your tracker or carry your phone for a normal week before changing anything. Then add 500 to 1,000 steps a day, hold that for a week or two, and add again. Gradual increases stick better than a sudden leap to 10,000.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Walking is safe for almost everyone. See a doctor if walking brings on chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, or calf pain that comes on as you walk and eases when you rest, which can be a sign of [peripheral artery disease](/conditions/peripheral-artery-disease). Chest pain that does not settle within a few minutes of rest is an emergency: call your local emergency number.

## The bottom line

You do not need 10,000 steps to benefit. Aim for 6,000 to 8,000 a day if you are 60 or older and 8,000 to 10,000 if you are younger, and remember that the first few thousand are the most valuable. If you are far below that today, add 1,000 steps and build from there.
