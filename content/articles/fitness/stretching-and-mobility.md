Stretching has a reputation as the responsible thing to do. Stretch before you run so you do not get hurt. Stretch afterwards so you are not sore. Stretch every day because tight muscles are bad.

Most of that reputation turns out to be unearned. Stretching does one thing well, which is to make you more flexible, and several popular claims for it have not survived testing. This piece sorts the solid from the shaky and ends with a short routine that is actually worth your time.

## Flexibility, mobility and the kinds of stretching

A few terms get used loosely, so it helps to pin them down.

- **Flexibility** is how far a joint can be moved, for example how far your leg lifts when someone else raises it.
- **Mobility** is how much of that range you can control and use on your own, such as sitting into a deep squat.
- **Static stretching** means holding a muscle in a lengthened position, usually for 10 to 30 seconds.
- **Dynamic stretching** means moving through a range under control, as in leg swings, arm circles or walking lunges.

## What stretching does well

Regular stretching reliably increases range of motion. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommends stretching each major muscle group at least two or three days a week. Hold static stretches for 10 to 30 seconds, to the point of tightness or slight discomfort, and repeat until you have accumulated about 60 seconds per stretch. Older adults may gain more from holds of 30 to 60 seconds, and stretching works best when the muscle is already warm.[^6]

What changes is not quite what most people picture. Over the first weeks of a stretching program, the gain in range seems to come mainly from your nervous system tolerating the stretch better, not from the muscle becoming physically longer.[^7] That is still a real and useful change: you can reach further, and it feels easier.

## What stretching does not do

| Claim | Verdict | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| It prevents soreness | No | A Cochrane review of 12 studies found stretching before or after exercise reduced soreness by about 1 point on a 100-point scale[^1] |
| It prevents injuries | Mostly no | In a meta-analysis of 25 trials with 26,610 participants, stretching showed no protective effect[^2] |
| It improves performance | Not right before explosive effort | Static stretching just before testing reduced performance by about 4% on average; dynamic stretching improved it by about 1%[^3] |

The injury finding deserves a closer look, because the same analysis showed what does work. Strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third, and balance training cut them by almost half.[^2] If your goal is to stay uninjured, time spent getting stronger is better invested than time spent stretching.

There is one nuance. The review of acute effects found some evidence that stretching as part of a warm-up may reduce muscle strains in running and sprinting sports, so the question is not entirely closed for athletes in those sports.[^3]

The performance cost is also smaller than headlines suggest. It showed up mostly with holds of 60 seconds or more per muscle, tested immediately afterwards. Short stretches followed by a few minutes of dynamic movement had little effect.[^3]

> [!STAT] 1 point
> How much stretching reduced muscle soreness, on a 100-point scale, in a Cochrane review of 12 studies. Too small to feel.[^1]

## Strength training is mobility training

Here is the finding that surprises people most. A meta-analysis of 11 studies that compared strength training with stretching found no significant difference in range-of-motion gains between them.[^4] A larger review of 55 studies confirmed that resistance training on its own improves range of motion.[^5]

The condition is that you train through a full range. Half squats will not open up your hips. These will:

- **Deep goblet squats** for hips and ankles.
- **Romanian deadlifts** for hamstrings.
- **Split squats** for the hip flexors of the back leg.
- **Overhead presses and hanging from a bar** for shoulders.
- **Calf raises off a step**, lowering the heel fully, for ankles.

You get strength at the end of the range as well as the range itself, which is what mobility means. Our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) and [exercise library](/exercises) cover the basics. Yoga, Pilates and tai chi are other options the NHS lists for improving strength and flexibility together.[^9]

## How to warm up

A warm-up has two jobs: raise your temperature and rehearse the movements you are about to do. Static stretching does neither very well.

Start with five to ten minutes of easy movement. The NHS warm-up of marching on the spot, heel digs, knee lifts, shoulder rolls and knee bends takes about six minutes.[^8] Then add dynamic moves that resemble your session: leg swings and easy strides before a run, bodyweight squats and a light first set before lifting.

If a tight spot needs a static stretch beforehand, keep it short and follow it with dynamic movement.[^3] Afterwards, stretch if you enjoy it. Just do not expect it to spare you from soreness.[^1]

## A 10-minute routine

Do this after a walk, a workout or a warm shower, when your muscles are warm.[^6] Ease into each position, breathe normally, and never bounce.

1. **Cat-cow** on hands and knees: 8 to 10 slow reps (1 minute).
2. **Lunge with rotation:** step into a long lunge, place both hands down, then reach one arm to the ceiling. Five per side (2 minutes).
3. **Supported deep squat:** hold a door frame or counter and sit into your deepest comfortable squat. Two holds of 30 seconds (1 minute).
4. **Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch:** two holds of 30 seconds per side (2 minutes).
5. **Hamstring stretch:** lie on your back and raise one straight leg, using a towel around the foot. Two holds of 30 seconds per side (2 minutes).
6. **Thread the needle** for the upper back: six slow reps per side (1 minute).
7. **Calf stretch** against a wall: 30 seconds per side (1 minute).

Three sessions a week is enough to see change within a few weeks.[^6]

## When tightness needs assessment

> [!NOTE]
> More flexibility is not always better. Some people have naturally very flexible joints. If yours are both very flexible and painful, or you keep getting sprains or dislocations, see a doctor instead of stretching further, because joint hypermobility syndrome needs a different approach.[^10]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Tightness that eases as you warm up is normal. Get it assessed if stiffness follows an injury and the joint is swollen, locks or gives way; if morning stiffness lasts longer than about 30 minutes or comes with swollen joints; if it comes with numbness, tingling or weakness; or if it has not improved after a few weeks of regular stretching. A calf that is painful, swollen and warm on one side needs urgent medical attention, because it can signal a blood clot. Do not stretch it.

## The bottom line

Stretch if you want more range of motion or simply like how it feels, and expect it to do that job and little else. For injury prevention and usable mobility, strength training through a full range does more. Warm up with movement, save long holds for afterwards, and see a professional when stiffness comes with swelling, numbness or pain that does not settle.
