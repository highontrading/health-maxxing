According to legend, the Greek wrestler Milo of Croton got strong by carrying a calf on his shoulders every day until it grew into a bull. The livestock is optional. The idea is not: ask your body for slightly more than it is used to, repeat, and it adapts.

That idea is called progressive overload, and it sits underneath every strength program that works. This piece explains what it means, the six ways to apply it, how hard and how much to train, and what to do when progress stalls.

## What progressive overload means

Muscles adapt to the work you give them, then stop adapting once that work is no longer a challenge. Progressive overload is the fix: you gradually raise the demand so your body has a reason to keep getting stronger.

It is the organizing principle of the American College of Sports Medicine's (ACSM) 2009 position stand on progression in resistance training.[^1] The 2026 update, an overview of 137 systematic reviews, reaches the same headline conclusion: healthy adults should do resistance training that is progressive.[^2]

Two things about the word "progressive" are worth knowing. First, it means gradual. The NHS advice for anyone starting strength work is to begin slowly and build up over a period of weeks.[^9] Second, it does not mean adding weight every session forever. Progress comes quickly when you are new and slows as you become more trained, which is normal and not a sign that you are doing something wrong.[^1]

## Six dials you can turn

More weight is the obvious way to make training harder. It is not the only one.

| Dial | What changes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Load | The weight goes up | 25 kg to 27.5 kg |
| Reps | More repetitions with the same weight | 8 reps to 10 |
| Sets | More total sets per week | 2 sets to 3 |
| Range | A fuller range of motion | A deeper squat |
| Tempo | Slower reps or added pauses | Three seconds to lower |
| Density | The same work in less time | Rests cut from 2 minutes to 90 seconds |

Load and reps do most of the work for most people. The other four earn their place when you train at home, when the next dumbbell up is too big a jump, or when a joint dislikes heavier weights. That flexibility matters, because the ACSM's 2026 guidance counts resistance bands, bodyweight exercises and home training as effective options.[^3]

Change one dial at a time. If you add weight, reps and sets in the same week, you will not know what worked.

## Double progression: the simplest system that works

The easiest method uses two dials in turn. Pick a repetition range, say 8 to 12. Keep the weight the same and add reps over the weeks until you reach the top of the range on every set. Then add weight, which knocks you back toward the bottom of the range, and start climbing again.

Here is how a dumbbell row might progress:

| Session | Weight | Reps in 3 sets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 25 kg (55 lb) | 8, 8, 8 |
| 3 | 25 kg | 10, 9, 8 |
| 5 | 25 kg | 12, 11, 10 |
| 7 | 25 kg | 12, 12, 12 |
| 8 | 27.5 kg (60 lb) | 9, 8, 8 |

The ACSM's rule of thumb fits neatly: when you can do one or two repetitions more than your target, increase the load by 2% to 10%.[^1] The jump in the table, from 25 kg to 27.5 kg, is 10%.

> [!TIP] Small jumps win
> On a 5 kg dumbbell, the next size up can be a leap of 20% or more. With light weights, keep adding reps for longer, or slow the lowering phase to bridge the gap before you move up.

## How hard should each set be?

Overload only works if your sets are hard enough to count. The practical gauge is "reps in reserve": how many more good repetitions you could have done when you stopped. On the scale researchers formalized in 2016, a set that leaves nothing in the tank rates 10 out of 10, a set with one rep in reserve rates 9, and so on down.[^8]

You might expect that going to failure on every set, to the point where you cannot complete another rep, would build the most muscle. The evidence does not support that. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found only a trivial advantage for training to failure, and no advantage at all in the studies that defined failure strictly as being unable to complete another repetition.[^7] The ACSM's 2026 guidance agrees that training to failure did not consistently change results for the average healthy adult.[^3]

A reasonable working target is to finish most sets with two or three reps in reserve: demanding, but repeatable on the next set and in the next session.

## How much work per week?

Once effort is in place, the next question is volume, usually counted as hard sets per muscle group per week.

A meta-analysis of 15 studies found a graded dose-response relationship between weekly sets and muscle growth: more sets, more growth.[^5] The ACSM's 2026 guidance puts a number on it, suggesting about 10 sets per muscle group a week for people whose goal is muscle size.[^3] Three sets of squats and three of leg press on Monday, repeated on Thursday, would put your thighs at 12.

> [!STAT] ~10 sets
> The weekly volume per muscle group that the ACSM's 2026 guidance suggests for people training to build muscle size.[^3]

If your priority is strength, the dial to turn is load. A network meta-analysis of 178 studies found that heavier weights, above 80% of the most you can lift once (your one-repetition maximum, or 1RM), produced the largest strength gains, while muscle growth was similar across a wide range of approaches.[^4] For strength, the ACSM suggests two to three sets per exercise at around 80% of 1RM.[^3] Our [one-rep max calculator](/tools/one-rep-max) estimates yours from a set of several reps, so you never have to test a true maximum.

Frequency ties these together. Higher training frequencies were associated with more muscle growth in a meta-analysis of 10 studies, and the ACSM's baseline is to train every major muscle group at least twice a week.[^6][^3]

None of these numbers is an entry fee. The same ACSM guidance says the biggest gain comes from moving from no resistance training to some, and the American Heart Association describes even a single set per exercise, done twice a week, as highly effective.[^3][^10] If you are new, start low and earn your volume.

## Plateaus, deloads and when not to push

Sooner or later a lift stops moving. Before you change the program, check the basics for two or three weeks. Are you sleeping enough, eating enough [protein](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need) and turning up consistently? Our [recovery guide](/articles/rest-recovery-and-overtraining) covers all three.

If the basics are sound, turn a different dial. Stuck at 10 reps? Add a set. Stuck on load? Slow the lowering phase or use a fuller range for a few weeks, then come back to the heavier weight. Complex periodization, the practice of cycling training variables in planned blocks, did not consistently change results for the average healthy adult in the ACSM's review.[^3]

Many coaches also schedule a "deload," an easier week with fewer sets or lighter weights, every several weeks or whenever fatigue builds up. Treat it as a practical convention, not a proven rule. If you feel run down and your numbers are sliding, an easier week is a reasonable response.

> [!WARNING] Technique first, load second
> If you have to shorten the movement, swing the weight or hold your breath to finish a rep, the load went up too soon. Drop back, own the movement, then progress. Extra reps done badly are not progress.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Ordinary muscle soreness is one thing. Sharp pain during a lift, pain that lingers or gets worse, joint swelling, or numbness and tingling are another: stop that exercise and have it assessed. Chest pain or pressure, or feeling faint during exercise, needs urgent medical help.

## Track it, or you are guessing

Progressive overload is impossible to apply from memory. For each exercise, record the weight, the reps in each set, and roughly how many reps you had in reserve. A notebook or a phone note is fine.

Then look back every month, not every session. Day-to-day numbers bounce around. What matters is whether the trend over four to six weeks points up on at least one dial. If it does, the program is working.

## The bottom line

Getting stronger comes down to asking for a little more over time: one more rep, a slightly heavier weight, an extra set. Work hard but stop short of failure, train each muscle group at least twice a week, change one thing at a time and write it down. When progress stalls, check your sleep and food before you blame the program.
