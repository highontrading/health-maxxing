Few fitness arguments generate as much heat as this one. One camp says high-intensity interval training, or HIIT, torches fat and makes long workouts obsolete. The other says slow, steady cardio is the foundation of everything and intervals are a shortcut to injury.

The research is calmer than either camp. Both methods work, their results are closer than you would guess, and they are better together than apart. Here is how they compare where it matters.

## What each one is

**HIIT** alternates hard efforts with easy recovery. The hard parts are hard: you can say a few words, then you need a breath. Efforts range from 20-second all-out sprints to 4-minute climbs.

**Steady-state cardio**, which researchers call moderate-intensity continuous training, is a single unbroken effort at a pace you could hold while chatting: brisk walking, easy jogging, relaxed cycling or swimming.

The American Heart Association puts moderate intensity at about 50% to 70% of your maximum heart rate and vigorous intensity at about 70% to 85%.[^10] The World Health Organization treats them as interchangeable at a rate of roughly two to one: 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity a week meets the same target as 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity.[^1] That exchange rate is the whole case for intervals in one line.

## Fitness: a small edge for intervals

Both approaches raise VO2 max, the standard measure of cardiorespiratory fitness. A meta-analysis of controlled trials in healthy young to middle-aged adults found large improvements from each, with intervals ahead by about 1 mL/kg/min.[^2]

The gap looks wider in people with health conditions. Across 10 trials in 273 patients with heart disease, high blood pressure, obesity or metabolic syndrome, fitness rose by about 19% with HIIT and about 10% with moderate continuous training.[^3]

The most striking finding concerns time. In a 12-week trial in sedentary men, one group did three 20-second all-out bike sprints inside a 10-minute session, three times a week. Another did 45 minutes of steady cycling three times a week. Fitness improved by 19% in both, and a measure of blood sugar control improved similarly.[^4] It was a small study, but it shows how far a little hard work can stretch.

> [!STAT] 19%
> The fitness gain in both groups of a 12-week trial: one did 10-minute sessions containing one minute of all-out sprints, the other 45 minutes of steady cycling.[^4]

## Fat loss: roughly a tie

This is where marketing most outruns evidence.

A meta-analysis of 13 trials in adults with overweight or obesity found that HIIT and steady cardio both reduced body fat and waist circumference, with no significant difference between them. HIIT did it in about 40% less training time.[^5] A second meta-analysis reached the same conclusion and added a sobering note: over the short term, neither produced large fat losses on its own.[^6]

One widely quoted review found that interval training reduced absolute fat mass 28.5% more than moderate training. Both approaches lowered body fat percentage, though, and the difference between them amounted to well under a kilogram.[^7]

The fair summary is that exercise of either kind helps modestly with fat loss, and that what you eat matters more. Our guide to [weight loss that lasts](/articles/weight-loss-that-lasts) covers that side.

## Time, enjoyment and sticking with it

HIIT's clear advantage is efficiency. If you have 20 minutes, intervals give you more for them.

Its weakness is that hard exercise is hard. In a 12-month study, adults with overweight chose between unsupervised HIIT and conventional moderate exercise. Adherence to HIIT fell steadily, and by the end only about one in five were still doing it as prescribed. Results were no better than with conventional exercise.[^9]

Steady cardio asks less of your willpower and your recovery. You can do it the day after a strength session, with a friend, or while listening to something. The best choice is the one you will still be doing next year.

## Safety and who should start gentler

Reassuring data come from the group you might worry about most. Among 4,846 patients with coronary heart disease in supervised cardiac rehabilitation, there was one fatal cardiac arrest in about 129,000 hours of moderate exercise and two non-fatal cardiac arrests in about 46,000 hours of HIIT. The authors concluded that the risk was low with both.[^8]

For healthy people, the more common problem is not the heart but the legs. Sprinting and jumping load muscles and tendons heavily, and a body that is not used to it complains. Two habits lower the risk: build a base of steady cardio for four to six weeks first, and do your intervals on a bike, rower or hill instead of an all-out sprint on flat ground.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Get medical advice before starting HIIT if you have heart disease, high blood pressure that is not controlled, chest pain, unexplained breathlessness, or dizziness or fainting with exertion, or if you are pregnant and new to vigorous exercise. Stop and call your local emergency number if you develop chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness or faintness during a session.

## Example sessions

| Session | How to do it | Total time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter intervals | 5 minutes easy, then 6 rounds of 1 minute hard and 2 minutes easy, then 5 minutes easy | 28 minutes |
| 4 x 4 | 10 minutes easy, then 4 rounds of 4 minutes hard with 3 minutes easy between, then 5 minutes easy | About 40 minutes |
| Short sprints | A 10-minute bike session containing three 20-second all-out sprints | 10 minutes |
| Steady | 30 to 60 minutes at a conversational pace | 30 to 60 minutes |

The 4 x 4 format comes from Norwegian research, in which the hard efforts were done at 90% to 95% of maximum heart rate.[^11] The short-sprint format is the one used in the 12-week trial above.[^4] Our [heart rate zone calculator](/tools/heart-rate-zones) can estimate your ranges, but the talk test works just as well.

## Putting both in one week

You do not have to choose. A balanced week looks like this:

- **One or two interval sessions** on non-consecutive days
- **Two or three steady sessions** of 30 to 60 minutes
- **Two strength sessions**, which guidelines ask for regardless of how you do your cardio[^1]

One 4 x 4 session plus three 45-minute brisk walks comfortably clears the weekly aerobic target. If you are short on time, lean on intervals. If you are tired, stressed or sore, lean on steady work. For more on easy training and fitness testing, see [zone 2 and VO2 max](/articles/zone-2-and-vo2-max).

> [!TIP] Make hard days hard and easy days easy
> The common mistake is doing every session at a medium-hard effort: too hard to recover from quickly, too easy to count as intervals. Keep your easy sessions truly conversational so you are fresh enough to push on interval day.

## The bottom line

HIIT is not better than steady cardio, just faster. Fitness gains are slightly larger per minute with intervals, fat loss is about the same, and steady work is easier to recover from and to stick with. Do mostly easy cardio, add one or two short interval sessions a week once you have a base, and choose low-impact options if your joints object.
