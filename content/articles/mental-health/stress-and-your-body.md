Stress has a terrible reputation, and only half of it is deserved. The system that makes your heart pound before a presentation is the same one that gets you out of the path of a speeding car. The trouble starts when it never switches off.

This guide explains what stress does inside your body, how to tell useful pressure from the harmful kind, and which stress-relief tools are backed by trials rather than by wishful thinking.

## The first few minutes

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines stress as a state of worry or mental tension caused by a difficult situation. It calls stress a natural human response that prompts us to deal with challenges and threats.[^1]

That response arrives in two waves.[^2]

**Wave one: adrenaline.** When the amygdala, your brain's alarm center, senses a threat, it signals the hypothalamus, which switches on the sympathetic nervous system. Your adrenal glands release adrenaline (also called epinephrine). Your heart beats faster, your blood pressure rises, your breathing quickens, and stored sugar and fat pour into the blood as fuel. All of this starts before you have consciously finished registering what is happening.

**Wave two: cortisol.** If your brain still sees danger, a hormonal relay called the HPA axis (hypothalamus, pituitary gland and adrenal glands) releases cortisol, which keeps the body on high alert.

When the threat passes, cortisol falls and the parasympathetic nervous system, the body's brake, calms everything down.[^2]

## When stress helps

Short bursts of stress are not a malfunction. WHO puts it plainly: a little stress is good and helps us carry out daily activities.[^1] The jolt of energy before an exam, a race or a difficult conversation is the system doing its job.

The stress response was built for emergencies that end. What matters for your health is less how hard it fires than whether it gets a chance to stand down afterward.

## When it will not switch off

Money worries, caring for a sick relative, a hostile workplace and bad news on a loop do not resolve in minutes. The system stays partly switched on, and over months and years that has costs.

**Heart and blood vessels.** Over time, frequent adrenaline surges are thought to wear on the arteries and push blood pressure up, which makes heart attacks and strokes more likely.[^2] In a pooled analysis of almost 200,000 European workers, those with job strain, meaning high demands combined with little control, had a 23% higher risk of coronary heart disease.[^5]

**Immune defenses.** In a classic experiment, 394 healthy volunteers were given nose drops containing a cold virus. The more stress they had reported beforehand, the more likely they were to develop a cold: 27% of the least stressed group, rising to 47% of the most stressed.[^6]

**Muscles, gut and hormones.** Long-term stress keeps muscles tense, which contributes to tension headaches and migraines, and it can cause bloating, pain and nausea by disrupting communication between the brain and the gut.[^3]

**Habits.** Chronic stress can worsen existing health problems and increase the use of alcohol, tobacco and other substances.[^1]

A caution about the numbers: stress cannot be randomly assigned, so most of this research shows associations. The pattern across studies is consistent, but stress is rarely the only cause.

## Signs your body is under strain

Stress often shows up physically before you name it. WHO and the American Psychological Association list signs such as:[^1][^3]

- headaches or other body pains
- tight muscles, especially in the neck, shoulders and jaw
- an upset stomach
- trouble sleeping
- eating much more or much less than usual
- difficulty concentrating
- irritability or feeling on edge

## Tools that work

No technique removes the cause of your stress. What these tools do is turn down the body's response, which helps you think clearly enough to deal with the cause.

### Move your body

This has the deepest evidence. An overview of 97 systematic reviews, covering more than 1,000 trials and 128,000 participants, found that physical activity produced medium-sized reductions in psychological distress, anxiety and depression compared with usual care.[^7] Any kind counts. See [exercise for mental health](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health) for how to start.

### Slow your breathing

Slow breathing is the one part of the stress response you can control directly. A meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials found that breathing practices lowered self-reported stress compared with control conditions. The effect was small to medium, and the authors urged caution because many trials were at risk of bias.[^8]

> [!TIP] A five-minute reset
> Sit comfortably. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four, then out for a count of six. Keep the breaths gentle rather than deep, and continue for five minutes. Slow and steady matters more than getting the counts exactly right.

### Protect your sleep

Stress wrecks sleep, and poor sleep makes the next day's stress harder to handle. Getting plenty of sleep and keeping a daily routine are on WHO's short list of ways to cope.[^1] Our guide to [sleep habits](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) covers the practical side.

### Talk to people

Connecting with others is another WHO recommendation, and the US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) advises reaching out to friends or family who can help you cope.[^1][^4] Saying a worry out loud often shrinks it.

### Get outside

In a survey of nearly 20,000 people in England, those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in nature were more likely to report good health and high well-being than those who spent none. It did not matter whether the time came in one long visit or several short ones.[^9] The study was observational, so it cannot prove the park did the work, but the cost of testing it yourself is low.

### Train your attention and your thoughts

Mindfulness meditation programs showed moderate evidence of benefit for anxiety and depression in a review of 47 trials, with weaker evidence for stress itself.[^10] We look at this in [meditation and mindfulness](/articles/meditation-and-mindfulness).

The NIMH also suggests a skill taken from cognitive behavioral therapy: notice and challenge your negative and unhelpful thoughts.[^4] WHO publishes a free illustrated guide, Doing What Matters in Times of Stress, with self-help techniques that take a few minutes a day.[^1]

> [!WARNING]
> Some popular coping methods backfire. Extra caffeine winds the body up further, and constant news-checking keeps the alarm ringing: the NIMH advises avoiding excess caffeine, and WHO suggests limiting the time you spend following the news. Leaning on alcohol or tobacco, which chronic stress makes more likely, adds health problems of its own.[^1][^4]

## When stress becomes a health problem

Stress turns into something else when the symptoms do not fade once the pressure eases, or when they begin to run your life. The NIMH's rule of thumb is simple: if you are struggling to cope, or your symptoms will not go away, it is time to talk to a professional.[^4]

Persistent worry may be an [anxiety disorder](/articles/understanding-anxiety). Weeks of low mood and lost interest may be [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment). Exhaustion and cynicism tied to your job may be [burnout](/articles/burnout-how-to-recognize-and-recover). These conditions are treatable, and self-help is not a replacement for that treatment.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if stress symptoms last more than a few weeks, disrupt your sleep or work, or lead you to lean on alcohol or drugs. Never put chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting down to stress: call your local emergency number.

> [!NOTE]
> If you are thinking about harming yourself, or feel you cannot go on, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line now. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^11]

## The bottom line

Stress is a normal response that becomes harmful when it is constant. Move most days, practice slow breathing, guard your sleep and stay in touch with people. If the symptoms persist or start to take over, treat that as a health problem and get professional help.
