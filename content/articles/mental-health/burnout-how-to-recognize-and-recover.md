Everyone has weeks when work is too much. Burnout is what happens when those weeks turn into months, the tank stays empty no matter how much you sleep, and a job you once cared about starts to feel pointless.

The word gets used loosely, but it has a precise meaning, a well-studied set of causes and a recovery path. That path is rarely "take a bubble bath and try harder", and it usually involves changing something about the work as well as about you.

## What burnout is

The World Health Organization (WHO) includes burnout in its International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It has three dimensions:[^1]

- **Exhaustion.** Feeling depleted of energy.
- **Mental distance.** Feeling detached from your job, or negative and cynical about it.
- **Reduced efficacy.** Feeling that you are no longer good at what you do.

Two details in the WHO definition matter. First, burnout is classed as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. Second, the term applies specifically to work and, according to WHO, should not be used to describe experiences in other areas of life.[^1]

"Not a medical condition" does not mean "not serious". A review of 36 long-term studies found that burnout predicted later problems including insomnia, depressive symptoms, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, musculoskeletal pain, and more sick leave.[^4]

## Burnout or depression?

The two overlap, and telling them apart matters because depression needs medical treatment. A meta-analysis found a moderate correlation between burnout and depression and concluded that they are related but distinct.[^3]

| Feature | Burnout | Depression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it shows | Tied to work, at least at first | Every area of life |
| Core feature | Exhaustion, cynicism about the job, feeling ineffective | Persistent low mood or loss of interest in almost everything |
| Status | Occupational phenomenon | Medical diagnosis |

Researchers who have studied burnout for decades describe it as job-related and specific to the work situation, whereas depression is general and follows you everywhere.[^2] A rough test: imagine being away from work for two weeks. If the heaviness would lift, burnout is more likely. If it would come with you, think about [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment).

Burnout can also turn into depression, so the line is not fixed.[^4]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if exhaustion comes with persistent low mood, loss of interest in things outside work, feelings of worthlessness, or major changes in sleep or appetite. Get checked too if you have chest pain, palpitations or tiredness that rest does not touch, because medical problems can look like burnout.

## Where burnout comes from

Burnout is often framed as a problem of individual resilience. The research points elsewhere. The psychologists Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter identify six areas of working life where a mismatch between the person and the job drives burnout:[^2]

1. **Workload.** Too much to do, with too little time or too few resources.
2. **Control.** Little say over how you do your work.
3. **Reward.** Too little pay, recognition or satisfaction for the effort.
4. **Community.** Poor support, isolation or unresolved conflict.
5. **Fairness.** Decisions about workload, pay or promotion that seem unjust.
6. **Values.** Being asked to work in ways that clash with what you think matters.

Workload gets most of the attention, but in this model the other five matter just as much.[^2]

Long hours carry their own risks. WHO and the International Labour Organization estimate that working 55 hours or more a week is associated with a 35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease, compared with working 35 to 40 hours.[^6]

## What actually helps

### Fix the job, not only the person

Most burnout programs teach individuals to cope: mindfulness, stress management, resilience training. These help a little. Changes to the work itself help more.

A meta-analysis of trials in doctors found that programs aimed at the organization, such as changes to schedules and workloads, reduced burnout more than programs aimed at the individual. The effect sizes were 0.45 and 0.18 respectively, which is the difference between a moderate benefit and a small one.[^8] A second review concluded that both kinds can produce meaningful reductions in burnout, and called for research on how best to combine them.[^7]

Most of this evidence comes from healthcare, but WHO's advice to all employers starts in the same place: deal with the risks in the work itself, including excessive workloads, long or inflexible hours, and lack of control, alongside training for managers and support for individuals.[^5]

### What you can do

- **Work out which of the six areas is hurting.** "I am exhausted" gives you nothing to act on. "I have no control over my deadlines" does.
- **Ask for specific changes.** Take a concrete proposal to your manager, human resources or occupational health: fewer projects, clearer priorities, flexible hours. WHO lists flexible hours and modified assignments among reasonable adjustments for workers.[^5]
- **Switch off properly.** People who mentally detach from work during time off report less exhaustion and better sleep.[^9] That means no email in the evening and a clear end to the working day.
- **Protect the basics.** [Sleep](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work), physical activity and time with people you like are how the body recovers from stress. See [stress and your body](/articles/stress-and-your-body).
- **Take leave, with realistic expectations.** The benefits of a vacation fade soon after you return to work.[^10] Use the break to rest and to plan what will change.

> [!WARNING]
> Pushing through is the most common response to burnout and the least effective. In a Swedish study, people who had lived with symptoms for longer before seeking help were slower to recover.[^11]

## How long recovery takes

There is no reliable timetable, and anyone who promises one is guessing. It depends on how deep the burnout is and on whether the conditions that caused it change.

Milder burnout can ease fairly quickly once the load lifts, though there is little research to put numbers on it. Severe burnout is slower. In a Swedish clinic that followed 232 people treated for stress-related exhaustion, symptoms improved a great deal over time, but about a third of patients still had burnout symptoms after 18 months.[^11]

Recovery is rarely a straight line, and setbacks along the way are normal. If the six-area mismatch cannot be fixed where you are, changing role or employer is a legitimate option, not a defeat.

## When to see a professional

Talk to a doctor or a mental health professional if symptoms have lasted more than a few weeks, if you are starting to dread or avoid work, if you rely on alcohol to unwind, or if rest makes no difference. They can check for depression, an [anxiety disorder](/articles/understanding-anxiety) and physical causes, and can support time off or adjustments at work.

> [!NOTE]
> If you feel hopeless or have thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^12]

## The bottom line

Burnout is exhaustion, cynicism and a sense of ineffectiveness caused by chronic work stress. Rest helps, but recovery sticks only when the job changes too, so identify the mismatch and ask for specific fixes. If low mood follows you outside work, or nothing improves after a few weeks, see a professional.
