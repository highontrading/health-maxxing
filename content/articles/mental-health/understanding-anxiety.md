Anxiety is not a design flaw. It is your brain's smoke alarm, and a life without one would be short. The trouble comes when the alarm goes off at burnt toast, or will not stop ringing at all.

That is an anxiety disorder, the most common kind of mental health condition in the world.[^1] This guide covers how to tell ordinary anxiety from a disorder, what the main types look like, which treatments have the best evidence, and what you can do yourself alongside them.

## Ordinary anxiety versus an anxiety disorder

Feeling anxious before an exam, a job interview or a medical result is normal. It is tied to the situation and fades when the situation passes.

An anxiety disorder differs in three ways. The fear or worry persists, often for months, and may build rather than fade. It is out of proportion to any real danger and hard to rein in. And it starts to get in the way of your job, your studies or your relationships. For generalized anxiety disorder, the usual yardstick is excessive worry on most days for at least six months.[^2]

These conditions are common. The World Health Organization (WHO) puts the number of people living with an anxiety disorder at more than 300 million, about 4% of the world's population.[^1] In the United States, an estimated 31% of adults have one at some point in their lives, and women are affected more often than men.[^3]

> [!STAT] 1 in 4
> The share of people with an anxiety disorder who receive any treatment, according to WHO, even though effective treatments exist.[^1]

## The main types

"Anxiety" covers several distinct conditions:[^2]

| Type | What defines it |
| --- | --- |
| Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) | Persistent, hard-to-control worry about everyday things such as health, money and work |
| Panic disorder | Repeated, unexpected panic attacks and fear of the next one |
| Social anxiety disorder | Intense fear of being watched or judged by other people |
| Specific phobias | Intense fear of a particular thing or situation, such as flying, heights, needles or certain animals |
| Agoraphobia | Fear of places where escape might be difficult, such as crowds or public transport |

Our reference pages on [generalized anxiety disorder](/conditions/generalized-anxiety-disorder) and [panic disorder](/conditions/panic-disorder) go into more detail.

## What it feels like in the body

Anxiety is not only in your head. Common physical symptoms include feeling restless or on edge, tiring easily, muscle aches, headaches, stomachaches, trouble concentrating and poor sleep.[^2]

A panic attack is a sudden wave of intense fear that peaks within minutes. It brings a pounding heart, sweating, trembling, chest pain and a sense of losing control or of impending doom.[^2]

Other things can produce the same symptoms. Thyroid problems, heart rhythm disturbances, caffeine and some medicines can all cause or aggravate anxiety, which is why a physical check-up is part of a proper assessment.[^2]

## What anxiety is not

**It is not weakness.** Both genes and environment contribute to the risk, including temperament and stressful or traumatic life events.[^2]

**It is not solved by avoidance.** Dodging what you fear brings quick relief, but it teaches your brain that the danger was real. The fear grows and your life shrinks. This is why facing fears gradually is central to therapy.[^2]

**It is not untreatable.** WHO describes the available treatments as highly effective.[^1] The main obstacle is getting them.

## Treatments that work

### Talking therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied psychological treatment. It teaches you to spot and test anxious thoughts and to change how you respond to them. One of its methods, exposure therapy, involves confronting feared situations step by step so that you can return to the activities you have been avoiding.[^2]

How well does it work? A meta-analysis of 41 trials that compared CBT with a placebo found a moderate benefit on the symptoms being treated.[^6] In plain terms: most people improve noticeably, and not everyone recovers completely.

For GAD, England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) describes a typical course as 12 to 15 weekly sessions of an hour each.[^4]

### Medication

The first-choice medicines are antidepressants of the SSRI and SNRI types, which treat anxiety as well as depression. NICE advises offering an SSRI first.[^4] They take several weeks to work, and you should not stop them without talking to your prescriber.[^2]

Benzodiazepines act quickly, but tolerance and dependence can develop. NICE says they should not be offered for GAD except as a short-term measure during a crisis.[^4] Beta-blockers can ease physical symptoms such as a racing heart and trembling in predictable situations, such as public speaking.[^2]

Which should you choose? NICE recommends a stepped approach: education and monitoring first, then guided self-help, then a choice between CBT and medication if symptoms persist, guided by what you prefer.[^4]

### Mindfulness-based programs

In a 2023 trial, 276 adults with anxiety disorders were randomly assigned to an eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction course or to the SSRI escitalopram. Symptoms improved by a similar amount in both groups, and far fewer people in the mindfulness group reported side effects (about 15% compared with 79%).[^9]

Two cautions. There was no placebo group, and the course was demanding: weekly classes plus daily home practice. We cover the wider evidence in [meditation and mindfulness](/articles/meditation-and-mindfulness).

## What you can do yourself

Self-help suits mild symptoms and works alongside treatment. It is not a substitute when anxiety is disrupting your life.

- **Move.** An overview of more than 1,000 trials found that physical activity produced medium-sized reductions in anxiety symptoms compared with usual care.[^7] See [exercise for mental health](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health).
- **Check your caffeine.** In people with panic disorder, a dose of caffeine equal to about five cups of coffee triggered a panic attack in roughly half, compared with fewer than 2% of healthy volunteers.[^8] If you are prone to anxiety, cut down gradually.
- **Breathe slowly.** In a meta-analysis of randomized trials, breathing practices led to small improvements in anxiety symptoms.[^10]
- **Keep a steady sleep routine.** Our [sleep habits guide](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) can help.
- **Approach, do not avoid.** Take small, planned steps toward the things you have been dodging.

## When and how to get help

Start with a primary care doctor, who can rule out physical causes and explain the local options for therapy. Do not be surprised to be handed a short questionnaire: the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults under 65 for anxiety disorders.[^5]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if worry or fear is present on most days, has lasted for weeks, or is stopping you from working, studying, socializing or sleeping. Go too if you are having panic attacks, or using alcohol or drugs to cope. Get emergency help for chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting. Do not assume it is panic, especially the first time.

> [!NOTE]
> If you are thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^11]

## The bottom line

Anxiety is a normal alarm that sometimes gets stuck on. If it is persistent and limiting your life, it is a health condition with effective treatments, chiefly CBT and SSRI-type medicines. Use exercise, less caffeine and slow breathing as support, and ask for help sooner than feels strictly necessary.
