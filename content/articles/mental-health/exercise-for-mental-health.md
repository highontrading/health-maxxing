A doctor treating depression will usually talk about therapy and medication. A walking plan comes up far less often, yet the authors of two of the largest reviews of the research argue that physical activity belongs among the core treatments.[^1][^2]

This explainer looks at what the trials show, how solid the evidence is, how much activity it takes, and how to start on the days when getting off the sofa feels like a major project. One point up front: for a diagnosed mental health condition, exercise works alongside professional treatment, not in place of it.

## What the trials show for depression

The most comprehensive analysis so far was published in The BMJ in 2024. It pooled 218 randomized trials with 14,170 participants who had depression, and compared different kinds of exercise against controls such as usual care or a placebo tablet.[^1]

Several types of exercise produced what statisticians call moderate reductions in symptoms:[^1]

| Type of exercise | Effect size (larger is better) |
| --- | --- |
| Walking or jogging | 0.62 |
| Yoga | 0.55 |
| Strength training | 0.49 |
| Mixed aerobic exercise | 0.43 |
| Tai chi or qigong | 0.42 |

To make sense of those numbers: an effect of 0.2 is usually called small, 0.5 moderate and 0.8 large. In the same analysis, cognitive behavioral therapy scored 0.55 and SSRI antidepressants 0.26 against the same kind of control.[^1] That comparison flatters exercise somewhat, for reasons covered below, but it shows exercise is in the same league.

Three other findings are useful:[^1]

- **Harder helped more.** Benefits rose with the intensity of the exercise, though even gentle activity helped.
- **It worked across the board.** People with more severe depression and with other health conditions benefited as much as others.
- **People stuck with strength training and yoga.** These had the fewest dropouts.

## How sure can we be?

Less sure than the headlines suggest, and the researchers say so themselves.

Only one of the 218 trials met the strictest criteria for a low risk of bias, and the authors rated confidence in the evidence as low for walking or jogging and very low for the rest.[^1] The main problem is unavoidable: you cannot hide from someone whether they are exercising, so expectations may inflate the results.

An earlier Cochrane review makes the point sharply. Across 35 trials, exercise had a moderate effect on depression. When the reviewers counted only the six most rigorous trials, the effect shrank to a small one that could have been due to chance.[^3]

A fair summary: exercise very probably helps depression, the true effect is likely smaller than the biggest estimates, and because the side effects are mostly good ones, the uncertainty is easy to live with.

## Anxiety and everyday distress

The benefits are not limited to depression. An overview that combined 97 systematic reviews, covering 1,039 trials and more than 128,000 participants, found that physical activity produced medium-sized improvements in depression, anxiety and psychological distress compared with usual care. Higher-intensity activity brought greater improvements.[^2]

The same caution applies: most of the underlying reviews were rated low in quality.[^2] But the direction of the findings was consistent across healthy adults, people with mental health conditions and people with long-term physical illness.

## Prevention: a little goes a long way

Activity also seems to lower the odds of becoming depressed in the first place. An analysis of 15 studies that followed more than 190,000 adults found that, compared with people who did no activity:[^4]

- those doing **half** the recommended amount had an 18% lower risk of depression
- those doing the recommended amount, equal to about 2.5 hours of brisk walking a week, had a 25% lower risk.

Beyond that level the extra benefit was small. The authors estimated that about 1 in 9 cases of depression might be prevented if every inactive adult reached the recommended level.[^4] These were observational studies, so some of the link may run the other way: people who are becoming depressed move less.

> [!STAT] 25%
> The lower risk of depression among adults who do the equivalent of 2.5 hours of brisk walking a week, compared with adults who do none.[^4]

## Why it might work

Nobody knows exactly, and it is probably several things at once. Researchers have proposed two broad sets of mechanisms:[^5]

- **Biological.** Exercise appears to reduce inflammation, helps regulate stress hormones and promotes the growth of new connections between nerve cells.
- **Psychological and social.** Finishing a session builds a sense of mastery and self-esteem, and many forms of exercise bring contact with other people.

These are plausible explanations with supporting evidence, not settled facts.

## How much, and what kind?

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week and notes that regular activity reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety.[^6] For mental health specifically, three practical rules follow from the research:

1. **Something beats nothing.** Half the guideline already brings much of the benefit.[^4]
2. **Push a little if you can.** More vigorous sessions tended to do more.[^1]
3. **Pick what you will repeat.** Walking, yoga, weights and dancing all helped in trials, so preference should decide.[^1]

Structure helps too. For less severe depression, England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists a supervised group exercise program among its treatment options.[^7] Our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) explains the general targets.

## Starting when motivation is low

Low mood kills motivation, which is exactly why "just exercise" is useless advice. These tactics work with that reality:

- **Shrink the task.** Aim for five or ten minutes. If you carry on, fine. If not, you still did it.
- **Schedule it.** Decide when, where and what in advance, so that you do not rely on feeling like it.
- **Lower the barrier.** Put your shoes by the door. Choose a route that starts at your front step.
- **Add a person.** Walk with a friend or join a class. An appointment is harder to skip.
- **Notice the after-effect.** Rate your mood before and after. The evidence that it helps you personally is the best motivator.

> [!TIP] Action first, motivation second
> With depression, waiting until you feel like moving can mean waiting for weeks. Therapists use the opposite order: do a small planned activity first and let the mood follow. A ten-minute walk is a legitimate starting dose.

## Alongside treatment, not instead of it

Exercise is not a cure-all, and it is not a reason to delay or drop other treatment. For more severe depression, NICE puts talking therapy combined with an antidepressant at the top of its list, with exercise further down.[^7] In the BMJ analysis, exercise combined with medication or therapy also performed well.[^1]

If you take an antidepressant, do not stop it suddenly or on your own, even if exercise is helping. Stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal symptoms.[^8] Read more in our guide to [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment) and to [anxiety](/articles/understanding-anxiety).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if low mood or anxiety has lasted two weeks or more or is interfering with your life, rather than trying to exercise your way through it alone. Also seek help if exercise has become compulsive or is tied to anxiety about food or weight. Check with a doctor before starting vigorous exercise if you have a heart condition or get chest pain on exertion.

> [!NOTE]
> If you are thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^9]

## The bottom line

Regular physical activity reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety and lowers the risk of developing depression, although the best trials are imperfect. Start with ten minutes of something you can tolerate, build toward 150 minutes a week, and make it harder as you get fitter. Keep your doctor, your therapy and your medication in the picture.
