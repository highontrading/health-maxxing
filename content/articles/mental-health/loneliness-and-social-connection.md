Loneliness has an image problem. We treat it as something slightly embarrassing, a sign that a person is unlikeable or not trying hard enough. Biology tells a different story. Loneliness is a signal, like hunger or thirst, that a basic need is not being met.

When that signal sounds for months or years, it becomes a health issue in the full sense of the term. This explainer covers what the research shows, why a feeling can affect your heart, and which ways of reconnecting have evidence behind them.

## Lonely and isolated are different things

The World Health Organization (WHO) separates two ideas that often get blurred:[^1]

- **Loneliness** is the painful feeling that arises from a gap between the social connection you want and the connection you have.
- **Social isolation** is the objective lack of enough social contacts and relationships.

You can live alone, see few people and feel fine. You can also be surrounded by colleagues, family or a partner and feel deeply lonely. Both states matter for health, and they call for somewhat different fixes: the first for more contact, the second often for closer or better contact.

## How common it is

Very. A WHO commission reported in 2025 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide is affected by loneliness. Rates are highest among teenagers and young adults and in low-income countries, where about 24% of people report feeling lonely, roughly twice the rate in high-income countries.[^1]

In the United States, the Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on the subject noted that about half of adults reported experiencing loneliness.[^2]

> The share of people worldwide affected by loneliness, according to the WHO Commission on Social Connection.[^1]

## What it does to your health

The strongest evidence concerns the bluntest outcome: staying alive.

A meta-analysis of 70 studies that followed about 3.4 million people found that the likelihood of dying during follow-up was 26% higher for people who reported loneliness, 29% higher for those who were socially isolated and 32% higher for those living alone.[^3] An earlier analysis of 148 studies approached it from the other side: people with stronger social relationships were 50% more likely to survive over the study period, an effect its authors described as comparable to quitting smoking.[^4]

Specific conditions show the same pattern:

- **Heart disease and stroke.** Poor social relationships were associated with a 29% higher risk of coronary heart disease and a 32% higher risk of stroke.[^5]
- **Dementia.** Chronic loneliness and isolation are linked with about a 50% higher risk of dementia in older adults.[^2]
- **Depression.** People who are lonely are about twice as likely to become depressed.[^1]

WHO estimates that loneliness is linked to around 871,000 deaths a year worldwide, about 100 every hour.[^1]

A reality check on these numbers: they come from observational studies, which cannot prove cause and effect. Illness itself can isolate people, so some of the link runs backwards. The much-quoted claim that loneliness is "as bad as 15 cigarettes a day" is a comparison of statistical effect sizes from the Surgeon General's advisory, not a measured equivalence.[^2] What is not in doubt is how consistent the finding is across many countries and decades.

## Why a feeling affects the body

The Surgeon General's advisory describes three routes from social disconnection to disease:[^2]

- **Biology.** Humans evolved in groups, and being cut off registers as a threat. Stress hormones stay raised and inflammation increases.
- **Behavior.** Connected people tend to sleep better, move more, smoke less and stick with medical treatment. People around you notice when something is wrong and nudge you to deal with it.
- **Psychology.** Relationships supply meaning and purpose, and they soften the impact of [stress](/articles/stress-and-your-body).

There is also a trap. Loneliness makes people more alert to social threat and more likely to expect rejection, so they withdraw, which deepens the loneliness.[^6] Knowing about that loop is the first step in breaking it.

## What helps

### Question the lonely mind-set

A meta-analysis of loneliness programs compared four approaches: improving social skills, increasing social support, creating more opportunities for contact, and changing unhelpful thinking about social situations. The last approach had the largest effect.[^6]

In practice, this means catching thoughts such as "they would not want to hear from me" or "I would only be a burden" and testing them. They are often wrong.

### Make contact regular

One-off catch-ups do less than a standing arrangement. The Surgeon General's advice is to invest in relationships through consistent, frequent, good-quality contact and to take time each day to reach out to a friend or family member.[^2] A weekly call, a fixed walk or a monthly dinner removes the effort of arranging each meeting.

### Do things alongside other people

Shared activity gives friendship something to grow from. The advisory recommends taking part in groups built around fitness, hobbies, faith, work or community service.[^2] A class, a choir, a running club or a team gives you repeated contact with the same faces and something to talk about.

### Volunteer

Helping others connects you with them. In a review of 40 studies, volunteers had a lower risk of death than non-volunteers and reported less depression and greater life satisfaction. Trials that randomly assigned people to volunteer have not confirmed the effect, so healthier people may simply volunteer more.[^7] The downside risk is small.

### Take small talk seriously

Brief exchanges with people you barely know count for more than you would expect. In one study, people felt happier and a greater sense of belonging on days when they had more of these "weak tie" interactions than usual.[^8]

We also misjudge how these moments will go. In experiments with commuters in Chicago, people predicted that talking to a stranger would make the journey worse. Those who were asked to do it reported a more pleasant commute than those who sat in silence.[^9]

> [!TIP] The two-minute version
> Today, send one message to someone you have not spoken to in a while, with no agenda beyond "thinking of you". Then say more than "thanks" to one person who serves you. That is a full dose of practice.

## Technology: a mixed blessing

Phones can hold relationships together across distance, and for people who are housebound they can be a lifeline. They can also crowd out the real thing.

In a study of 1,787 young adults in the United States, those who used social media for more than two hours a day had about twice the odds of feeling socially isolated as those who used it for less than 30 minutes.[^10] The study was a snapshot, so it cannot say whether heavy use causes isolation or lonely people simply scroll more.

A workable rule: use technology to arrange and deepen contact, such as calls, video chats and plans to meet, and be wary of passive scrolling that replaces it. We look at this in [screen time and digital wellbeing](/articles/screen-time-and-digital-wellbeing).

## When loneliness needs more than a plan

Loneliness and [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment) feed each other, and [social anxiety](/articles/understanding-anxiety) can make every suggestion above feel impossible. Those are treatable conditions, and treating them often makes reconnection possible.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if loneliness comes with low mood on most days, loss of interest, trouble sleeping, or a fear of social situations that stops you from doing things. Bereavement, a move, retirement and new parenthood are common triggers, and asking for support through them is sensible, not weak.

> [!NOTE]
> If you feel hopeless or are thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^11]

## The bottom line

Chronic loneliness is common and bad for your health, and it is not a personal failing. Treat connection the way you treat exercise: schedule it, start small and keep it regular. Reach out to one person this week, join one thing that meets repeatedly, and get professional help if low mood or anxiety is standing in the way.
