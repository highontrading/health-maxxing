Meditation has traveled from monasteries to phone apps and picked up some extravagant claims on the way: that it rewires your brain, cures anxiety, sharpens focus and makes you a calmer person by Thursday.

The research is more modest and more interesting. There is decent evidence that mindfulness programs ease anxiety, low mood and pain, one strong trial in which a mindfulness course held its own against a standard medicine, and honest evidence of limits and occasional harms. Here is where the science stands.

## What mindfulness is

Meditation is an umbrella term for practices that train attention and awareness. Some involve focusing on a particular sensation, such as the breath, a sound or a repeated word. Mindfulness is one form: keeping your attention on the present moment without making judgments about it.[^1]

In practice, that usually means resting your attention on your breathing, noticing when your mind has wandered off, and bringing it back. Then doing that again, a few hundred times. The wandering is not a failure. The returning is the exercise.

Most of the research has tested two structured courses:[^1]

- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR)**, an eight-week group program that combines meditation with discussion and other strategies.
- **Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT)**, which blends mindfulness practice with elements of cognitive behavioral therapy.

These are substantial commitments. The MBSR course used in one major trial involved weekly classes of two and a half hours, a day-long retreat and 45 minutes of home practice a day.[^3]

## What the best evidence shows

The most widely cited summary is a 2014 systematic review of 47 randomized trials with 3,515 participants. It graded both the size of the effect and the strength of the evidence:[^2]

| Outcome | What the trials showed |
| --- | --- |
| Anxiety | Moderate evidence of improvement |
| Depression | Moderate evidence of improvement |
| Pain | Moderate evidence of improvement |
| Stress and quality of life | Low-strength evidence of improvement |
| Sleep, attention, weight, substance use | No effect, or too little evidence to tell |

Two caveats keep this in proportion. The improvements were small to moderate in size. And the reviewers found no evidence that meditation programs worked better than other active treatments, such as medication, exercise or other behavioral therapies.[^2]

That makes mindfulness one reasonable option among several, alongside the likes of [exercise](/articles/exercise-for-mental-health), not a superior one.

## Head to head with a medicine

The most rigorous single test came in 2023. Researchers randomly assigned 276 adults with anxiety disorders to an eight-week MBSR course or to escitalopram, a commonly prescribed SSRI antidepressant.[^3]

After eight weeks, anxiety had fallen by a similar amount in both groups, and the course met the trial's standard for being "non-inferior" to the drug. Side effects differed sharply: about 79% of the medication group reported at least one, compared with about 15% of the mindfulness group.[^3]

Some honest footnotes. There was no placebo group. Participants knew which treatment they were getting. And the course demanded far more time than swallowing a tablet, which will not suit everyone.

## Preventing depression from coming back

MBCT was designed for people who have recovered from depression and want to stay well. A pooled analysis of nine trials and 1,258 patients found that those who took MBCT were about 31% less likely to relapse over the following 60 weeks than those who did not.[^4]

On that basis, England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) includes MBCT among its options for preventing relapse, and lists group mindfulness and meditation as one choice for less severe depression.[^5]

## Where it falls short

Mindfulness is not a universal upgrade. When a large UK trial taught it in schools to about 8,000 students aged 11 to 14, it did no better than normal teaching at improving mental health or well-being a year later.[^6]

One reading is that mindfulness helps most when people have a problem they want to work on. Offered to everyone as prevention, the benefits are far less clear.

## Can meditation make you feel worse?

Sometimes. Sitting quietly with your own mind is not always pleasant.

A systematic review of 83 studies with 6,703 participants found that about 8% reported an unwanted effect. Anxiety and low mood were the most common.[^7] The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that this is similar to the rate seen with psychological therapies.[^1]

> [!STAT] 8%
> The share of participants in meditation studies who reported an unwanted effect, most often anxiety or low mood.[^7]

A closer look came from a study of 96 people taking eight-week mindfulness programs. Asked in detail, 58% reported at least one unpleasant meditation-related effect, such as anxiety, emotional numbness or the return of distressing memories. For most these were brief, but 6% had effects that lasted more than a month.[^8]

> [!WARNING]
> If you have a history of trauma, psychosis, or severe depression or anxiety, talk to a clinician before starting, and be especially careful with intensive retreats. If a practice brings panic, flashbacks or a feeling of being detached from yourself, stop and seek advice. Ending a session early is fine.

## A five-minute practice to try

1. **Sit comfortably**, on a chair or cushion, with your back upright but not stiff. Set a timer for five minutes.
2. **Close your eyes** or lower your gaze. Take two slow breaths, then let your breathing find its own rhythm.
3. **Pick an anchor.** Notice the sensation of breathing wherever it is clearest: the nostrils, the chest or the belly.
4. **Notice the wandering.** Within seconds your mind will be elsewhere. That is normal. Note where it went, without scolding yourself, and return to the breath.
5. **Finish gently.** When the timer sounds, notice how your body feels before you get up.

> [!TIP] Make it stick
> Tie the practice to something you already do every day, such as your first coffee. Five minutes daily beats thirty minutes once a week. In one small study, 13 minutes of guided meditation a day improved mood and attention after eight weeks, but not after four, so give it time.[^10]

## Apps or courses?

Courses have the strongest evidence. The trials above used eight-week programs led by trained teachers, with a group and daily homework.

Apps are cheaper and easier to fit into a day. A meta-analysis of randomized trials of mindfulness apps found small-to-moderate improvements in stress, anxiety and low mood.[^9] A sensible route is to start with an app or the five-minute practice above, and to look for an MBSR or MBCT course if you want to go further or are dealing with a specific problem.

## Not a substitute for treatment

Mindfulness can sit alongside therapy or medication, and for some people a structured course is a treatment in its own right. But do not use it to replace conventional care or to put off seeing a professional about a problem.[^1] If you have symptoms of [anxiety](/articles/understanding-anxiety) or [depression](/articles/depression-signs-and-treatment) that are affecting your life, see a doctor, and do not stop prescribed medication because meditation seems to be helping without discussing it first.

> [!NOTE]
> If you are thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists free, confidential services by country.[^11]

## The bottom line

Mindfulness is a trainable skill with real but modest benefits for anxiety, low mood and pain, roughly on a par with other active treatments. Try five minutes a day for a couple of months, and consider a structured course if it helps. Treat it as one tool for [managing stress](/articles/stress-and-your-body), and keep professional care in the picture when symptoms are serious.
