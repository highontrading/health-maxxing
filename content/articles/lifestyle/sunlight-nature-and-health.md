"Get some fresh air" is advice your grandmother gave without a single citation. It turns out she was largely right, although the science is patchier than wellness marketing suggests: strong in a few places, suggestive in most, and thin in some.

This piece sorts the solid from the hopeful. It covers time in green space, daylight and your body clock, children's eyesight, vitamin D and sun safety, and the fashionable practice of forest bathing.

## The 120-minute finding

The most quoted number in this field comes from a 2019 study of 19,806 adults in England. People who had spent at least 120 minutes in natural settings such as parks, woods and beaches in the previous week were noticeably more likely to report good health and high well-being than those who had spent none.[^1]

> [!STAT] 120 min
> The weekly time in nature at which better self-reported health and well-being appeared. Benefits peaked at about 200 to 300 minutes a week.[^1]

Below two hours there was no clear benefit. It made no difference whether people got there in one long outing or several short ones, and the pattern held for older adults and people with long-term illnesses.[^1]

The caveat is important. This was a snapshot survey based on what people reported, so it cannot show that nature caused the better health. People who feel well may simply get out more.

## What else we know about green space

Other research points the same way, with the same limitation.

A meta-analysis of 143 studies linked greater exposure to green space with lower stress hormone levels, heart rate and blood pressure, and with lower rates of type 2 diabetes and death from cardiovascular disease.[^2] A separate analysis of nine long-term studies covering more than 8 million people found that living in a greener neighborhood was associated with a slightly lower risk of early death: about 4% lower for each step up in a satellite measure of greenness.[^3]

Why might greenery help? Probably through several routes at once. Green areas invite walking and play, bring people together, and tend to be cooler, quieter and less polluted. Untangling those from the effect of nature itself is hard, and wealthier areas are often greener.

That is why one experiment stands out. In Philadelphia, researchers randomly chose which of several hundred derelict vacant lots would be cleaned up and planted with grass and trees. Eighteen months later, residents living near the greened lots were about 40% less likely to report feeling depressed than those near untouched lots.[^4] It is a single trial that relied on self-report, but the random design makes cause and effect much more plausible.

## Daylight keeps your body clock on time

Light is the main signal your brain uses to set its roughly 24-hour clock, and daylight is in a different league from indoor light. A typical lit room is dim by comparison: outdoor light is many times brighter, even on an overcast day.[^5]

An international expert group has recommended that adults get bright, blue-enriched light during the day, far more than most indoor spaces supply, and keep light low in the three hours before bed and as dark as possible during sleep. The group names daylight as the best daytime source.[^5]

Mood seems to follow. Among more than 400,000 UK adults, those who spent more time outdoors in daylight reported fewer depressive symptoms, less tiredness and easier mornings.[^6] That is observational evidence, but bright light is also an established treatment: for winter-pattern seasonal affective disorder, sitting in front of a 10,000-lux light box for 30 to 45 minutes each morning is a standard therapy.[^7]

> [!TIP] Morning is the best time
> Get outside within an hour or two of waking when you can, even for ten minutes. Morning light anchors your body clock, which helps you feel alert in the day and sleepy at night. See our guide to [circadian rhythm and light](/articles/circadian-rhythm-and-light).

## Outdoor time protects children's eyesight

Nearsightedness (myopia) has become far more common, especially in East Asia, and here the evidence for outdoor time is experimental. In a trial across 12 elementary schools in Guangzhou, China, six schools added one 40-minute outdoor class to every school day. After three years, 30.4% of children in those schools had become nearsighted, compared with 39.5% in the schools that carried on as normal.[^8]

Bright outdoor light is the leading explanation. For parents, this is one of the most practical findings in the field: daily outdoor play is good for eyes as well as for fitness and sleep.

## Sunlight and vitamin D without the burn

Your skin makes vitamin D when ultraviolet B rays reach it. How much depends on the season, latitude, time of day, cloud, skin tone and age: darker skin and older skin produce less, and glass blocks UVB altogether. Some researchers suggest that about 5 to 30 minutes of sun on the face, arms and legs around the middle of the day, at least twice a week, is usually enough.[^9]

The same rays cause skin cancer, sunburn and premature skin aging, and contribute to cataracts.[^10] So the sensible position is a balance, not a tan:

- **Short and regular beats long and rare.** There is no benefit to vitamin D in staying out until you redden.
- **Check the UV index.** The WHO advises sun protection when it reaches 3 or above: shade around midday, clothing, a hat, sunglasses and broad-spectrum sunscreen.[^10]
- **Use food and supplements as a backstop.** Where winter sun is too weak, or if you cover up or have darker skin, diet and supplements are a safe way to meet the recommended 15 micrograms (600 IU) a day for most adults.[^9]

## Forest bathing: pleasant, modestly proven

Shinrin-yoku, or forest bathing, means spending slow, attentive time among trees. A meta-analysis found that levels of the stress hormone cortisol were lower after time in a forest than after time in a city. It also found that cortisol fell before the forest visit had begun, suggesting that looking forward to a nice day out does part of the work. Most studies were small and at high risk of bias.[^11]

Claims that forest air boosts immunity rest on even thinner evidence. Enjoy the woods for what they reliably offer: quiet, movement and a break from screens.

## Getting your dose in a city

- Walk or cycle through a park on the way to work, even if it adds ten minutes.
- Take lunch, a phone call or a meeting outside.
- Move your workout outdoors once or twice a week.
- Use weekends for one longer visit to water, woods or hills.
- For children, protect daily outdoor play, whatever the weather.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor about a mole or patch of skin that is new, changing, itching or bleeding, as our guide to [skin cancer and mole checks](/articles/skin-cancer-and-mole-checks) explains. Also seek help for low mood that lasts more than two weeks or returns each winter. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact local emergency services or a crisis line straight away.

## The bottom line

Aim for about two hours a week somewhere green and some daylight every morning, and get children outside daily. Enjoy the sun in short spells and protect your skin when the UV index is 3 or more. The evidence is not perfect, but the cost is a pair of shoes and the side effects are mostly good.
