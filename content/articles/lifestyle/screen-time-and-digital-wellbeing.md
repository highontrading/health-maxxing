Depending on which headline you read last, screens are either rewiring our brains or a moral panic on a par with the fear of novels. The research is less dramatic than both, and more useful.

This piece sets out what studies do and do not show about screens and well-being, where the evidence of harm is strongest (sleep and tired eyes), what the guidance says for children, and which boundaries are worth your effort.

## What the research actually shows

Start with the biggest question: does screen time make people unhappy? In one of the most rigorous analyses, researchers examined three large surveys covering more than 350,000 adolescents. Digital technology use was negatively associated with well-being, but it explained at most 0.4% of the variation between teenagers. For comparison, the association was about the same size as that for regularly eating potatoes.[^1]

A small average does not mean no one is harmed. An average can hide a minority who are badly affected and others who benefit. The US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on social media noted that teenagers who spent more than three hours a day on social media had about double the risk of symptoms of depression and anxiety in one study, and concluded that there is not yet enough evidence to call social media sufficiently safe for young people.[^2]

Both findings are correlations. Unhappy or lonely people may turn to their phones more, so cause could run in either direction.

The fair summary: total hours are a blunt measure. What seems to matter more is the content (connecting with friends is not the same as late-night doom-scrolling), the timing, and what the screen time displaces.[^2]

## Sleep: the clearest cost

If screens harm health through one main route, it is probably sleep. A 2024 National Sleep Foundation expert panel reviewed the evidence and agreed that screen use in general, and the content viewed before bed in particular, impair sleep health in children and adolescents. The panel also agreed that behavioral strategies can reduce the damage.[^3]

Notice what is driving this. Light from the screen gets most of the attention, but the panel's clearest agreement was about content: an engaging feed keeps you awake past your bedtime and leaves your mind busy when you finally put it down.[^3]

That points to boundaries that work with human nature. Charge your phone outside the bedroom, use a cheap alarm clock, and set a wind-down time. Our guide to [sleep habits that work](/articles/sleep-hygiene-habits-that-work) covers the rest.

## Eyes: strain is real, lasting damage is not

Hours of screen work can leave your eyes dry, tired and blurry, sometimes with a headache. Eye doctors call this digital eye strain. The main cause is mundane: people blink far less than usual when staring at a screen, so the surface of the eye dries out.[^4] The symptoms are temporary, and there is no good evidence that screen light damages the eye.[^4]

> [!TIP] The 20-20-20 habit
> Every 20 minutes, look at something about 6 meters (20 feet) away for 20 seconds.[^4] Position the screen about an arm's length away and slightly below eye level, reduce glare, and use lubricating eye drops if your eyes feel dry.[^5]

Blue-light glasses are a different story. A 2023 Cochrane review of 17 randomized trials concluded that blue-light filtering lenses may make no difference to eye strain compared with ordinary lenses, and that any effect on sleep was unclear.[^6] The American Academy of Ophthalmology does not recommend them for computer use.[^4]

For children there is one longer-term concern: nearsightedness (myopia) is becoming more common, and it is linked with lots of close-up work and too little time outdoors.[^12] Daylight appears protective, as we explain in [sunlight, nature and health](/articles/sunlight-nature-and-health).

## Necks, shoulders and sitting still

Screens also keep us still. Aches in the neck and shoulders are common after long spells hunched over a device, and the remedy is the same as for desk work: raise the screen, change position often and take movement breaks. See our guides to [posture and tech neck](/articles/posture-and-tech-neck) and [sitting all day](/articles/sitting-all-day-what-to-do).

## Children and teenagers

Guidance is firmest for the youngest children, whose development depends on sleep, active play and face-to-face interaction.

| Age | Guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Under 2 | WHO: no sedentary screen time. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) makes an exception for video calls with family |
| 2 to 4 | WHO: no more than 1 hour a day, and less is better. AAP: choose high-quality programs and watch together |
| School age and teens | AAP: no single hours limit. Agree consistent family rules and protect sleep, physical activity and homework |

The WHO figures come from its 2019 guidelines for children under five, which emphasize replacing screen time with active play and good sleep.[^7] The AAP's advice for young children is similar,[^8] and for older children it recommends a written family media plan in place of a universal limit.[^9]

For teenagers, the questions worth asking are about function more than minutes. Are they sleeping enough? Still seeing friends in person, moving and keeping up with school? Do they seem worse after time online, or are they hiding what they do there? The Surgeon General's advisory highlights harmful content, harassment and lost sleep as the main risks, and suggests keeping family mealtimes and bedrooms device-free.[^2]

## Boundaries worth trying

Few digital habits have been tested in good trials, so treat most tips as cheap experiments. One exception: in a randomized trial of 467 adults, blocking mobile internet on their phones for two weeks, while leaving calls and texts working, improved sustained attention, mental health and life satisfaction. Participants spent more time socializing in person, exercising and outdoors. Many found the block hard to keep up, which is itself informative.[^10]

Lower-effort versions of the same idea:

- **Bedroom and table rules.** No phones in the bedroom overnight or at meals.
- **Prune notifications.** Leave on only those from real people who need you.
- **Add friction.** Log out of the stickiest apps, move them off your home screen, or try a grayscale display.
- **Schedule it.** Batch email and social media into set windows instead of grazing.
- **Swap, do not just stop.** Decide what the freed time is for: a walk, a call, a book.

These changes stick better when they become routine, which is the subject of our guide to [building habits](/articles/how-to-build-habits-that-stick).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See an optometrist or doctor for eye discomfort that does not ease with breaks, persistent blurred or double vision, or frequent headaches. Seek help from a doctor or mental health professional if you or your child cannot cut back despite real problems with sleep, school, work or relationships, or if time online is tied up with low mood, anxiety, bullying or self-harm. Anyone thinking about harming themselves should contact local emergency services or a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline lists services by country.[^11]

## The bottom line

Stop counting hours and start asking what your screen time is costing you. Protect sleep first by keeping devices out of the bedroom, give your eyes regular breaks, and skip the blue-light glasses. For children, hold the line on sleep, play and time with people, and keep under-5s' screen time minimal.
