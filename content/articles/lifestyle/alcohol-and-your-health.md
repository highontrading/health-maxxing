For decades, one of the most comforting lines in health reporting was that a glass of red wine is good for your heart. It has not aged well.

The research has shifted, and official positions with it: the World Health Organization now says that no level of drinking is free of risk.[^1] This piece explains what that does and does not mean, why the heart-health story came apart, how national guidelines compare, and what to do if drinking less is proving hard.

## What "no safe level" really means

In January 2023 the WHO's European office published a statement in The Lancet Public Health saying that, when it comes to alcohol, there is no safe amount that does not affect health.[^1]

The reasoning is narrower than the headline. To call a level "safe" you would need evidence that drinking at or below it carries no risk of illness or injury. For cancer, no such threshold has been found: the risk appears to start with small amounts and climb from there.[^1]

That is not the same as saying one drink is as dangerous as ten. The same statement stresses that the less you drink, the safer you are.[^1] Think of a slope rather than a cliff edge.

## The cancer link is the firmest part

The International Agency for Research on Cancer has placed alcohol in Group 1, its category for things known to cause cancer in humans, for decades.[^1] The label describes how sure scientists are, not how potent something is. Alcohol is linked to at least seven cancers: breast (in women), bowel, liver, esophagus, mouth, throat and voice box.[^2]

> [!STAT] 7 cancers
> Alcohol raises the risk of at least seven types of cancer. In the United States it is the third leading preventable cause of cancer, after tobacco and obesity.[^2]

Absolute numbers are more useful than scary-sounding relative ones. The 2025 US Surgeon General's advisory estimated the lifetime risk of developing one of these alcohol-related cancers at different drinking levels:[^2]

| Average intake | Women | Men |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Less than 1 drink a week | 16.5% | 10.0% |
| 1 drink a day | 19.0% | 11.4% |
| 2 drinks a day | 21.8% | 13.1% |

In plain terms, a drink a day means about 2.5 extra cancers for every 100 women over a lifetime, and about 1.4 for every 100 men. That is a real increase, not a dramatic one, and it grows as intake rises. If [breast cancer](/conditions/breast-cancer) runs in your family, alcohol is one of the few risk factors you can change.

## What happened to the heart benefit?

Older studies kept finding a J-shaped curve: moderate drinkers seemed to live longer than people who did not drink at all. Two problems have weakened that picture.

**The "sick quitter" problem.** Many studies lumped lifelong non-drinkers together with people who had stopped drinking, often because of poor health. That flatters moderate drinkers by comparison. A 2023 meta-analysis of 107 cohort studies, covering more than 4.8 million people, corrected for this. It found no significant reduction in the risk of death for people drinking less than 25 g of alcohol a day, just under two US standard drinks. Risk was clearly higher from 25 g a day in women and 45 g a day in men.[^3]

**The healthy-lifestyle problem.** In a study of 371,463 UK adults, light-to-moderate drinkers exercised more, ate more vegetables and smoked less than abstainers, and accounting for those habits shrank the apparent heart benefit. The researchers also used Mendelian randomization, which compares people whose genes nudge them toward drinking more or less and is harder to fool with lifestyle differences. Genetically predicted drinking was linked with more high blood pressure and coronary artery disease: small increases at light intake, much steeper ones at heavier intake.[^4]

The debate is not completely closed. A 2024 review by the US National Academies concluded, with moderate certainty, that moderate drinking was associated with a lower death rate from any cause than never drinking, and also with a higher risk of breast cancer. None of its conclusions reached high certainty.[^5]

Either way, the WHO found no studies showing that any heart or diabetes benefit of light drinking outweighs the cancer risk at the same level, so "for my health" is not a good reason to drink.[^1] Our guide to [heart disease risk factors](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors) covers better-proven options.

## How the guidelines compare

A "drink" is not the same size everywhere. A US standard drink contains 14 g of pure alcohol: about 355 mL (12 fl oz) of 5% beer, 150 mL (5 fl oz) of 12% wine or 45 mL (1.5 fl oz) of 40% spirits.[^9] A Canadian standard drink is about the same, at roughly 13 g.[^7] A UK unit is smaller, at 8 g, so a 175 mL glass of 12% wine is about 2 units.[^6]

| Who | Advice for adults | Alcohol a week |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UK Chief Medical Officers | No more than 14 units a week, spread over 3 or more days | About 112 g |
| Canada's Guidance (2023) | 2 drinks or fewer is low risk, 3 to 6 moderate, 7 or more increasingly high | Under 30 g at the low-risk line |
| US Dietary Guidelines (2025 to 2030) | "Consume less alcohol for better overall health" | No number given |

The US change is recent. Earlier editions capped intake at two drinks a day for men and one for women.[^2] The edition released in January 2026 dropped the numbers, while still telling some groups to avoid alcohol completely, including pregnant women, people recovering from alcohol use disorder and people taking medicines that interact with alcohol.[^8] UK and Canadian guidance also say that not drinking is the safest choice in pregnancy.[^6][^7]

The numbers differ, but the direction is the same everywhere: less is better.

## What you gain by cutting down

Because cancer risk tracks the amount you drink, every reduction lowers it, whatever your starting point.[^2]

Blood pressure responds too, mainly in heavier drinkers. In a meta-analysis of 36 trials with 2,865 participants, people who drank more than two drinks a day lowered their [blood pressure](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) when they cut back. Those who started at six or more drinks a day and roughly halved their intake saw average falls of 5.5 mm Hg in systolic and 4 mm Hg in diastolic pressure. People at two drinks a day or fewer saw no significant change.[^10]

Canada's guidance also lists better sleep among the benefits of not drinking,[^7] which we cover in [caffeine, alcohol and sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep).

> [!TIP] Small changes that add up
> Plan several drink-free days each week, as UK guidance suggests.[^6] Measure your pours at home, since a customary serving is often bigger than a standard drink.[^9] Choose lower-strength options, and decide your number before the evening starts.

## When drinking becomes hard to control

Alcohol use disorder is a medical condition, not a character flaw, in which a person finds it hard to stop or control their drinking despite the problems it causes. Signs that it is worth talking to a professional include:[^11]

- Often drinking more, or for longer, than you meant to.
- Wanting to cut down, or trying to, and not managing it.
- Strong cravings, or needing more alcohol to get the same effect.
- Drinking that gets in the way of work, family or things you used to enjoy.
- Feeling shaky, sweaty, anxious or unable to sleep when the alcohol wears off.

Clinicians count how many of 11 such criteria applied in the past year: two or three points to a mild disorder, four or five to moderate, and six or more to severe.[^11] Volume is another rough check: US experts define heavy drinking as 8 or more drinks a week for women and 15 or more for men.[^9]

Treatment works, and there is more than one kind: talking therapies, mutual-support groups and prescription medicines that reduce craving or help people stay off alcohol.[^11] Your own doctor is a good first stop. In the US, the NIAAA Alcohol Treatment Navigator helps people find evidence-based care.[^11] In the UK, a GP can refer you to local alcohol services.[^12] Most countries also have a national alcohol helpline.

> [!WARNING] Do not stop suddenly if you may be dependent
> If you drink heavily every day, or get withdrawal symptoms such as shaking, sweating or anxiety when you cut back, get medical advice before you stop. Stopping abruptly can be dangerous.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number if someone withdrawing from alcohol has a seizure, or sees or hears things that are not there.[^12] See a doctor if you cannot cut down despite trying, or if drinking is affecting your mood, work or relationships. If you are thinking about harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line straight away.

## The bottom line

Alcohol is not the heart tonic it was once made out to be, and it carries a real cancer risk that grows with the amount you drink. You do not have to quit to benefit: drinking less is worthwhile at any level. If cutting down feels harder than it should, that is a medical issue with effective treatments, and asking for help is the sensible move.
