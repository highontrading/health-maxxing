"Sitting is the new smoking" is a great slogan and a poor summary of the science. Smoking is far worse. But the grain of truth is worth taking seriously: if your day runs from car seat to desk chair to sofa, your health pays a price, and a gym session three times a week may not fully cover it.

The useful news is that the fix is neither heroic nor expensive. This guide covers how risky sitting really is, how much movement offsets it, what short breaks do, and where standing desks fit.

## How risky is sitting, really?

Large studies consistently link long sitting with earlier death, heart disease and type 2 diabetes. The details matter, though.

The clearest picture comes from a 2016 analysis that pooled data on more than a million adults. People who sat for more than eight hours a day and did almost no physical activity had a 59% higher risk of death during follow-up than the most active people who sat for less than four hours.[^2] Studies that measured movement with wearable trackers, rather than relying on memory, found that risk started to climb more steeply beyond about nine and a half hours of sedentary time a day.[^3]

Two cautions. First, these are observational studies. People who are already unwell sit more, which can make sitting look more harmful than it is. Second, the risk was concentrated among people who did little activity. That second point is the good news.

## The offset: how much movement cancels it out

In the same million-person analysis, people who did about 60 to 75 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a day showed no increased risk of death from sitting more than eight hours.[^2] Brisk walking and cycling at an easy pace count as moderate.

That is a lot, and it relied on self-reported activity, which people tend to overestimate. When researchers repeated the exercise with tracker data from more than 44,000 adults, about 30 to 40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity was enough to substantially weaken the link between sitting and early death.[^4]

> [!STAT] 30-40 min
> The daily amount of moderate-to-vigorous activity that largely offset long sitting in tracker-based studies.[^4]

This matches the WHO's advice: limit sedentary time, replace it with movement of any intensity, and if you sit a lot, aim for more than the minimum 150 minutes of activity a week.[^1] Our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) shows how to build that into a week.

## Break it up: what short breaks do

Separate from your total activity, there is the question of long unbroken stretches. Here the evidence comes from small, tightly controlled lab studies that measure blood markers over a single day.

In one, 19 overweight adults who interrupted sitting with two minutes of walking every 20 minutes had after-meal blood sugar responses about a quarter lower, and lower insulin levels, than on a day of uninterrupted sitting.[^6]

A 2023 trial tested different "doses" of break in 11 middle-aged and older adults. Five minutes of light walking every 30 minutes cut the blood sugar spike after a meal by more than half compared with sitting all day. Every walking pattern tested, including one minute every hour, lowered systolic blood pressure by 4 to 5 mm Hg.[^5]

These studies are short and small, and they measure markers, not heart attacks. Still, the direction is consistent, the cost is close to zero, and the WHO guideline points the same way.[^1]

> [!TIP] A realistic target
> Stand up and move for two to five minutes every 30 to 60 minutes. Fetch water, climb a flight of stairs, or walk while you take a call. If you cannot leave your desk, stand and do calf raises or a few squats.

## Do standing desks help?

They do one thing reliably: reduce sitting. A Cochrane review of workplace trials found that sit-stand desks cut sitting at work by about 100 minutes a day in the first three months, and by about an hour over the longer term. The evidence was low quality, and effects on health were unclear.[^7]

Standing still is not the same as moving. A tracker study of more than 80,000 UK adults found that time spent standing was not associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, and that standing for more than two hours a day was linked with a higher risk of circulation problems in the legs, such as varicose veins.[^8] That is observational evidence and needs confirming, but it fits common sense: the benefit comes from muscles working, not from being vertical.

So treat a standing desk as a way to change position and a nudge to walk off, not as exercise.

## Movement snacks for a working day

Short bursts add up. Among adults who did no formal exercise, those whose trackers recorded about three brief bouts of vigorous everyday activity a day, each a minute or two long, had a markedly lower risk of death than those with none.[^11] It is an association, not proof, but an encouraging one.

Ideas that fit around work:

- Take the stairs, fast, whenever there are fewer than four floors.
- Walk for ten minutes after lunch, when blood sugar is rising.
- Stand or pace for phone calls and one-to-one meetings.
- Get off public transport a stop early, or park at the far end.
- Keep a resistance band or kettlebell where you can see it.

If you track [steps](/articles/how-many-steps-a-day), use the hourly count as a prompt instead of only the daily total.

## Set up your desk so sitting hurts less

No chair makes eight motionless hours healthy, but a decent setup reduces aches.[^9]

- Feet flat on the floor or on a footrest, with thighs and hips supported.
- Back supported, shoulders relaxed, elbows close to your body and bent at roughly 90 to 120 degrees.
- Screen directly in front of you at about arm's length, with the top at or just below eye level.
- Change position often. The best posture is your next one.

UK workplace guidance adds that short, frequent breaks from screen work are better than occasional long ones, for example 5 to 10 minutes every hour.[^10] For neck and shoulder trouble, see our guide to [posture and tech neck](/articles/posture-and-tech-neck).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly if you develop throbbing pain, swelling, warmth or redness in one leg, usually the calf, especially after a long period of not moving. These can be signs of a blood clot. Call your local emergency number if leg symptoms come with sudden breathlessness or chest pain.[^12] Back pain with numbness, leg weakness or bladder changes also needs urgent assessment.

## The bottom line

Sitting matters most when it replaces movement entirely. Aim for at least 30 to 40 minutes of brisk activity most days, interrupt long sitting spells every half hour to an hour, and use a standing desk, if you have one, as a reason to shift and walk. None of this requires a gym, only the habit of getting up.
