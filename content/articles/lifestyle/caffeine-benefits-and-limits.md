Caffeine is the most widely used mind-altering substance on the planet, and most of us take it without reading the label. That is mostly fine. At ordinary doses it is one of the better-studied and safer things in your kitchen.

It is also a real drug, with a dose, a duration and side effects. This piece covers how it works, how much is in what you drink, where the limits sit, what it does well, and the fine print on sleep, anxiety, pregnancy and energy drinks.

## How caffeine works

Through the day, a chemical called adenosine builds up in your brain and makes you feel sleepy. Caffeine blocks the receptors adenosine acts on, so the "tired" signal is muffled for a while. It does not create energy. It hides fatigue.

Caffeine reaches its peak level in your blood within about an hour, and you can feel its effects for four to six hours.[^3] Its half-life, the time your body takes to clear half of it, is also about four to six hours, which means a mid-afternoon coffee is still partly on board at bedtime.[^1] People differ a great deal in how fast they clear caffeine, and pregnancy and some medicines slow it down.[^1]

## How much is in your cup

Amounts vary with the bean, the brew and the size of the mug, so treat these as typical figures.[^1][^11]

| Drink or food | Typical caffeine |
| --- | --- |
| Mug of filter coffee | 80 to 140 mg |
| Mug of instant coffee | About 100 mg |
| Mug of tea | 30 to 75 mg |
| Can of cola (330 to 355 mL) | 30 to 40 mg |
| Energy drink (250 mL can) | About 80 mg, and much more in large or "extra strength" cans |
| Decaf coffee | 2 to 15 mg |
| Dark chocolate (50 g) | Less than 25 mg |

Coffee-shop servings can be far larger than a home mug, and energy shots, pre-workout powders and some painkillers add to the total.

## Where the limits are

| Who | Advised limit |
| --- | --- |
| Most healthy adults | Up to 400 mg a day, and no more than about 200 mg in one go |
| Pregnant or breastfeeding | Up to 200 mg a day |
| Children and teenagers | About 3 mg per kg of body weight a day at most. Pediatricians advise avoiding energy drinks altogether |

The adult figure comes from the US Food and Drug Administration, which says 400 mg a day is not generally associated with negative effects, and from the European Food Safety Authority, which reached the same number and added the single-dose and pregnancy figures.[^1][^2] The NHS also advises a 200 mg ceiling in pregnancy, because higher intakes are linked with complications such as low birth weight.[^11] Our guide to [pregnancy nutrition and exercise](/articles/pregnancy-nutrition-and-exercise) covers other foods and drinks.

> [!NOTE]
> These are ceilings for the average healthy adult, not targets. If one cup makes you jittery, your personal limit is lower, and that is normal.

## What caffeine does well

**Alertness.** Caffeine improves attention, vigilance and reaction time, most noticeably when you are tired.[^5]

**Exercise performance.** The International Society of Sports Nutrition concludes that 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight, taken about 60 minutes before exercise, consistently improves performance. Endurance benefits most, with smaller gains in strength and sprinting. Very high doses, around 9 mg per kg, bring more side effects and no extra benefit.[^5] For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, the low end is about 210 mg, or two cups of coffee. Note that the upper end of that range would exceed the 400 mg daily guideline, so more is not better.

**Long-term health, with a caveat.** A BMJ umbrella review pulling together more than 200 meta-analyses found that coffee drinking was more often linked with benefit than harm. The largest difference appeared at three to four cups a day, which was associated with a 17% lower risk of death from any cause compared with drinking none, along with lower rates of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and liver disease.[^4]

> [!STAT] 3-4 cups
> The daily coffee intake linked with the lowest risk of early death in pooled observational studies. It is an association, not proof that coffee is protective.[^4]

The caveat matters. These were almost all observational studies, and coffee drinkers differ from non-drinkers in many ways. The authors' conclusion was modest: coffee appears generally safe at usual intakes, except in pregnancy and possibly for women at high risk of fracture.[^4] It is a reason not to worry about your coffee, not a reason to start drinking it.

## The fine print

### Sleep

This is where caffeine quietly costs most people something. In a controlled trial, 400 mg taken six hours before bedtime reduced total sleep by more than an hour, and participants largely did not notice the difference.[^6] A 2023 meta-analysis found caffeine shortened sleep by about 45 minutes on average and reduced deep sleep. It estimated that a cup of coffee should be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed, and a typical pre-workout supplement more than 13 hours before, to avoid losing sleep.[^7]

A randomized trial that assigned 100 adults to coffee or no-coffee days found the same trade-off in daily life: on coffee days people took about 1,000 more steps and slept about half an hour less.[^8] For more, see [caffeine, alcohol and sleep](/articles/caffeine-alcohol-and-sleep).

### Anxiety, heart and stomach

Too much caffeine can cause restlessness, shakiness, a fast heartbeat, headaches and an upset stomach, and it can worsen anxiety and [heartburn](/articles/acid-reflux-and-heartburn).[^3] In the trial above, coffee did not increase the early atrial heartbeats that researchers were watching for, which is reassuring for most people.[^8] If you have an [anxiety disorder](/articles/understanding-anxiety), a heart rhythm problem, high blood pressure or reflux, your tolerance may be lower, and it is worth testing how you feel with less.[^3]

### Dependence and withdrawal

Regular use leads to mild physical dependence. Stop abruptly and symptoms start within 12 to 24 hours, peak at 20 to 51 hours and last two to nine days. Headache is the most common, affecting about half of people, followed by fatigue, low mood and difficulty concentrating.[^9] Cutting down gradually over a week or two avoids most of it.

## Energy drinks and caffeine powder

Energy drinks combine caffeine with sugar and other stimulants, are easy to drink fast, and are marketed to the young. The American Academy of Pediatrics says they have no place in the diets of children and adolescents.[^10] Mixing them with alcohol is a bad idea at any age, because caffeine masks how drunk you feel without making you any less impaired.[^3]

> [!WARNING] Pure caffeine products can kill
> Powdered or highly concentrated liquid caffeine sold as a supplement is extremely potent. One teaspoon of the pure powder can contain as much caffeine as about 28 cups of coffee, and seizures can occur after rapidly taking around 1,200 mg. A kitchen spoon cannot measure a safe dose. Avoid these products.[^1]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number or a poison center if someone has taken a large amount of caffeine and develops chest pain, a very fast or irregular heartbeat, vomiting, confusion or a seizure. See a doctor if you get palpitations, persistent anxiety or insomnia that does not improve when you cut back. If you are pregnant or take regular medicines, ask your doctor or pharmacist how much caffeine is appropriate for you.

## The bottom line

For most adults, up to 400 mg a day is safe, and moderate coffee drinking is nothing to feel guilty about. Keep it to the morning and early afternoon to protect your sleep, halve the limit in pregnancy, keep energy drinks away from children, and never buy pure caffeine powder. If caffeine makes you anxious or sleepless, less is the right dose for you.
