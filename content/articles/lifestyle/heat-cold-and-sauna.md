Sitting in a very hot room and then jumping into very cold water is an old human pastime. It has recently been rebranded as a longevity protocol, with barrels, apps and precise temperature targets.

Some of the enthusiasm is earned. Sauna research is more interesting than most wellness trends, though it has serious limits. The evidence for cold plunges is thinner, and both carry risks that social media tends to skip. Here is a level look at both, plus the basics of heat illness that everyone should know.

## Sauna: what the Finnish data show

The landmark study followed 2,315 middle-aged men in eastern Finland for about 20 years. Compared with men who took one sauna a week, those who went two to three times a week had a 24% lower risk of death from any cause, and those who went four to seven times a week had a 40% lower risk. Deaths from cardiovascular disease and sudden cardiac death followed the same pattern, and longer sessions were linked with fewer fatal heart events than short ones.[^1]

> [!STAT] 40%
> The lower risk of death over about 20 years among Finnish men who used a sauna four to seven times a week, compared with once a week. This is an association, not proof of cause.[^1]

Those are striking numbers, and they deserve caution.

- **It is observational.** Men who sauna almost daily may also be healthier, wealthier, less stressed or more sociable. Men who are already unwell may go less often.
- **It is one population.** In Finland nearly everyone uses a sauna, so there was almost no "never" group, and most follow-up findings on blood pressure, stroke and dementia come from the same cohort.[^2]
- **It is a specific kind of sauna.** The data apply to traditional Finnish saunas: dry air at about 80 to 100 °C (176 to 212 °F), for 5 to 20 minutes at a time. Infrared cabins and steam rooms are far less studied.[^2]

There is a plausible mechanism. In a hot sauna your skin blood vessels open wide, your heart rate climbs to 100 to 150 beats a minute, roughly what it does during moderate exercise, and blood pressure tends to fall afterwards.[^2] Short experiments support those effects. What nobody has done is a long randomized trial showing that sauna use prevents heart attacks.

A fair summary: if you enjoy saunas, the evidence suggests they are safe for most people and may be good for you. They are an addition to [exercise](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need), not a replacement.

## Cold plunges: modest and mixed

Cold-water immersion has far more testimonials than trials. A 2025 systematic review found only 11 studies in healthy adults, with 3,177 participants in total. Its findings were time-dependent and mixed: stress levels were lower about 12 hours after immersion but not immediately, some studies reported better sleep and quality of life, and there was no significant effect on mood. Markers of inflammation actually rose straight afterwards.[^4]

Most of those participants came from one Dutch trial of cold showers. About 3,000 adults finished their usual warm shower with 30 to 90 seconds of cold water for a month. They took 29% fewer sick days from work than the control group, but reported just as many days of feeling ill, so the cold may have changed how people coped more than how often they got sick.[^5]

For sore muscles, the picture is a little clearer. A Cochrane review of 17 small trials found that cold-water immersion after exercise reduced muscle soreness over the following days compared with resting, although the studies were of low quality.[^6] Our guide to [rest and recovery](/articles/rest-recovery-and-overtraining) covers other options.

There is a catch for anyone lifting weights. In a 12-week trial, men who sat in 10 °C (50 °F) water for 10 minutes after every strength session gained less muscle and strength than men who did an easy warm-down instead. Cold appears to dampen the signals that tell muscle to grow.[^7]

> [!TIP] Keep cold away from strength sessions
> If building muscle matters to you, save cold plunges for rest days, or leave several hours between lifting and cold water. Using cold to feel fresher during a tournament or a heavy week of endurance training is a different situation, where short-term recovery is the goal.

Claims that cold exposure burns meaningful fat, strengthens immunity or extends life remain unproven.[^8]

## The risks people skip

**In the sauna**, serious problems are uncommon in healthy people. The biggest avoidable danger is alcohol, which raises the risk of low blood pressure, heart rhythm disturbances and accidents. People with unstable angina, a recent heart attack or severe narrowing of the aortic valve are advised not to use saunas, while most people with stable heart disease tolerate them well.[^3]

**In cold water**, the danger arrives in the first minute. Sudden immersion triggers the cold shock response: an involuntary gasp, rapid uncontrollable breathing and a surge in heart rate and blood pressure. If your head is under water when you gasp, you can inhale water and drown, and the strain can trigger dangerous heart rhythms in vulnerable people. The response is strongest in water below about 15 °C (59 °F) and eases after a minute or two.[^8]

> [!WARNING]
> Never plunge into cold water alone, after drinking alcohol, or by jumping or diving in. Enter slowly, keep your head above water and focus on slow breathing until the first minute passes. Open water adds currents and the risk of losing the strength to swim.[^8]

## Heat illness: know the signs

Whether the heat comes from a sauna, a workout or a heatwave, the warning signs are the same. Heat is the leading cause of weather-related deaths worldwide, and older adults, young children and people with long-term conditions are most at risk.[^10]

**Heat exhaustion** causes tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating, cramps, intense thirst and a fast pulse. Move the person somewhere cool, remove extra clothing, give them water or a sports drink, and cool their skin with water and a fan. They should start to feel better within 30 minutes.[^9]

**Heatstroke** is an emergency. Call your local emergency number if someone is still unwell after 30 minutes of cooling, has a very high temperature, has hot skin that is not sweating, is breathing fast, or is confused, having a seizure or unresponsive.[^9]

## Sensible ways to start

| | Sauna | Cold water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start with | 5 to 10 minutes on a lower bench | A cool finish to your shower, 30 to 90 seconds |
| Build to | 15 to 20 minutes, a few times a week | 1 to 3 minutes in water at 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59 °F) |
| Always | Drink water, cool down gradually, skip alcohol | Enter slowly, stay with someone, warm up afterwards with dry clothes and movement |
| Stop if | You feel dizzy, sick or unwell | You cannot control your breathing, feel chest pain, or start shivering hard |

The sauna and shower figures mirror how the studies were run.[^2][^5] The plunge figures are deliberately cautious, because no trial has shown that colder or longer is better.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Check with a doctor before using saunas or cold plunges if you have heart disease, a heart rhythm problem, high or low blood pressure, a history of fainting, or if you are pregnant. Call your local emergency number for chest pain, collapse, confusion or breathing difficulty during or after either one.

## The bottom line

Saunas have encouraging but unproven links with heart health and are safe for most people who skip the alcohol. Cold plunges offer modest, short-lived benefits and a real risk in the first minute. Treat both as optional extras you do because you like them, layered on top of the basics: movement, sleep and not smoking.
