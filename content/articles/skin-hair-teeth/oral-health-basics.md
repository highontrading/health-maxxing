The most common health condition in the world is not a virus or back pain. It is untreated tooth decay. Taken together, diseases of the mouth affect close to 3.5 billion people, according to the World Health Organization's 2022 global oral health status report.[^1]

Most of that is preventable with a routine that takes about four minutes a day and costs very little. This guide covers what that routine is, which upgrades are worth it, what gum disease does, and how far the claims about your mouth and the rest of your body hold up.

## The routine that does the work

**Brush twice a day for about two minutes with fluoride toothpaste.** Make one of those times last thing at night.[^2] Fluoride is the active ingredient. A Cochrane review of dozens of trials confirmed that fluoride toothpaste prevents decay compared with fluoride-free paste, and that in children and adolescents stronger pastes prevent more.[^3] The NHS advises adults to use a toothpaste with at least 1,350 parts per million (ppm) of fluoride, a figure printed on the tube.[^2]

**Spit, do not rinse.** After brushing, spit out the excess and leave it at that. Rinsing with water, or with mouthwash, washes away the concentrated fluoride that would otherwise keep working on your teeth.[^2]

**Clean between your teeth once a day.** A brush cannot reach the surfaces where teeth touch. Use interdental brushes where they fit and floss where they do not.[^2]

**Be thorough, not forceful.** Cover the outside, inside and biting surface of every tooth, and angle the bristles toward the gumline. Scrubbing harder does not clean better.

> [!TIP] Small children need less paste
> The NHS advises a smear of fluoride toothpaste for children under 3 and a pea-sized amount from 3 to 6, with an adult helping or supervising.[^2]

## Electric or manual? Floss or brushes?

Both questions have been tested, and the answers are less dramatic than the advertising.

A Cochrane review of more than 50 trials found that powered toothbrushes reduced plaque by 11% after one to three months and by 21% after more than three months, compared with manual brushing. Gum inflammation fell by 6% and 11% over the same periods. The authors could not say how much those differences matter for long-term dental health.[^4] The NHS position is practical: either type is fine as long as you clean every surface, and some people find an electric brush makes that easier.[^2]

> [!STAT] 21%
> The extra reduction in plaque with powered toothbrushes compared with manual ones after more than three months of use, in a Cochrane review.[^4]

For cleaning between teeth, another Cochrane review found that adding floss or interdental brushes to toothbrushing may reduce gum inflammation and plaque more than brushing alone, and that interdental brushes may do better than floss. The certainty of that evidence was rated low to very low, and most of the trials were short.[^5] That is weaker proof than you might expect for such standard advice, but the logic is sound and the cost is small.

## Sugar: how often matters

Decay starts when bacteria in dental plaque turn sugars into acid, which dissolves minerals from the tooth surface. Every sugary snack or drink sets off another round, so frequency counts as well as quantity.[^6]

The WHO recommends keeping free sugars, meaning sugar added to food and drink plus the sugar in honey, syrups and fruit juice, below 10% of your daily energy intake, and suggests that below 5% gives further protection against decay.[^6] For an adult eating 2,000 calories, 5% is about 25 g, or six teaspoons. A single can of sugary soda can exceed that, and the label will tell you: see [how to read a nutrition label](/articles/how-to-read-a-nutrition-label).

Two habits make that easier: keep sweet foods and drinks to mealtimes instead of grazing on them, and make water your default drink in between.

## Gum disease: from bleeding gums to lost teeth

Gum disease comes in two stages.[^7]

| Stage | What is happening | Signs | Reversible? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gingivitis | Plaque inflames the gums | Red, swollen gums that bleed when you brush | Yes, with good cleaning |
| Periodontitis | Inflammation destroys the tissue and bone holding teeth in place | Receding gums, bad breath, loose teeth | Damage is permanent, but treatment can halt it |

Severe gum disease affects about 19% of adults worldwide, more than a billion people.[^1] Smoking is the most significant risk factor, and diabetes raises the risk too.[^7] If your gums bleed, the instinct is to brush that area less. Do the opposite: clean more carefully, and if the bleeding has not stopped after a week or two, see a dentist. More detail is on our [gum disease](/conditions/gum-disease) page.

## Does your mouth affect the rest of your body?

This is where claims run ahead of evidence, so it is worth separating two cases.

**Diabetes: reasonably good evidence.** Gum disease and diabetes make each other worse. A Cochrane review of 35 randomized trials found that treating periodontitis lowered HbA1c, the standard measure of long-term blood sugar, by about 0.4 percentage points after three to four months, with moderate certainty.[^8] That is enough to matter clinically. If you have [type 2 diabetes](/conditions/type-2-diabetes), dental care is part of your treatment.

**Heart disease: an association, not proof.** People with gum disease have more heart disease, and that link holds after accounting for shared risk factors such as smoking. But an American Heart Association scientific statement concluded that the evidence does not show that gum disease causes cardiovascular disease, or that treating it prevents heart attacks or strokes.[^9]

So look after your gums for the sake of your teeth, and count any wider benefit as a possible bonus.

## Check-ups, mouthwash and whitening

**Check-ups.** The six-month rule is tradition more than science. NICE guidance in the UK sets the interval between check-ups for adults anywhere from 3 months to 2 years, depending on individual risk.[^10] A Cochrane review found little or no difference in decay, gum health or quality of life between six-monthly and risk-based check-ups in adults over four years.[^11]

**Mouthwash.** Optional. A fluoride mouthwash can add protection if you are prone to decay, but use it at a different time from brushing, not straight afterward.[^2]

**Whitening.** Peroxide-based whitening is generally safe when a dental professional provides or prescribes it. The NHS notes that it does not work on dentures, crowns, fillings or veneers, that results are not permanent, and that home kits and salon treatments carry more risk of gum irritation and poor results.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a dentist for gums that keep bleeding, a loose adult tooth, toothache lasting more than a day or two, or a mouth ulcer, lump or red or white patch that has not healed after three weeks. Go to an emergency department, or call your local emergency number, if swelling in your face or neck is spreading, or makes it hard to breathe, swallow or open your mouth.

## The bottom line

Brush twice a day for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste, spit without rinsing, and clean between your teeth daily. Keep sugar to mealtimes, [quit smoking](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) if you smoke, and see a dentist as often as your own risk warrants. It is one of the cheapest and best-proven habits in all of health.
