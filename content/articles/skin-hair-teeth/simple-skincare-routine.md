Most bathroom shelves hold more skincare than skin needs. Strip away the marketing and a routine that covers the essentials has three steps, takes about two minutes, and can come from any pharmacy.

Beyond that, a short list of active ingredients has real clinical trials behind it, and one of them, the retinoid family, is worth learning to use properly. This guide covers the basics, the extras that earn their place, and the claims you can ignore.

## The three essentials

**A gentle cleanser.** The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) advice is refreshingly plain: use a mild, non-abrasive cleanser without alcohol, wet your face with lukewarm water, apply with your fingertips, and skip the scrubbing, which irritates skin. Wash in the morning, at night and after heavy sweating. More is not better.[^1]

**A moisturizer.** Use one if your skin feels dry, tight or itchy.[^1] It works best on skin that is still slightly damp. Thick creams and ointments hold water in better than thin lotions, which matters if your skin is very dry.

**Sunscreen.** Broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher, on exposed skin every morning.[^2] This is the step with the strongest evidence: in a randomized trial of 903 Australian adults, those assigned to daily sunscreen showed 24% less skin aging over four and a half years than those who used it at their own discretion.[^3] Our [sunscreen guide](/articles/sunscreen-guide) covers how much to use.

Put together, the whole routine looks like this:

| Step | Morning | Evening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Cleanser | Cleanser |
| 2 | Treatment product, if you use one | Treatment product, if you use one |
| 3 | Moisturizer | Moisturizer |
| 4 | Sunscreen | None |

## Active ingredients with evidence behind them

"Actives" are the ingredients meant to change something in the skin, not just clean or coat it. These are the ones worth knowing:

| Ingredient | Mainly used for | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene, retinol) | Acne, fine lines, uneven tone | Strongest |
| Azelaic acid | Acne, dark marks | Moderate |
| Salicylic acid (a BHA) | Blackheads, clogged pores | Limited to moderate |
| Niacinamide | Blotchiness, spots, fine lines | Limited |
| Vitamin C | Dark spots, dullness | Limited |
| AHAs (glycolic, lactic acid) | Rough texture | Limited |

**Retinoids** are vitamin A relatives and the best-studied group. A controlled trial published in 1988 showed that tretinoin cream improved sun-damaged facial skin over 16 weeks, and decades of studies have followed.[^4] For acne, the AAD's 2024 guidelines strongly recommend topical retinoids, alongside benzoyl peroxide.[^6] Tretinoin needs a prescription. Adapalene 0.1% gel is sold over the counter in the US, and retinol is a weaker cosmetic cousin.[^5]

**Azelaic acid and salicylic acid** both get a conditional recommendation for acne in those guidelines, meaning they help but the evidence is thinner.[^6]

**Niacinamide** (vitamin B3) is gentle and popular. In its best-known trial, 50 women used a 5% niacinamide moisturizer on half the face for 12 weeks and saw modest improvements in fine lines, dark spots and red blotchiness. The study was run by a cosmetics manufacturer.[^7]

**Vitamin C** is an antioxidant with small studies suggesting it can fade dark spots and improve sun-damaged skin. Its weakness is chemistry: the active form, L-ascorbic acid, breaks down easily when exposed to air and light, so formulation and packaging matter.[^8]

**AHAs** such as glycolic and lactic acid loosen dead cells at the surface. They also make skin more sensitive to the sun, so the FDA advises sun protection while you use them.[^9]

Add one active at a time and give it a fair trial. The AAD notes that most products need at least six weeks to work, and some need three months.[^2]

## How to start a retinoid

Retinoids work, but they commonly cause dryness, redness and peeling in the first weeks. Going slowly gets most people through it.[^5]

1. **Pick one.** For acne, adapalene. For signs of sun damage, retinol, or ask a doctor about tretinoin.
2. **Apply it at night** to clean, dry skin. A pea-sized amount covers the whole face. More only adds irritation.
3. **Start with every second or third night** for a couple of weeks, then increase as your skin allows.
4. **Moisturize.** If your skin is sensitive, apply moisturizer first and the retinoid on top.
5. **Wear sunscreen every morning.** Retinoids can make skin more sun-sensitive.
6. **Hold the other actives** such as AHAs and scrubs until your skin has settled.
7. **Give it three months** before you judge the result.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not use retinoids if you are pregnant or trying to conceive. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists lists topical retinoids and isotretinoin among the treatments to avoid in pregnancy. Benzoyl peroxide, azelaic acid, topical salicylic acid and glycolic acid are considered acceptable over-the-counter options.[^10]

## Adjust for your skin

**Oily or acne-prone.** Choose products labeled oil-free or non-comedogenic (formulated not to clog pores) and a light gel or lotion moisturizer. Salicylic acid, benzoyl peroxide or adapalene fit here. Our [acne guide](/articles/acne-causes-and-treatments) goes deeper.

**Dry.** Use a creamy cleanser, wash with lukewarm rather than hot water, and choose a thicker cream. Introduce actives slowly, if at all.

**Sensitive or reactive.** Fewer products, all fragrance-free, and patch test anything new. Skin that stings, flushes or flakes with most products may have a condition such as [rosacea](/conditions/rosacea) or [eczema](/conditions/eczema) that needs its own treatment.

**Prone to dark marks.** Marks left behind by pimples or irritation are more common in deeper skin tones. Daily sunscreen, a retinoid and azelaic acid are the usual tools, and not picking helps most of all.

## What is mostly marketing

**"Hypoallergenic."** In the US, no federal standard or definition governs the term. The FDA says manufacturers can use it to mean whatever they want it to mean.[^11]

**Price.** The AAD is blunt on this point: effective products come in every price range, and expensive does not mean better.[^2]

**Long routines.** Every additional product is another chance of irritation, and layering several new ones makes it impossible to tell which caused a problem. A ten-step routine is a hobby, not a medical need.

**Before-and-after photos.** A moisturizer can plump fine lines within minutes, which looks impressive and changes nothing underneath.[^2]

> [!TIP] Patch test before you commit
> The AAD method: apply the product to a small patch of skin, such as the inner arm or the bend of the elbow, twice a day for 7 to 10 days. If there is no redness, itching or swelling by then, use it on your face.[^12]

## When a routine is not enough

Skincare products manage normal skin. They do not treat skin disease.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor or dermatologist if acne is leaving scars or has not improved after about three months of over-the-counter treatment, if a rash keeps coming back, or if you have persistent facial redness, flaking or sores that do not heal. Any mole that is new or changing deserves a look: see our [mole check guide](/articles/skin-cancer-and-mole-checks). Get urgent help if a product causes swelling of the lips, face or throat, or trouble breathing.

## The bottom line

Cleanse gently, moisturize if you need to, and wear sunscreen every morning. If you want more, add a retinoid slowly and give it three months. Everything else is optional, and the label on the front of the bottle tells you less than the ingredient list on the back.
