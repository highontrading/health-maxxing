Plenty of creams promise younger-looking skin on the strength of a small, short study, if that. Sunscreen is the exception. It was put through a randomized trial of more than 1,600 adults that ran for four and a half years, with researchers still counting cancers a decade after it ended.[^1][^2]

The results are why dermatologists sound like a broken record on the subject. This guide covers what the label means, what the trial found, how to apply enough for the number on the bottle to be true, and what the argument over ingredients is really about.

## What the label is telling you

Sunlight carries two kinds of ultraviolet (UV) radiation that reach your skin. UVB is the main cause of sunburn. UVA is tied to premature skin aging, such as wrinkles and age spots.[^3] UV exposure, from the sun or from sunbeds, is the main cause of skin cancer.[^4]

Three terms on the bottle matter:[^5][^6]

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| SPF | Protection against UVB only. SPF 30 filters about 97% of UVB and SPF 50 about 98%. |
| Broad spectrum | The product also passed a test for UVA protection. |
| Water resistant | It keeps working through 40 or 80 minutes of swimming or sweating, as the label states. Nothing is waterproof. |

SPF, the sun protection factor, comes from a test of how much UV it takes to redden protected skin compared with bare skin.[^5] It is a filter strength, not a time allowance: a higher number does not buy you a longer safe stay in the sun.[^6]

In the US, only broad-spectrum sunscreens of SPF 15 or higher may claim to lower the risk of skin cancer and early skin aging, and only as part of wider sun protection. Anything else can claim to help prevent sunburn and nothing more.[^5] The American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recommends broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher and water resistance.[^3]

## What the trial found

Nambour is a town in Queensland, Australia. In 1992, researchers randomly assigned 1,621 of its residents, aged 25 to 75, to one of two groups: apply sunscreen to the head and arms every day, or carry on using it whenever they saw fit.[^1]

**Skin cancer.** Ten years after the trial ended, 11 people in the daily group had developed a new melanoma, compared with 22 in the discretionary group. That halving sat right at the edge of statistical significance, so chance cannot be ruled out. For invasive melanomas, those that have grown beyond the skin's top layer, the count was 3 versus 11: a 73% reduction, though one based on very few cases.[^1]

**Skin aging.** Among 903 participants younger than 55, skin aging over the trial was 24% less in the daily group. In fact, the daily users showed no detectable increase in skin aging across four and a half years. Beta-carotene supplements, tested in the same trial, had no overall effect.[^2]

> [!STAT] 24%
> How much less skin aging daily sunscreen users showed over 4.5 years, compared with people who applied it at their own discretion.[^2]

The caveats: this is one trial in one town, melanoma was a secondary outcome rather than the main question, and the aging study had only modest power to detect moderate effects.[^1][^2] Even so, a randomized trial with a decade of follow-up is rare on the beauty shelf. For the short list of other products with decent evidence, see our [simple skincare routine](/articles/simple-skincare-routine).

## How to apply it so the label is true

The SPF on the bottle is measured with 2 mg of sunscreen on every square centimeter of skin. In real life, people put on about 25% to 50% of that, which means they get less protection than the label promises.[^7]

To close the gap:

- **Use enough.** An adult needs about 30 mL (1 ounce), roughly a full shot glass, to cover the body. Larger bodies need more.[^3][^7]
- **Apply early.** Put it on dry skin about 15 minutes before you go out.[^8]
- **Cover the usual misses.** Ears, neck, the tops of your feet, and your lips, which need a balm with SPF 30 or higher.[^8]
- **Reapply.** At least every two hours outdoors, and sooner after swimming or sweating, whatever the SPF.[^5]
- **Ignore the clouds.** The FDA recommends sunscreen on cloudy days too.[^5]

> [!TIP] The amounts, in kitchen terms
> Body: a full shot glass, or about two tablespoons. Face: a dollop roughly the size of a US nickel, about 2 cm across.[^7] A 200 mL bottle holds only six or seven full-body applications, so if yours lasts all summer, you are probably using too little.

## Mineral or chemical, and is it safe?

Sunscreen filters come in two families. Mineral filters, zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, sit on the surface of the skin. Chemical filters, such as avobenzone and oxybenzone, soak up UV rays like a sponge. Both protect you. The AAD suggests mineral formulas for sensitive skin and for infants and toddlers, because they are less likely to irritate.[^3]

On safety, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has proposed that zinc oxide and titanium dioxide be classed as generally recognized as safe and effective. For 12 other filters, including avobenzone, oxybenzone and octocrylene, it has asked manufacturers for more data. The agency stresses that this is a request for evidence, not a finding that the ingredients are unsafe.[^9]

Part of the reason is absorption. In an FDA-led randomized trial of 48 adults, all six chemical filters tested reached blood levels above 0.5 ng/mL, the threshold at which the agency expects further safety studies, after a single application.[^10] Nobody yet knows whether that matters for health: the researchers called for further study, and the AAD points out that being absorbed is not the same as being harmful.[^3][^10]

> [!NOTE]
> Sunscreen rules differ by country, and the list of permitted filters changes. In June 2026 the FDA added a new filter, bemotrizinol, to its list of permitted active ingredients.[^9] If you would rather avoid the filters still under review, mineral sunscreens are a simple alternative.

## Darker skin, babies and vitamin D

**Darker skin.** More melanin lowers the risk of skin cancer but does not remove it, and the AAD recommends sunscreen for everyone.[^3] The more common everyday concern is pigmentation. Visible light, not just UV, can worsen melasma and dark spots, particularly in darker skin tones, so dermatologists suggest a tinted sunscreen that contains iron oxide and has an SPF of 30 or higher.[^11]

**Babies and children.** For babies under 6 months, the FDA's advice is shade and protective clothing rather than sunscreen, keeping them out of the sun around the middle of the day, and asking a health professional before applying any product. From 6 months, use sunscreen as directed on the label.[^5]

**Vitamin D.** In lab experiments with artificial UV, sunscreen does cut vitamin D production. But a 2019 review concluded that the real-world risk is low: randomized field trials found no effect of daily sunscreen on vitamin D levels, and observational studies mostly found none either. The catch is that the trial sunscreens were moderate, around SPF 16, and today's high-SPF products have not been tested the same way.[^12] If you are worried about [vitamin D deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-d-deficiency), a blood test will tell you more than skipping sunscreen will.

## Sunscreen is the backup, not the plan

No sunscreen blocks everything, which is why the AAD and the FDA both pair it with shade and clothing.[^3][^5] The World Health Organization's rule of thumb is to protect your skin whenever the UV index, a forecast measure of UV strength, reaches 3 or above.[^4]

On those days, limit your time in the midday sun, wear long sleeves, a broad-brimmed hat and sunglasses, and use sunscreen on whatever is still exposed.[^5] Daylight has its upsides too, which we cover in [sunlight, nature and health](/articles/sunlight-nature-and-health).

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor about any mole or patch of skin that is new, changing, itching or bleeding. Our [mole check guide](/articles/skin-cancer-and-mole-checks) shows what to look for. Get medical advice for any sunburn in a baby, and for a sunburn with widespread blistering, fever, chills, confusion or faintness. If a sunscreen gives you a rash, stop using it and ask a pharmacist or doctor about alternatives.

## The bottom line

Pick a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher that you like enough to use, apply a full shot glass for the body, and reapply every two hours outdoors. Treat it as one layer alongside shade, clothing and a hat. The evidence that this lowers skin cancer risk and slows visible aging is better than most products on the shelf can offer.
