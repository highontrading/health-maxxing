Beauty supplements make a tidy promise: swallow this, and your skin, hair and nails will improve from the inside. It is an appealing idea and a profitable one. The evidence behind it ranges from "some, with caveats" to "essentially none".

This explainer goes through the main products one at a time, says how good the research is, flags the one safety issue that even careful people miss, and ends with what demonstrably works better.

## The short version

| Supplement | What the research shows | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collagen peptides | Small trials show modest gains in skin hydration and elasticity. The effect looks weaker in independent trials | Possible small benefit |
| Biotin | No good trials in people without a deficiency | Skip unless deficient |
| Hair, skin and nail blends | Rarely tested as sold | Skip, and check the doses |
| Vitamin C, zinc, iron | Help when a deficiency is corrected | Test first |

## Collagen: a signal, heavily sponsored

Collagen is the main structural protein in skin, and the body makes less of it with age. Collagen supplements are that protein from cattle, pigs, fish or chicken, broken into small fragments called peptides so that it dissolves and digests easily.

The first thing to understand is that your gut does not deliver those fragments to your face. Collagen is digested like any other protein into amino acids and short peptides, which the body then uses wherever it needs them. The claim is that some of those peptides signal skin cells to make more collagen. That is plausible, and not settled.

What do trials show? A 2021 meta-analysis pooled 19 randomized trials with 1,125 participants, almost all women, and found that hydrolyzed collagen taken for around 90 days improved skin hydration, elasticity and wrinkles compared with placebo.[^1] A 2023 meta-analysis of 26 trials reached a similar conclusion for hydration and elasticity.[^2]

Three cautions belong next to those numbers:

- **The trials are small and short.** Most ran for 8 to 12 weeks, and nobody knows what happens over years.
- **The outcomes are instrument readings.** Hydration and elasticity are measured with devices on the skin. A statistically significant change on a probe is not the same as looking younger.
- **Funding matters.** Many trials were paid for by companies that sell collagen. A 2025 meta-analysis found that the benefit was no longer evident when the analysis was limited to trials without industry funding, or to those of higher quality.[^3]

Safety is the reassuring part: the trials report few side effects.[^1][^2] So collagen is a low-risk purchase with an uncertain payoff. If you try it, a trial of 12 weeks with before-and-after photos in the same light is a fair test.

> [!NOTE]
> In the US, supplements do not need approval from the Food and Drug Administration before they are sold, and manufacturers do not have to prove that a product works. Seals from independent testers such as USP or NSF confirm that a product contains what the label says. They do not show that it does anything.[^4]

## Biotin: thin evidence, real downside

Biotin is a B vitamin, and severe deficiency does cause hair loss and brittle nails. That observation built an industry. The catch is that deficiency is rare, because biotin is found in many foods and adults need only about 30 mcg a day.[^5] Hair and nail products often contain 5,000 or 10,000 mcg, more than 150 times that amount.

A review of the published evidence found just 18 reported cases of biotin improving hair or nails, and every one of those patients had an underlying condition that caused poor hair or nail growth in the first place. The authors found no trials supporting biotin in healthy people.[^6]

The downside is specific and under-appreciated. Many laboratory tests use biotin in their chemistry, and high levels in your blood can push results falsely high or falsely low. The FDA has warned about this, highlighting falsely low results on troponin, the test used to diagnose a heart attack. Thyroid tests can be distorted too.[^5]

> [!WARNING]
> If you take biotin, or any hair, skin and nail product that contains it, tell your doctor and the laboratory before a blood test, and ask whether you should stop it beforehand. In an emergency, mention it to the staff treating you.[^5]

## Hair, skin and nail blends

These products usually combine a very large dose of biotin with vitamins A, C and E, zinc and assorted plant extracts. The specific mixtures have rarely been tested in good trials, and stacking them on top of a multivitamin makes it easy to overshoot.

Two ingredients deserve a look at the label:

- **Vitamin A.** Too much preformed vitamin A, the kind listed as retinol or retinyl palmitate, can damage the liver and cause birth defects. The upper limit for adults is 3,000 mcg a day from all sources.[^7]
- **Zinc.** The adult upper limit is 40 mg a day. Taking more for long periods can cause copper deficiency.[^8]

## When a nutrient does matter

Deficiencies are real, and correcting one can make a visible difference.

**Vitamin C** is needed to build collagen. A true deficiency causes scurvy, with bleeding gums and poor wound healing. Adults need 75 to 90 mg a day, an amount that fruit and vegetables supply easily, and smokers need a little more.[^9]

**Zinc** deficiency can cause hair loss, skin rashes and slow wound healing. It is more likely in people with digestive diseases, those who drink heavily, and some vegetarians.[^8]

**Iron** deficiency is linked with hair shedding, as our guide to [hair shedding and thinning](/articles/hair-shedding-and-thinning) explains.

The pattern is consistent: supplements fix shortages. In people who are not short, there is no good evidence that extra improves skin, hair or nails. If you suspect a deficiency, a blood test will tell you more than a gummy will. For the wider picture, see [vitamins and supplements: who needs them](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them).

## What works better

If the goal is skin that ages well, three things have stronger evidence than anything in a capsule.

**Sunscreen.** In a randomized trial of 903 adults, daily sunscreen users showed 24% less skin aging over four and a half years than people who used it at their own discretion.[^10] See our [sunscreen guide](/articles/sunscreen-guide).

**A retinoid.** Prescription tretinoin has improved sun-damaged skin in controlled trials since 1988.[^11] Our [simple skincare routine](/articles/simple-skincare-routine) explains how to start one.

**Not smoking.** Smoking has been linked to premature facial wrinkling for decades, independent of sun exposure.[^12]

Enough sleep and a varied diet with adequate protein are sensible too, though the research tying them to appearance is thinner.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor about sudden or patchy hair loss, nails that change shape or color, or skin changes that come with tiredness, weight change or other symptoms. These can signal thyroid disease, iron deficiency or other conditions that a supplement will not fix. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking supplements if you are pregnant or take regular medication.

## The bottom line

Collagen might give a small improvement in skin hydration and elasticity, and it is unlikely to hurt. Biotin and hair-skin-nail blends are not worth buying unless a test shows you are deficient, and biotin can interfere with blood tests. Spend the money on sunscreen first.
