Skin cancer has one feature that most cancers lack: it grows where you can see it. More than 1.5 million cases were diagnosed worldwide in 2020, and nearly all of them began as a spot that someone could see.[^1]

That makes a mirror and a few minutes a month a reasonable investment. This guide covers the three main types of skin cancer, the signs that separate a harmless mole from a worrying one, how to check yourself properly, and when to hand the job to a professional.

## Three cancers, three levels of urgency

| Type | How it tends to look | How it behaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basal cell carcinoma | A pearly or waxy bump, a pink patch, or a sore that will not heal | The most common. Grows slowly and rarely spreads, but damages nearby tissue if left |
| Squamous cell carcinoma | A firm lump or a scaly, crusted patch | Common. Can spread if untreated |
| Melanoma | A new or changing mole or dark mark | The least common and the most dangerous |

The first two are grouped as non-melanoma skin cancers. They mostly appear on skin that has had years of sun, such as the face, ears, scalp, hands and shoulders, and are usually cured by removing them.[^2]

[Melanoma](/conditions/melanoma) starts in the pigment-making cells and can spread to other organs.[^3] Timing is everything. In US data, five-year survival is above 99% when melanoma is found while still confined to the skin, and around 35% once it has spread to distant parts of the body.[^4]

> [!STAT] 99%
> Five-year relative survival for melanoma found before it has spread, in US cancer registry data.[^4]

## Who should pay closest attention

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, from the sun and from sunbeds, is the main cause of skin cancer.[^1] On top of that, your risk of melanoma is higher if you have:[^3][^5]

- pale skin that burns easily, red or fair hair, or many freckles
- a lot of moles (more than 50) or large, irregular ones
- a history of sunburn, especially in childhood
- a close relative with melanoma, or a previous skin cancer yourself
- a weakened immune system.

## What to look for

Most adults have between 10 and 40 ordinary moles: small, evenly colored, round or oval, with a clear edge.[^5] The ABCDE rule describes how a melanoma tends to differ:[^6]

| Letter | Stands for | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Asymmetry | One half does not match the other |
| B | Border | The edge is ragged, scalloped or blurred |
| C | Color | Several shades of brown, tan or black, sometimes with red, white or blue |
| D | Diameter | Larger than about 6 mm, the width of a pencil eraser, though melanomas can be smaller |
| E | Evolving | Any change in size, shape, color or feel |

Two additions make the rule more useful.

**The ugly duckling.** Your moles tend to resemble one another. One that stands out from the family, whether darker, larger, smaller or just different, is worth showing to a doctor even if it passes the ABCDE test.[^7]

**New spots count.** A meta-analysis found that only about 30% of melanomas arise in an existing mole. The rest appear on previously normal skin.[^8] So do not only watch the moles you know.

For the non-melanoma cancers, the warning sign is simpler: a lump, patch or sore that does not heal within about four weeks, or that keeps bleeding, crusting or coming back.[^2]

## How to do a skin self-check

The Skin Cancer Foundation recommends a head-to-toe check once a month.[^9] You need a bright room, a full-length mirror, a hand mirror and, the first time, your phone camera.

1. **Face and scalp.** Check your face, lips, ears and behind the ears. Part your hair in sections with a comb or hair dryer, or ask someone to look.
2. **Hands and arms.** Palms, backs of the hands, between the fingers and under the nails. Then forearms, elbows, upper arms and underarms.
3. **Front of the body.** Neck, chest and abdomen. Lift the breasts to check the skin underneath.
4. **Back.** With your back to the full-length mirror, use the hand mirror for your neck, shoulders, back, buttocks and the backs of your legs.
5. **Legs and feet.** Sit down and check the fronts of your legs, then your feet: tops, soles, heels, between the toes and under the toenails.
6. **Genital area.** Use the hand mirror.

Photograph anything you want to track, with a coin or ruler beside it for scale. Comparing pictures is far more reliable than memory.

> [!NOTE]
> Be realistic about the evidence. The US Preventive Services Task Force concluded in 2023 that there is not enough evidence to say whether routine skin checks by a clinician, in adults with no symptoms, do more good than harm.[^10] That is a statement about screening everyone. It does not apply to getting a suspicious spot examined, which is always worthwhile.

## Darker skin: check palms, soles and nails

Skin cancer is less common in people with darker skin, but it is often found later, when it is harder to treat. It also turns up in different places: the soles of the feet, the palms, under the nails, the lower legs, the groin and inside the mouth.[^11]

Look for a dark spot or patch that is growing or changing, a sore that will not heal, or a dark line running the length of a fingernail or toenail that was not caused by an injury.[^3][^11] These areas get little sun, so sun protection alone does not prevent them. Looking does.

## What a doctor will do

A skin examination is quick and painless. The doctor looks over your skin, often with a dermatoscope, a handheld magnifier with a light that shows structures beneath the surface.

If a spot looks suspicious, the next step is a biopsy: the area is numbed and all or part of the spot is removed and examined under a microscope. A biopsy is the only way to confirm or rule out skin cancer.[^3] People at high risk may be offered regular checks, sometimes with photographs of the whole skin for comparison.

## Sunbeds and prevention

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classes UV-emitting tanning devices as carcinogenic to humans, the category it reserves for agents with the strongest evidence.[^1] In its review of the evidence, people who first used a sunbed before the age of 35 had a markedly higher risk of melanoma, 75% higher in the agency's pooled analysis.[^12] There is no safe tan from a machine.

Outdoors, shade, clothing, a hat and sunscreen all reduce UV exposure. Our [sunscreen guide](/articles/sunscreen-guide) covers how to use them, [sunlight, nature and health](/articles/sunlight-nature-and-health) covers the benefits of daylight, and [cancer screening explained](/articles/cancer-screening-explained) puts skin checks in the context of other cancers.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor within a couple of weeks for a mole that has changed in size, shape or color, a mole that itches, hurts, bleeds or crusts, a new or unusual mark that has not gone after a few weeks, a sore that has not healed in four weeks, or a dark streak under a nail with no injury to explain it. You do not need to be sure. Being checked and reassured is a good outcome.

## The bottom line

Learn what your own skin looks like, check it about once a month, and photograph anything you are unsure of. Act on change, on newcomers and on the odd one out, and remember the palms, soles and nails. Most spots turn out to be harmless, and the ones that are not are far easier to treat early.
