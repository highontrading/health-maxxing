Menopause is a single day: the point at which you have gone 12 months without a period.[^1] What most people mean by the word is the years around it, when shifting hormones can bring hot flashes, broken sleep, mood changes and a body that suddenly follows new rules.

Those years are also crowded with marketing. This guide covers what is happening, what is typical, how well hormone therapy works and for whom, the non-hormonal options with real evidence, and what to look after once your periods have stopped.

## The timeline

- **Perimenopause** is the lead-up. The ovaries' output of estrogen and progesterone becomes erratic, periods change and symptoms often begin. The US National Institute on Aging says it most often starts between 45 and 55 and usually lasts about seven years, though it can run to 14.[^1]
- **Menopause** is confirmed after 12 months without a period. The average age is 51.[^8]
- **Postmenopause** is everything after that.

Menopause can also arrive abruptly, after surgery to remove the ovaries or after some cancer treatments.[^3] When it happens before 45 it is called early, and before 40 it is called premature ovarian insufficiency. Both deserve medical assessment, and hormone therapy is generally recommended at least until the usual age of menopause.[^4]

If you are over 45 with typical symptoms, you do not need a blood test to confirm what is going on. The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises diagnosing on symptoms alone.[^6] You can still become pregnant during perimenopause, so keep using contraception until your doctor confirms you no longer need it, which is usually at least a year after your final period.[^8]

## Symptoms beyond the hot flash

Hot flashes and night sweats, which doctors call vasomotor symptoms, affect up to 80% of women.[^5] They also last longer than most people expect.

> [!STAT] 7.4 years
> The median total duration of frequent hot flashes and night sweats in a large US study that followed women through the transition. They continued for a median of 4.5 years after the final period.[^7]

Other common symptoms include:[^1][^2]

- periods that become irregular, heavier or lighter
- trouble sleeping
- mood changes, anxiety and low mood
- problems with memory and concentration, often called brain fog
- vaginal dryness, pain during sex and urinary symptoms
- joint aches, palpitations and a lower sex drive

Vaginal and urinary symptoms behave differently from the rest. Hot flashes eventually fade, but these tend to persist or get worse without treatment.[^4]

Not every midlife symptom is menopause. Thyroid problems, depression and sleep disorders can look similar, so mention anything that does not fit the pattern. Our [thyroid guide](/articles/thyroid-101) covers one common look-alike, and our [insomnia guide](/articles/insomnia-what-actually-works) can help if sleep is the main casualty.

## Hormone therapy: the most effective option

Hormone therapy (HT, also called HRT) replaces estrogen. If you still have a uterus you also need a progestogen, because estrogen on its own can overstimulate the lining of the uterus. It comes as tablets, skin patches, gels and sprays.[^4]

The Menopause Society's 2022 position statement is direct: hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes, night sweats and vaginal symptoms, and it prevents bone loss and fractures.[^4] The long-running argument has been about risk, and the answer depends heavily on when you start.

- **Under 60, or within 10 years of menopause, with no contraindications:** the balance of benefits and risks is favorable for treating bothersome symptoms.[^4]
- **Starting after 60, or more than 10 years after menopause:** the balance looks less favorable, because the absolute risks of heart disease, stroke, blood clots and dementia are higher.[^4]

The main risks, according to the same statement:[^4]

- **Breast cancer.** In the large Women's Health Initiative trial, combined estrogen and progestogen therapy was linked with fewer than one extra case of breast cancer per 1,000 women per year of use. Estrogen alone, taken by women who had had a hysterectomy, was not linked with an increase.
- **Blood clots and stroke.** Estrogen tablets raise the risk slightly. Observational studies suggest that patches and gels, which deliver estrogen through the skin, carry a lower risk of clots.

Hormone therapy is generally not advised for women who have had breast cancer, a blood clot, a stroke or a heart attack, or who have unexplained vaginal bleeding or active liver disease.[^4]

> [!NOTE]
> Hormone therapy treats symptoms. It is not a heart or dementia prevention drug: the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends against taking it to prevent chronic conditions after menopause.[^9]

The Menopause Society also advises against custom-compounded "bioidentical" hormones, which are not tested or regulated like approved medicines. Approved products that contain hormones identical to the body's own are widely available.[^4]

## Non-hormonal options that work

If you cannot take hormones, or would rather not, several alternatives have good evidence. The Menopause Society's 2023 review of non-hormonal treatments for hot flashes recommends:[^5]

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and clinical hypnosis.** NICE also recommends CBT as an option for hot flashes, sleep problems and low mood linked to menopause.[^6]
- **Some antidepressants (certain SSRIs and SNRIs), gabapentin and oxybutynin.** These reduce hot flashes, with side effects of their own.
- **Fezolinetant.** This newer non-hormonal medicine acts on the part of the brain that controls body temperature. It requires liver blood tests before and during treatment because of rare cases of liver injury.
- **Weight loss,** for women who are overweight, on more limited evidence.

The same review looked at popular alternatives and does not recommend them for hot flashes, because the evidence is lacking or negative. The list includes herbal supplements, soy products, acupuncture, yoga, exercise, cooling techniques, avoiding triggers and paced breathing.[^5]

That is no reason to stop exercising. Exercise protects your bones, heart and mood. It simply has not been shown to reduce hot flashes.

On supplements, the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says studies of black cohosh and soy isoflavones have given inconsistent results, and black cohosh has been linked with rare cases of liver damage.[^10]

| Option | Helps with | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hormone therapy | Hot flashes, sleep, vaginal symptoms, bone loss | Most effective; best started before 60 or within 10 years of menopause |
| Vaginal estrogen | Dryness, pain during sex, urinary symptoms | Very little reaches the bloodstream |
| Fezolinetant | Hot flashes and night sweats | Needs liver blood tests |
| CBT, clinical hypnosis | Hot flashes, sleep, mood | No drug side effects |

### Vaginal symptoms

Lubricants and vaginal moisturizers are the first step. If they are not enough, low-dose vaginal estrogen as a cream, tablet or ring treats dryness and pain effectively. Very little is absorbed into the bloodstream, and no progestogen is needed with it.[^4]

## After menopause: bones and heart

Estrogen helps maintain bone, so the risk of osteoporosis rises after menopause.[^2] The American Heart Association describes the menopause transition as a period of accelerating cardiovascular risk, with less favorable cholesterol levels and more fat stored around the abdomen.[^11]

The basics matter more from here on: strength and weight-bearing exercise, enough calcium, vitamin D and protein, not smoking, and knowing your blood pressure and cholesterol. Our guides to [osteoporosis and bone health](/articles/osteoporosis-and-bone-health) and [heart disease risk factors](/articles/heart-disease-risk-factors) go deeper.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor about any vaginal bleeding after menopause, however light, and about very heavy, prolonged or between-period bleeding during perimenopause.[^12] Seek advice too if symptoms start before 45, or if they are disrupting your sleep, work or relationships. If low mood turns into thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line or your local emergency number straight away.

## The bottom line

[Menopause](/conditions/menopause) is a normal transition, but you do not have to grit your teeth through it. For most healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of their last period, hormone therapy is the most effective treatment, and its benefits generally outweigh its risks. If you cannot or would rather not take it, CBT, hypnosis, fezolinetant and several other medicines have real evidence. Skip the supplements, treat vaginal symptoms early, and use the transition as a prompt to look after your bones and heart.
