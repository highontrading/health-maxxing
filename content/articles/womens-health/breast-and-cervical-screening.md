Breast and cervical screening are two of the most studied preventive tests in medicine, yet the advice on when to start and how often to go still depends on which country, and which expert body, you ask. That is not a sign that nobody knows. It reflects honest disagreement about how to weigh cancers caught early against false alarms and unnecessary treatment.

This guide sets out what the main bodies recommend, where dense breasts and HPV come in, what an abnormal result usually means, and which symptoms should never wait for a screening invitation.

## The schedules at a glance

| | US Preventive Services Task Force | American Cancer Society | NHS (UK) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mammogram | Every 2 years, ages 40 to 74 | Yearly at 45 to 54 (optional from 40), then every 2 years or yearly | Every 3 years, ages 50 to 71 |
| Cervical screening | Pap test every 3 years from 21. From 30 to 65: Pap every 3 years, or HPV test every 5 | HPV test every 5 years, ages 25 to 65 | HPV test, ages 25 to 64; every 5 years after a negative result in England |

These schedules are for people at average risk who have no symptoms.[^1][^2][^3][^6][^7] If you have a strong family history, a known gene variant such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, or had radiotherapy to the chest when you were young, ask about starting earlier and adding MRI.[^2]

## Breast screening

A mammogram is a low-dose X-ray of the breast that can find cancers before they can be felt. Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide, with 2.3 million diagnoses and 670,000 deaths in 2022.[^12]

In 2024 the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) lowered its recommended starting age from 50 to 40. Its modeling suggests that screening every two years from 40 could avert about 19% more breast cancer deaths than starting at 50.[^1] The American Cancer Society offers a choice at 40 to 44, recommends yearly mammograms from 45 to 54, and then every two years or yearly for as long as you are in good health.[^2] The NHS invites women every three years between 50 and 71.[^3]

Why the difference? Starting earlier and screening more often finds more cancers. It also produces more false alarms and more overdiagnosis, which means finding cancers that would never have caused harm in a woman's lifetime.

### Benefits and harms, honestly

> [!STAT] 20%
> The estimated reduction in breast cancer deaths among women invited to screening, according to an independent UK review of the trials.[^5]

The same review put the trade-off in concrete terms. For every 10,000 UK women invited to screening from age 50 for 20 years, it estimated that 43 breast cancer deaths would be prevented and 129 cancers would be overdiagnosed: about three overdiagnoses for every death prevented.[^5]

False alarms are part of the picture too. In the NHS program, about 4 in every 100 women screened are called back for more tests, and about 1 of those 4 turns out to have cancer.[^3]

### Dense breasts

Breast density describes how much glandular and fibrous tissue you have compared with fat. It can only be seen on a mammogram, and nearly half of women aged 40 and over who have mammograms have dense breasts. It matters for two reasons: dense tissue makes cancers harder to spot, and it is linked with a somewhat higher risk of breast cancer. US mammography providers must now tell you whether your breasts are dense.[^4]

What to do with that information is less clear. The USPSTF says there is not enough evidence to recommend for or against extra ultrasound or MRI on the basis of density alone.[^1] It is a conversation to have with your doctor, in light of your other risk factors.

### Breast awareness

Self-examination on a fixed schedule is no longer recommended as a screening test, but knowing how your breasts normally look and feel is. Report any change promptly, even if your last mammogram was clear.[^2]

## Cervical screening

Almost all cervical cancer is caused by long-lasting infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus passed on through sexual contact. Cancer usually takes 15 to 20 years to develop, which gives screening a long window in which to find and treat abnormal cells. Even so, about 660,000 women were diagnosed and about 350,000 died in 2022, the great majority of them in low- and middle-income countries.[^8]

Two tests can be run on a sample from the cervix. The **HPV test** looks for the virus. The **Pap test**, also called cytology or a smear test, looks for abnormal cells. Programs are moving toward HPV testing because it flags risk earlier, which allows longer gaps between tests.

- **USPSTF (2018):** a Pap test every three years from 21 to 29. From 30 to 65, a Pap test every three years, an HPV test every five years, or both together every five years. A draft update published in late 2024 would make HPV testing the preferred option from 30 and would endorse self-collected samples, so check for the final version.[^6]
- **American Cancer Society:** an HPV test every five years from 25 to 65.[^2]
- **NHS:** HPV testing for everyone with a cervix aged 25 to 64. In England the interval is now five years after a negative result.[^7]
- **World Health Organization (WHO):** for countries building programs, an HPV test every 5 to 10 years from age 30, and earlier and more often for women living with HIV.[^8]

> [!TIP] Ask about self-collection
> HPV tests work on a vaginal sample you take yourself, and the WHO says self-collected samples are as reliable as those taken by a clinician.[^8] If the speculum examination is what has kept you away, ask whether self-collection is offered where you live.

### HPV vaccination

The HPV vaccine prevents infection with the virus types responsible for most cervical cancers, and it works best when given before any exposure. The WHO's main target group is girls aged 9 to 14.[^8] In the US, vaccination is routinely recommended at 11 or 12, with catch-up through age 26, and adults up to 45 can discuss it with their doctor.[^11]

The real-world results are striking. In England, cervical cancer rates were 87% lower among women who had been offered the vaccine at 12 to 13 than among earlier, unvaccinated generations.[^10] Vaccinated people still need screening, because the vaccines do not cover every cancer-causing type.[^11] Our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) covers who can still get it.

The WHO's elimination strategy rests on three targets for 2030: 90% of girls fully vaccinated by 15, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45, and 90% of women with cervical disease treated.[^9]

## If your result is abnormal

An abnormal screening result is common, and it is not a cancer diagnosis.

- **Cervical screening.** If HPV is found but your cells look normal, you will usually be asked back for a repeat test in a year, because most HPV infections clear on their own within about two years. If HPV is found along with abnormal cells, you will be offered a colposcopy, a closer look at the cervix, where abnormal cells can be removed before they turn into cancer.[^7]
- **Breast screening.** Being called back means more images or an ultrasound, and sometimes a needle biopsy. Most women who are called back do not have cancer.[^3]

## Symptoms that should not wait

Screening is for people without symptoms. If something has changed, do not wait for your next invitation.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly for a new lump or thickening in the breast or armpit, a change in the size or shape of a breast, dimpling or redness of the skin, a nipple that changes or leaks fluid, especially blood.[^12] The same goes for vaginal bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause, unusual discharge, or persistent pelvic pain.[^8] These symptoms usually have a harmless cause, but they need checking whatever your age and however recent your last normal test.

## The bottom line

Follow the program in your country, and treat the differences between guidelines as a range of reasonable choices, not a contradiction. Have mammograms from 40 or 50 depending on where you live, keep up cervical screening from your twenties to your mid-sixties, take the HPV vaccine if you are eligible, and act on symptoms straight away.
