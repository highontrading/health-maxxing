You have a muscle group you have probably never seen, rarely think about and rely on every time you cough, lift, laugh or look for a bathroom. When it works, you do not notice it. When it does not, the result can be leaks, a dragging heaviness or pain: problems that are common, treatable and too often endured in silence.

This guide explains what the pelvic floor does, how to tell a weak one from an overly tense one, how to train it properly and when to bring in a specialist. It is written mainly for women, though men have a pelvic floor too and the same exercises apply.[^11]

## What the pelvic floor does

The pelvic floor is a sling of muscles and connective tissue that stretches from the pubic bone at the front to the tailbone at the back. It holds up the bladder, the bowel and the uterus, tightens to keep the urethra and anus closed, and relaxes to let you empty them.[^7][^8]

A healthy pelvic floor is not just strong. It can contract firmly, hold, and then fully let go. Problems arise at both ends: muscles that are too weak, and muscles that cannot relax.

Problems are also very common. In a nationally representative US survey, 23.7% of women reported at least one pelvic floor disorder: 15.7% had urinary incontinence, 9.0% had fecal incontinence and 2.9% had symptoms of prolapse. The share rose with age and with the number of births.[^4]

## Weak, or too tight?

| | Underactive (weak) | Overactive (too tense) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical signs | Leaking urine with a cough, sneeze, laugh or jump; leaking gas or stool; a heavy or bulging feeling in the vagina | Pain during sex or when inserting a tampon; pelvic pain; trouble starting to pee or emptying fully; straining with bowel movements |
| What helps | Strengthening exercises | Learning to relax the muscles, usually with a specialist |

The second column matters because the standard advice to "do your Kegels" can make a tense pelvic floor worse. Pain or difficulty emptying are reasons to be assessed, not to squeeze harder.[^10]

## How to do pelvic floor exercises properly

1. **Find the muscles.** Imagine you are trying to stop yourself passing urine and passing gas at the same time. The squeeze and lift you feel inside is your pelvic floor. Use that image only to find the muscles: regularly stopping your urine midstream is not recommended, because it can harm the bladder.[^1]
2. **Squeeze and lift, then let go.** Sit comfortably. Tighten the muscles, hold for a few seconds, then relax completely for at least as long. The NHS suggests 10 to 15 squeezes in a session.[^1]
3. **Breathe.** Do not hold your breath, and keep your stomach, buttocks and thighs relaxed.[^1]
4. **Repeat through the day.** The UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends at least eight contractions, three times a day.[^3]
5. **Progress slowly.** Lengthen the holds, add some quick, strong squeezes, and practice lying down, sitting and standing.[^1][^11]
6. **Keep going.** Train for about three months before you judge the results, and continue once it is working.[^1][^3]

> [!TIP] Squeeze before you sneeze
> Tightening your pelvic floor just before a cough, sneeze or lift is a learned skill, sometimes called the Knack. In a small study of older women with mild stress incontinence, it sharply reduced leakage during coughing within a week of learning it.[^12]

### Common mistakes

- Holding your breath, or bearing down instead of lifting.
- Clenching your buttocks, thighs or stomach instead of the pelvic floor.
- Never fully relaxing between squeezes.
- Stopping as soon as things improve.

Getting it wrong is easy. In one study, only about half of women performed a correct contraction after brief verbal instruction, and a quarter used a technique that could make leakage worse.[^5] If you are not sure you have it, ask a pelvic health physiotherapist to check.

## Does it work?

For stress incontinence, the kind triggered by coughing, sneezing or exercise, the evidence is strong.

> [!STAT] 56% vs 6%
> The share of women with stress incontinence who reported being cured after pelvic floor muscle training, compared with those who had no treatment or a sham treatment, in a Cochrane review of 31 trials.[^2]

In other words, trained women were about eight times as likely to report a cure. Across all types of urinary incontinence the figures were 35% versus 6%.[^2] It is why NICE recommends at least three months of supervised pelvic floor training as the first treatment for stress or mixed urinary incontinence.[^3]

Two other measures from the same guideline are worth knowing: losing weight if your body mass index is 30 or more, and cutting back on caffeine if sudden urges are the problem.[^3]

## Pregnancy and after birth

Pregnancy and vaginal birth both load the pelvic floor, and training during pregnancy helps. A Cochrane review found that women with no leakage who did pelvic floor training while pregnant were 62% less likely to have urinary incontinence in late pregnancy and 29% less likely to have it three to six months after the birth.[^6] NICE advises encouraging pelvic floor training during pregnancy and afterwards.[^7]

After the birth, restart gently when it feels comfortable. Tell your midwife or doctor about leaks, heaviness or pain that have not settled by your postnatal check. A severe tear or an assisted delivery is a good reason to ask for a referral. Our [pregnancy guide](/articles/pregnancy-nutrition-and-exercise) covers exercise in pregnancy more broadly.

## Prolapse basics

Pelvic organ prolapse happens when the bladder, uterus or bowel slips from its usual position and bulges into the vagina. Typical symptoms are a feeling of heaviness or dragging, a sense that something is coming down, or a bulge you can feel or see. It is not life-threatening, and it becomes more likely after childbirth and [menopause](/articles/perimenopause-and-menopause).[^8]

Supervised pelvic floor training can help. In a randomized trial of 447 women with prolapse, those given an individualized program with a physiotherapist reported fewer symptoms a year later than those given a lifestyle advice leaflet.[^9] Other options include a vaginal pessary, which is a removable device that supports the organs, and surgery.[^8] Avoiding constipation and heavy straining helps as well; see [constipation relief that works](/articles/constipation-relief-that-works).

## When to see a pelvic health physiotherapist

Ask for a referral, or refer yourself where that is possible, if:

- you cannot feel the muscles working, or are not sure you are doing the exercises correctly
- you see no improvement after three months of consistent training
- you have pelvic pain, pain during sex, or trouble emptying your bladder or bowel
- you can feel a bulge or heaviness
- you leak stool or cannot control gas

A pelvic health physiotherapist can assess the muscles, usually with a gentle internal examination that you consent to, and build a program that fits what they find. Our page on [urinary incontinence](/conditions/urinary-incontinence) explains the medical options beyond exercise.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly for blood in your urine, pain or burning when you pee, or leaks that started suddenly. Go to an emergency department if you cannot pass urine at all, or if new numbness around your genitals or buttocks appears together with back pain, leg weakness or loss of bladder or bowel control. That combination can mean pressure on the nerves at the base of the spine and needs same-day treatment.

## The bottom line

Pelvic floor problems are common, and for most of them the first treatment is free: a few minutes of correctly performed exercises, three times a day, for three months. Learn to find the muscles, squeeze and lift without holding your breath, and relax fully between efforts. If you have pain, a bulge or no progress after three months, see a pelvic health physiotherapist, because the fix may be relaxation or support and not more squeezing.
