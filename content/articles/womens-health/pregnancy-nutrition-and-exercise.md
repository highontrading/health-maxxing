Pregnancy advice arrives from every direction, and a lot of it contradicts itself. The core guidance from the major health bodies is steadier than the noise suggests. This guide sticks to what the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the World Health Organization (WHO) and the UK's NHS advise, and says where they differ.

It covers the supplements that matter, how much extra food you need, what to avoid, how to stay active, which vaccines are offered and the symptoms that should never wait. If you have only just found out, our [due date calculator](/tools/due-date) will give you the timeline.

## The supplements that matter

### Folic acid: start before you conceive

Folic acid lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida. These arise in the first weeks of pregnancy, often before you know you are pregnant, which is why the advice is to start early.[^5]

- The NHS advises 400 micrograms (mcg) a day from before conception until 12 weeks.[^2]
- The US Preventive Services Task Force advises 400 to 800 mcg a day for anyone who is planning or could become pregnant, starting at least a month before conception and continuing through the first two to three months.[^5]
- The WHO recommends 400 mcg a day throughout pregnancy, alongside iron.[^4]

Some people need a much higher, prescription-only dose, for example after a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect, or if they have diabetes or take certain epilepsy medicines. Ask before you start trying, if you can.[^2]

> [!STAT] 400 mcg
> The daily folic acid dose advised before conception and in early pregnancy by the NHS, the WHO and US guidelines.[^2][^4][^5]

### The rest of the list

| Nutrient | What the guidance says | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin D | NHS: a 10 mcg (400 IU) supplement daily. US recommended intake: 15 mcg (600 IU) | Many people make too little from sunlight, especially in winter |
| Iron | US recommended intake: 27 mg a day. WHO: a daily supplement of 30 to 60 mg | The NHS tests for anemia and treats it if found |
| Iodine | US: 220 mcg a day. WHO: 250 mcg | Not every prenatal vitamin includes it |
| Choline | US: 450 mg a day | Most prenatal vitamins contain little or none |
| DHA, an omega-3 fat | No official daily target | Oily fish is the main source |

Those figures come from ACOG, the NHS, the WHO and the US Office of Dietary Supplements.[^1][^2][^4][^7][^8] Eggs, meat, fish and dairy are the main food sources of choline, and most pregnant women get less than the recommended amount.[^8]

A standard prenatal vitamin covers most of this, so check the label for iodine and choline. Two cautions: do not double up on multivitamins, and avoid supplements that contain vitamin A as retinol, including fish liver oil, because too much can harm a developing baby.[^2]

## Eating for one and a bit

You do not need to eat for two. For a single baby, ACOG estimates no extra calories in the first trimester, about 340 extra a day in the second and about 450 in the third.[^1] That is a substantial snack, not a second dinner.

Recommended weight gain depends on your body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy. The ranges used in the US come from the Institute of Medicine:[^1]

| BMI before pregnancy | Recommended total gain |
| --- | --- |
| Under 18.5 | 12.5 to 18 kg (28 to 40 lb) |
| 18.5 to 24.9 | 11.5 to 16 kg (25 to 35 lb) |
| 25 to 29.9 | 7 to 11.5 kg (15 to 25 lb) |
| 30 or more | 5 to 9 kg (11 to 20 lb) |

These ranges are for one baby, and they are a starting point for a conversation with your midwife or doctor, not a target to police yourself with. Our [BMI calculator](/tools/bmi) shows where you started.

## What to avoid, and why

The list is shorter than the internet implies. Most of it comes down to two infections and one metal.

- **Listeria.** This bacterium can grow in the fridge and is especially dangerous in pregnancy. Avoid unpasteurized milk and cheese, soft mold-ripened and blue cheeses unless cooked until steaming hot, pâté and refrigerated smoked seafood. ACOG adds deli meats and hot dogs unless they are heated until steaming.[^1][^3]
- **Toxoplasma.** This parasite lives in undercooked meat and in soil. Cook meat until no pink remains, and wash fruit, vegetables and salad well.[^3]
- **Mercury.** US agencies say to avoid shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, bigeye tuna and tilefish from the Gulf of Mexico.[^6] Fish itself is encouraged: aim for two to three servings a week, about 225 to 340 g (8 to 12 oz), of lower-mercury fish such as salmon, sardines and trout.[^6] The NHS caps oily fish at two portions a week.[^3]
- **Raw or undercooked eggs, meat and shellfish.** ACOG advises avoiding them.[^1] The NHS makes one exception: hen eggs with the British Lion stamp can be eaten runny, because of the salmonella controls behind that scheme.[^3]
- **Liver.** Liver and liver products are very high in vitamin A.[^3]
- **Alcohol.** No safe amount has been established, so the advice is to avoid it completely.[^3]

Caffeine is a limit, not a ban. The NHS sets it at 200 mg a day, and US guidance uses the same figure.[^3] The WHO advises anyone drinking more than 300 mg a day to cut down.[^4] By the NHS's reckoning, a mug of filter coffee has about 140 mg, a mug of instant about 100 mg, a mug of tea about 75 mg and a can of cola about 40 mg.[^3]

> [!NOTE]
> Food rules vary between countries because food safety systems do. If your national guidance is stricter or looser than what you read here, follow your own maternity team.

## Staying active

Unless you have been told otherwise, exercise in pregnancy is safe and encouraged. ACOG recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week, such as brisk walking, swimming or stationary cycling.[^9] That matches the WHO target in our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need). ACOG lists the benefits as less back pain and a possibly lower risk of gestational diabetes, preeclampsia and cesarean birth.[^9]

If you were active before, you can usually carry on and adjust as your body changes. If you were not, start small: the NHS suggests 15 minutes three times a week, building up to 30 minutes a day. You should be able to hold a conversation while you exercise.[^10]

Activities to skip include contact sports, anything with a high risk of falling such as downhill skiing or horse riding, scuba diving and hot yoga.[^9] The NHS also advises against lying flat on your back for long periods, particularly after 16 weeks.[^10] Pelvic floor exercises are worth starting now; see our [pelvic floor guide](/articles/pelvic-floor-health).

> [!WARNING]
> Stop exercising and call your doctor or midwife if you have vaginal bleeding, fluid leaking from the vagina, regular painful contractions, chest pain, dizziness or faintness, shortness of breath before you start, a headache, muscle weakness, or calf pain or swelling.[^9]

## Vaccines in pregnancy

Vaccination in pregnancy protects you and passes antibodies to your baby for the first months of life. The NHS offers three vaccines:[^11]

- flu vaccine, at any stage of pregnancy during flu season
- whooping cough (pertussis) vaccine, from 16 weeks
- RSV vaccine, from 28 weeks, to protect newborns against a common cause of serious chest infections

ACOG recommends the same three in the US on a slightly different timetable. Advice on COVID-19 vaccination in pregnancy now varies between countries, so ask your own maternity team what applies to you. Live vaccines such as MMR are not given during pregnancy, which is a good reason to check your immunity before you conceive.[^11] Our [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) has more.

## Symptoms that cannot wait

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your maternity unit, your doctor or your local emergency number straight away if you have vaginal bleeding or leaking fluid, your baby's movements slow down or stop, or you have a fever, chest pain, trouble breathing, pain or swelling in one calf, or thoughts of harming yourself. A severe headache, blurred vision or flashing lights, severe pain just below the ribs, vomiting, or sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet can be signs of preeclampsia, which needs same-day assessment.[^12] Do not wait for your next appointment.

## The bottom line

Take folic acid from before conception, add a prenatal vitamin and vitamin D, and eat a normal varied diet with two or three portions of lower-mercury fish a week. Skip alcohol, high-mercury fish and the short list of listeria-prone foods, keep caffeine under 200 mg a day, and aim for 150 minutes of moderate activity a week unless you have been advised otherwise. Take up the vaccines you are offered, and treat bleeding, a severe headache, vision changes or reduced movements as reasons to call now.
