Polycystic ovary syndrome is one of the most common hormone conditions in women and one of the most poorly named. It involves no true cysts, it is not only about the ovaries, and up to 70% of the women who have it have never been told.[^1]

In May 2026 the name finally changed. After a global consensus process, the condition is now officially polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS.[^3] This guide mostly says PCOS, because that is still what you will see on medical records, and explains what the condition is, how it is diagnosed and what the evidence supports for managing it.

## What it is, and why the name changed

PCOS is a long-term hormonal and metabolic condition. Its core features are higher than usual levels of androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which women also make in small amounts), irregular or absent ovulation, and ovaries that contain many small fluid-filled sacs.[^4] Those sacs are what a scan report calls "cysts". They are follicles, each holding an egg that started to mature and then stalled, not growths that need removing.

That misunderstanding is one reason for the new name. The consensus behind it involved 56 patient and professional organizations and more than 14,000 survey responses. The authors note that the old name wrongly implies abnormal ovarian cysts, and they chose "polyendocrine" and "metabolic" to reflect a condition that involves several hormone systems and the whole body.[^3] Only the name has changed. The diagnostic criteria and treatments described here come from the 2023 international guideline.[^2]

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that PCOS affects 10% to 13% of women of reproductive age, and calls it the most common cause of anovulation (not releasing an egg) and a leading cause of infertility.[^1] Estimates differ between studies, largely because of which diagnostic criteria are used.[^7]

> The share of women with PCOS worldwide who do not know they have it, according to the WHO.[^1]

## What drives it

Nobody knows the root cause. ACOG, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, describes several factors that appear to work together: insulin resistance, raised androgen levels and an irregular menstrual cycle.[^4]

[Insulin resistance](/articles/insulin-resistance-and-prediabetes) means the body's cells respond poorly to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar out of the blood, so the body makes more of it. Over time that can lead to type 2 diabetes. High androgen levels can stop the ovaries from releasing an egg each month, and they cause the unwanted hair growth and acne.[^4]

Weight is part of the picture but not the whole of it. The 2023 guideline stresses that prediabetes and type 2 diabetes are more common in PCOS regardless of age and body mass index.[^2]

## Symptoms

PCOS looks different from one person to the next, and you do not need every symptom to have it. The common ones are:[^1][^4]

- irregular, infrequent or absent periods
- excess hair on the face or body (hirsutism)
- acne or oily skin
- thinning hair on the scalp
- difficulty getting pregnant
- weight gain, especially around the middle
- patches of darker, velvety skin in body folds, a sign of insulin resistance

## How it is diagnosed

There is no single test. The 2023 international guideline, which updates the long-used Rotterdam criteria, bases the diagnosis on three features:[^2]

1. **Signs of high androgens**, either visible (excess hair, severe acne, scalp hair loss) or on a blood test.
2. **Irregular ovulation**, which shows up as irregular or absent periods.
3. **Polycystic-looking ovaries on ultrasound** or, as an alternative in adults, a raised blood level of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), which is made by small follicles.

An adult needs two of the three, once other causes have been ruled out. If the first two are present, no scan or AMH test is needed, and doing both tests is discouraged because it risks overdiagnosis. For teenagers the bar is higher: both of the first two features are required, and neither ultrasound nor AMH should be used, because they are not reliable at that age.[^2]

Expect blood tests as part of the workup, both to measure hormone levels and to rule out other hormone conditions that cause similar symptoms.[^5] Thyroid disease is one of them, and our [thyroid guide](/articles/thyroid-101) explains those tests.

> [!NOTE]
> Polycystic-looking ovaries on their own are not PCOS. Without irregular cycles or signs of high androgens, they do not meet the criteria.[^2]

## Long-term health: what to keep an eye on

PCOS is more than a period problem. The guideline recommends regular checks because several risks are raised:[^2]

- **Blood sugar.** Prediabetes and type 2 diabetes are more common, so blood sugar should be checked when PCOS is diagnosed and followed up over time.
- **Heart health.** Cardiovascular risk is higher, although the absolute risk before menopause is low. Blood pressure should be measured every year.
- **The lining of the uterus.** Before menopause, women with PCOS have a two- to six-fold higher risk of endometrial cancer, though the absolute risk remains low. Report long gaps between periods or unusual bleeding.
- **Mood.** Symptoms of depression and anxiety are significantly more common, and the guideline recommends screening everyone at diagnosis. If your mood is suffering, say so. It is part of the condition, and it is treatable.
- **Pregnancy and sleep.** Pregnancy complications and obstructive sleep apnea are also more common, so tell your maternity team about your diagnosis and mention loud snoring or daytime sleepiness.

## Management: built around your priorities

There is no cure, but symptoms can be treated.[^1] The right plan depends on what matters most to you.

| Goal | What guidelines suggest | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General health | Healthy eating and regular activity for everyone | No specific diet has been shown to beat another |
| Regular cycles, acne, excess hair | Combined contraceptive pill | Treats symptoms while you take it |
| Metabolic health | Metformin | Stomach upset is the usual side effect |
| Persistent excess hair | Anti-androgen medicines, hair removal | Reliable contraception is essential |
| Pregnancy | Letrozole to trigger ovulation | Other medicines or IVF if needed |

**Lifestyle.** The guideline recommends healthy eating and regular physical activity for everyone with PCOS, at any weight.[^2] For women who are overweight, ACOG notes that even a small amount of weight loss can make periods more regular and improve insulin and cholesterol levels.[^4] Evidence on newer weight-loss medicines specifically in PCOS is still limited.[^2] Our explainer on [GLP-1 drugs](/articles/glp-1-weight-loss-drugs-explained) covers how they work.

**The pill.** The combined pill is the first-line medicine for irregular cycles and for acne and excess hair, and no particular brand is recommended over another.[^2] It is a sound long-term treatment for women who do not want to become pregnant, but it manages PCOS; it does not cure it.[^6]

**Metformin.** This diabetes medicine helps the body respond to insulin, and in PCOS it can also lower androgen levels and improve ovulation.[^4]

**Anti-androgens.** If excess hair has not improved enough with the pill and hair removal, medicines that block androgens can be added. They must be combined with reliable contraception because they can affect a developing baby.[^2]

**Inositol.** This popular supplement gets a cautious verdict. The guideline says it can be considered according to personal preference, because it appears to do little harm and may improve some metabolic measures, but it has shown limited benefit for ovulation, excess hair or weight.[^2]

**Fertility.** Ovulation problems are often treatable. The guideline's first-choice medicine for triggering ovulation is letrozole, with clomiphene, metformin, injectable hormones and ovarian surgery as second-line options.[^2] Because ovulation in PCOS is unpredictable, not absent, you still need contraception if you do not want to become pregnant.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if your periods are irregular or have stopped (our [menstrual cycle guide](/articles/menstrual-cycle-explained) explains what counts as typical), if you notice new excess hair, persistent acne or thinning scalp hair, or if you have irregular periods and are trying to conceive. Get prompt advice if hair growth or other changes come on quickly, or if you bleed heavily after months without a period.

## The bottom line

PCOS, now officially PMOS, is common, lifelong and manageable. If your periods are irregular or you have signs of high androgens, ask for an assessment; the diagnosis rests on your history, an examination and a few tests. Build the plan around what matters most to you, whether that is cycles, skin, fertility or long-term health, and keep up the regular checks on blood sugar, blood pressure and mood.
