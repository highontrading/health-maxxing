Most of us learned the menstrual cycle from a tidy diagram: 28 days, ovulation on day 14, repeat. Real cycles are messier. In one analysis of more than 600,000 cycles logged in a fertility app, only about 13% lasted exactly 28 days.[^3]

This guide covers what your hormones do in each phase, what counts as typical, how to spot ovulation, whether "cycle syncing" your workouts is worth it, and which changes deserve a doctor's attention.

## The four phases at a glance

A cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next.[^1] Physiologists divide it into a follicular phase before ovulation and a luteal phase after it.[^2] Count menstruation and ovulation separately and you get the familiar four.

| Phase | When | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Menstruation | Day 1 to roughly day 5 | Estrogen and progesterone are low, and the lining of the uterus sheds |
| Follicular | Day 1 until ovulation | Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) matures an egg, and rising estrogen rebuilds the lining |
| Ovulation | Around mid-cycle | A surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) releases the egg |
| Luteal | Ovulation until the next period | Progesterone readies the lining. With no pregnancy, hormones fall and bleeding starts |

### Menstruation

Day 1 is the first day of bleeding. With no pregnancy under way, estrogen and progesterone have dropped, and the lining built up during the previous cycle comes away.[^2] A period generally lasts up to 7 days.[^1] The average in the app data was 4.[^3]

### The follicular phase

This phase also starts on day 1 and runs until ovulation. The pituitary gland at the base of the brain releases follicle-stimulating hormone, which prompts several follicles (small sacs in the ovary that each hold an egg) to grow. One becomes dominant and produces rising amounts of estradiol, the main form of estrogen, which thickens the uterine lining again.[^2]

This is the stretchy part of the cycle. When a cycle runs long or short, the follicular phase is usually why: in the app study it averaged 16.9 days, with most cycles falling between 10 and 30.[^3]

### Ovulation

When estradiol has been high for long enough, the pituitary responds with a burst of luteinizing hormone. The follicle releases its egg about 36 hours after that surge begins, or 10 to 12 hours after it peaks.[^2]

### The luteal phase

The emptied follicle turns into the corpus luteum, a temporary gland whose progesterone prepares the lining for a fertilized egg. If no pregnancy begins, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone and estrogen fall, and the next period arrives.[^2] This phase averaged 12.4 days in the app data and varied much less than the follicular phase.[^3] It is also when premenstrual symptoms tend to show up, if you get them.

## What counts as typical

- **Cycle length:** The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) describes a typical adult cycle as 21 to 35 days.[^1] FIGO, the international federation of gynecologists, uses 24 to 38 days.[^11]
- **Bleeding:** Up to 7 days by ACOG's definition, up to 8 by FIGO's.[^1][^11]
- **Regularity:** Some variation is expected. A cycle length that shifts by more than 7 to 9 days from one cycle to the next counts as irregular.[^1]
- **Teenagers:** Most teens have cycles of 21 to 45 days.[^12]

> [!STAT] 13%
> The share of cycles that lasted exactly 28 days in a study of more than 600,000 cycles. The average was 29.3 days, and about two in three women had cycles of 25 to 30 days.[^3]

One caveat: the data came from users of a single fertility app, and one of the authors worked for the company, so it is not a random sample of all women.[^3]

In the years before menopause, cycles often become less predictable, which we cover in our [perimenopause guide](/articles/perimenopause-and-menopause).

> [!NOTE]
> Everything here describes a cycle without hormonal contraception. Depending on the method, hormonal birth control can suppress ovulation, thin the uterine lining or both, and it can stop periods altogether while you use it.[^10]

## How to tell when you ovulate

Ovulation usually happens 12 to 14 days before your next period, which is not necessarily day 14.[^5] Three signs can help you find it:

- **Cervical mucus.** Around ovulation, vaginal discharge becomes thinner and stretchy, a bit like raw egg white.[^5]
- **LH tests.** Home ovulation kits detect the LH surge in urine, which shows up a day or so before the egg is released.[^2]
- **Basal body temperature.** Progesterone raises your resting temperature by roughly 0.3 to 0.6 °C (0.5 to 1 °F) after ovulation, so the rise confirms ovulation after the fact. It cannot predict it, and it is not considered a reliable way to pinpoint the day.[^6]

Sperm can survive in the fallopian tubes for up to 7 days, so the days before ovulation are fertile too. That is also why it is possible, if not very likely, to conceive from sex soon after a period ends, especially in a short cycle.[^5] If you do conceive, our [due date calculator](/tools/due-date) can map out the timeline.

## Your cycle as a vital sign

ACOG, together with the American Academy of Pediatrics, advises clinicians to treat the menstrual cycle as a vital sign in girls and adolescents: something to ask about at every checkup, like blood pressure, because unusual patterns can be an early clue to other health problems.[^4]

The same logic applies to adults. Ovulation depends on a chain of signals between the brain and the ovaries, and many things can interrupt it. Causes of irregular or missing periods include pregnancy, polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid problems, poorly controlled diabetes, eating disorders, very heavy exercise, stress and some medicines.[^4] A change in your cycle is information, not a verdict, but it is worth following up. Our guides to [PCOS](/articles/pcos-explained) and [thyroid problems](/articles/thyroid-101) go deeper on two of those causes.

> [!TIP] Track the basics
> Note the first day of each period, how many days you bleed, and anything unusual such as spotting or severe pain. A few months of notes gives a clinician far more to work with than "a bit irregular".

## Should you "cycle sync" your workouts?

Social media is full of plans that assign heavy lifting to the follicular phase and gentle yoga to the luteal phase, on the theory that shifting hormones change how you perform. The research does not back that confidence.

A 2020 meta-analysis of 78 studies found that exercise performance might dip very slightly in the early follicular phase, meaning the days of your period. The effect was trivial, results varied widely and most studies were of low quality, so the authors advised an individual approach over general rules.[^7] A 2023 umbrella review of strength training research concluded that it is premature to say cycle phase meaningfully changes strength performance or how muscles respond to training, partly because many studies never properly verified which phase participants were in.[^8]

The practical reading: train consistently and adjust for how you actually feel, not for what the calendar says. If cramps flatten you on day 1, an easier session is a reasonable call. Our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) works in any phase.

## Tracking apps and your privacy

Apps make logging easy, with two caveats.

First, their predictions are estimates. An app that assumes ovulation on day 14 will often be wrong, because the follicular phase varies so much between people and between cycles.[^3] Do not rely on a calendar prediction alone to avoid pregnancy.

Second, cycle data is sensitive health data, and it has not always been treated that way. In 2021 the US Federal Trade Commission finalized a settlement with the maker of the Flo app over allegations that it had shared users' health details with Facebook, Google and other analytics companies after promising to keep them private.[^9] Before you commit to an app, check what it shares, with whom, and whether you can delete your data.

## When to get checked

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if your periods come less than 21 or more than 35 days apart, vary by more than 7 to 9 days from cycle to cycle, last longer than 7 days, or stop for three months or more when you are not pregnant or using hormonal contraception. Bleeding between periods, after sex or after menopause also needs assessment, as does a first period that has not arrived by age 15.[^1][^10] Seek urgent care for very heavy bleeding with dizziness or fainting, or for sudden severe pelvic pain, especially if you could be pregnant.

Heavy or painful periods deserve attention too. ACOG says that needing to change a pad or tampon more than once every 1 to 2 hours is not normal.[^12] Our guide to [period pain and heavy periods](/articles/period-pain-and-heavy-periods) explains what helps.

## The bottom line

A typical cycle lasts 21 to 35 days with up to a week of bleeding, and very few people run on a perfect 28. Learn your own pattern, treat a lasting change as useful information, and feel free to ignore advice to organize your training around your hormones. If your cycle falls outside the typical range or changes suddenly, get it checked.
