Period pain is so common that it is easy to assume any amount of it is normal. Studies put the share of teenagers and young women who get it at somewhere between 50% and 90%.[^2] Heavy bleeding is common too: in the UK, about 1 in 20 women aged 30 to 49 see their family doctor each year about heavy periods or other menstrual problems.[^7]

Common is not the same as fine. This guide explains where ordinary cramps come from, how doctors define a heavy period, which treatments have evidence behind them, and the signs that something else is going on.

## Why periods hurt

Most period pain is what doctors call primary dysmenorrhea: cramping with no underlying disease. The culprits are prostaglandins, hormone-like chemicals made in the lining of the uterus that make its muscle contract.[^2] Levels are highest as bleeding starts and fall as the lining is shed, which is why the first day or two is usually the worst. This kind of pain often begins soon after periods first start, and for many women it eases with age or after giving birth.[^1]

Secondary dysmenorrhea is pain caused by a condition in the reproductive organs, such as endometriosis, adenomyosis or fibroids. It behaves differently. It tends to get worse over time, can start days before the bleeding does, and may not go away when the period ends.[^1]

## What helps period pain

### Anti-inflammatory painkillers

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen reduce prostaglandin production, so they go after the cause, and ACOG treats them as the first-line option.[^2]

A Cochrane review found they were clearly more effective than placebo: if 18% of women get moderate or excellent relief from a dummy pill, 45% to 53% will get it from an NSAID. No single NSAID stood out as better than the others, and they appeared to work better than paracetamol (acetaminophen). The trials were rated low quality, mostly because of poor reporting, but the result was consistent.[^3]

NSAIDs work best when you take them at the first sign of pain or bleeding, and most people need them for only a day or two.[^1] Use them as directed on the label.

> [!WARNING]
> NSAIDs are not for everyone. ACOG advises against them if you have a bleeding disorder, asthma, an aspirin allergy, liver damage, stomach problems or ulcers.[^1] If any of those apply, ask a pharmacist or doctor what to use instead.

### Heat

A heat patch, wrap or hot water bottle on the lower abdomen is more than a comfort. A 2018 systematic review found six randomized trials, and in those that compared heat with an unheated placebo, heat came out ahead. The trials were small, so the authors called the evidence suggestive, not conclusive.[^4] Heat is cheap and easy to try, with the obvious caveat of protecting your skin from burns.

### Exercise

A Cochrane review of 12 trials involving 854 women found that regular exercise, typically 45 to 60 minutes at least three times a week, may bring a large reduction in period pain: about 25 mm on a 100 mm pain scale. The evidence is low quality, and nobody knows whether shorter sessions work as well, but it is a reasonable thing to try.[^5] Our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) can help you build a routine.

### Hormonal contraception

If painkillers are not enough, hormonal methods are the usual next step. A 2023 Cochrane review of 21 trials found high-quality evidence that the combined pill reduces period pain more than placebo. In practical terms, women with a 28% chance of improving on placebo may have a 37% to 60% chance on the pill. The trade-offs were irregular bleeding and probably more headaches and nausea. Taking the pill continuously, without the monthly break, may reduce pain more than the standard pattern.[^6]

Other hormonal methods, including the hormonal IUD, the implant and the injection, are also used for painful periods.[^1]

## How heavy is too heavy?

Nobody can measure blood loss at home, so the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) defines heavy menstrual bleeding by its effect: excessive blood loss that interferes with your physical, social, emotional or material quality of life.[^7] If your period dictates what you wear, where you go or whether you sleep through the night, it counts.

The NHS lists practical signs that a period is heavy:[^8]

- needing to change a pad or tampon every 1 to 2 hours, or empty a menstrual cup more often than recommended
- needing two kinds of protection at once, such as a pad and a tampon
- passing clots larger than about 2.5 cm (1 inch)
- bleeding for more than 7 days
- bleeding through to your clothes or bedding

Losing that much blood month after month can drain your iron stores. Feeling tired or short of breath much of the time is worth mentioning, and a blood test can check for iron deficiency anemia.[^8] Our page on [iron deficiency anemia](/conditions/iron-deficiency-anemia) explains the tests and treatment.

## What might be behind it

Sometimes no cause is found. When there is one, the NHS lists these among the explanations:[^8]

- **Fibroids:** non-cancerous growths in or around the uterus. See [uterine fibroids](/conditions/uterine-fibroids).
- **Endometriosis:** tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows in other places.
- **Adenomyosis:** that same kind of tissue grows into the muscular wall of the uterus.
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease:** an infection of the upper reproductive tract.
- **Bleeding disorders** such as von Willebrand disease, and medicines such as some anticoagulants.

Rarely, heavy periods are a sign of cancer of the uterus, which is one reason a lasting change should be checked.[^8]

### Endometriosis

> [!STAT] 1 in 10
> Roughly 10% of women and girls of reproductive age worldwide, about 190 million people, have endometriosis.[^11]

Endometriosis deserves a closer look because it is common and slow to be recognized. Its hallmark is severe period pain, often with pain during sex or when using the toilet, and sometimes difficulty getting pregnant.[^11]

A 2024 review of studies from several countries put the average wait between first symptoms and diagnosis at six to seven years, with estimates for individual countries ranging from about 1.5 to more than 11 years.[^12] ACOG advises doctors to consider endometriosis when significant period pain persists despite NSAIDs and hormonal treatment.[^2] There is no cure yet, but symptoms can be treated.[^11] Our [endometriosis](/conditions/endometriosis) page has more.

### Bleeding disorders

These are easy to miss. In a pooled analysis of 11 studies, 13% of women with heavy periods turned out to have von Willebrand disease, an inherited clotting problem.[^10] Frequent nosebleeds, easy bruising, or prolonged bleeding after dental work, surgery or childbirth are clues worth mentioning to your doctor.[^9]

## Treatments for heavy bleeding

Treatment depends on the cause and on whether you want to become pregnant. For heavy bleeding with no structural cause, NICE recommends a hormonal IUD as the first option to consider. Alternatives include tranexamic acid, a non-hormonal tablet taken only during your period, as well as NSAIDs and the combined pill.[^7]

When fibroids or another structural problem are responsible, the options extend to procedures such as removing the fibroids, removing the lining of the uterus (endometrial ablation) or hysterectomy.[^8]

## When to see a doctor

| | Usually ordinary | Worth a visit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain | Cramps in the first day or two that ease with an NSAID or heat | Pain that keeps you from work, school or sleep, is getting worse, or lingers after your period |
| Flow | Up to 7 days of bleeding | Changing protection every 1 to 2 hours, large clots, flooding, or more than 7 days |
| Timing | Bleeding only during your period | Bleeding between periods or after sex |

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if heavy periods are affecting your life, if you have severe period pain, or if you bleed between periods or after sex.[^8] Pain that does not improve with NSAIDs or hormonal treatment should be assessed for causes such as endometriosis.[^2] Get urgent care if you are soaking through protection every hour for several hours and feel dizzy or faint, if you have sudden severe pelvic pain, or if you have heavy bleeding or pain and could be pregnant.

> [!TIP] Bring data
> For two or three cycles, note how many pads or tampons you use on your heaviest days, any clots, your pain out of 10, and days of work or school missed. Specifics make it much harder for symptoms to be waved away. Our [menstrual cycle guide](/articles/menstrual-cycle-explained) covers what else is worth tracking.

## The bottom line

Cramps on the first day or two that respond to an anti-inflammatory and a heat pack are ordinary. Pain that stops your life, bleeding that soaks through protection every hour or two, and periods that leave you exhausted are reasons to see a doctor, not tests of character. Effective treatments exist for both problems, and asking early is the best way to shorten the long delays that conditions like endometriosis are known for.
