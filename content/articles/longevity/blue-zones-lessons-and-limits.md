The story is hard to resist. Scattered around the world are a handful of places where people forget to die. They tend their gardens, eat beans, drink a little wine with friends and reach 100 without ever setting foot in a gym.

Those places are called Blue Zones, and the idea has shaped how millions of people think about aging. It deserves a careful look, because two things are true at once: some of the underlying data are disputed, and most of the advice is sound anyway.

## What the Blue Zones idea claims

The term began with demographers. In 2004, a team that included Michel Poulain and Gianni Pes described a cluster of mountain villages in central Sardinia with an unusually high number of men reaching 100.[^1] They had marked the area on their map in blue ink, and the name stuck.[^2]

The journalist Dan Buettner, working with National Geographic, later extended the label to five places:[^2]

- The highlands of **Sardinia**, Italy
- The islands of **Okinawa**, Japan
- The **Nicoya** Peninsula, Costa Rica
- The island of **Ikaria**, Greece
- **Loma Linda**, California, home to a large Seventh-day Adventist community

The claim is that people in these places reach very old age at unusually high rates, and that the habits they share explain why.

## The shared habits

Buettner and his colleagues distilled nine common features, which they called the Power 9:[^2]

1. **Move naturally.** Walking, gardening and manual chores, not workouts.
2. **Purpose.** A clear reason to get up in the morning.
3. **Downshift.** Daily routines that shed stress, such as naps, prayer or a social hour.
4. **The 80% rule.** Stopping eating when you are about 80% full.
5. **Plant slant.** Beans as a staple, with meat eaten rarely and in small portions.
6. **Wine at 5.** Moderate, regular drinking with food and friends (the Adventists excepted).
7. **Belong.** Membership of a faith community.
8. **Loved ones first.** Family kept close, including aging parents.
9. **Right tribe.** Social circles that support healthy behavior.

## The limits: how solid are the data?

The habits are appealing. The harder question is whether the extraordinary ages are real, and whether the habits caused them.

**Age records are fragile.** Proving that someone is 105 requires a reliable birth record from more than a century ago. The researcher Saul Newman has shown that even a tiny rate of errors in age records can dominate the statistics at the oldest ages, because genuine 105-year-olds are so rare that mistakes easily outnumber them.[^3]

In a later analysis, first posted in 2019 as a preprint (a paper released before peer review) and awarded an Ig Nobel Prize in 2024, Newman went further. He argued that reports of extreme old age cluster in regions with poor record-keeping, low incomes and short average lifespans, a pattern he says is better explained by clerical errors and pension fraud than by lifestyle.

The Blue Zones researchers reject this. They say the ages in their study areas were checked individually against civil and church records, as the original Sardinian study describes.[^1] The dispute is not settled.

**The advantage can fade.** Okinawa was once the longest-lived prefecture in Japan. By 2000, life expectancy for Okinawan men had slipped to 26th of the country's 47 prefectures, as younger generations moved away from the traditional diet and way of life.[^4] That traditional diet was low in calories and built around sweet potatoes, other vegetables and soy.[^12] Whatever protected the oldest Okinawans, it was not something in the water, and it was not passed on automatically.

**Stories are not experiments.** Visitors notice the charming habits, such as wine at sunset, and can miss duller explanations: genes in isolated communities, a lifetime of physical labor, or a youth spent with limited food. With no comparison villages, nobody can say which habits mattered.

> [!NOTE]
> Loma Linda is a different kind of evidence. It does not rest on counting centenarians. Researchers followed tens of thousands of living Adventists and found that Californian Adventists lived longer than other Californians, by about seven years for men and four for women.[^5] In a later study of 73,308 Adventists, vegetarians had a 12% lower death rate than non-vegetarians over about six years.[^6]

## What holds up regardless of the labels

You do not need to believe in the zones to believe in most of the habits. Each can be checked against research that has nothing to do with Sardinia or Okinawa.

| Habit | Independent evidence | How strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mostly plants, beans, olive oil | In a trial of 7,447 adults at high cardiovascular risk, a Mediterranean diet with extra olive oil or nuts cut major cardiovascular events by about 30%[^7] | Strong |
| Daily movement | The World Health Organization recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week and says any amount is better than none[^8] | Strong |
| Family, friends and community | Across 148 studies, people with stronger social ties were 50% more likely to survive over the follow-up period[^9] | Strong, though observational |
| Sense of purpose | A meta-analysis of 10 studies linked a higher sense of purpose with about 17% lower mortality[^10] | Moderate, observational |
| Daily wine | The World Health Organization says no level of alcohol consumption is risk-free[^11] | Weak |

> [!STAT] 30%
> The reduction in heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths with a Mediterranean diet in a trial of 7,447 high-risk adults.[^7]

The wine habit is the clear outlier. It may be that wine in these communities is a marker of meals shared with other people, and that the company is doing the work.

## How to apply the sound parts

The most useful lesson from the Blue Zones is not any single food. It is that in these places the healthy choice was the default: people walked because that was how you got around, and ate beans because that was what there was. You can copy the principle.

- **Build movement into your day.** Walk for errands, take stairs, garden, carry your shopping.
- **Make plants the base of your plate.** Beans, lentils, vegetables and whole grains most days, with meat as a side. Our [Mediterranean diet guide](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide) shows how.
- **Eat with other people** when you can, and protect regular time with friends and family. See [loneliness and social connection](/articles/loneliness-and-social-connection).
- **Find a reason to get up.** Volunteering, caring for someone, a craft or a cause all count.
- **Keep alcohol low**, and do not start drinking for your health.
- **Skip the branded products.** Nothing with a Blue Zones label has been shown to add years.

For a wider view of what predicts a long life, see [the habits that predict a longer life](/articles/habits-that-predict-a-long-life).

> [!DOCTOR]
> Lifestyle works alongside medical care, not instead of it. Keep taking prescribed medicines and attending screenings. Talk to your doctor before making big changes to your diet if you take diabetes medication or the blood thinner warfarin, or if you have kidney disease, because your treatment may need adjusting.

## The bottom line

Treat the Blue Zones as a good story with uncertain statistics. The numbers of centenarians may be less remarkable than advertised, but walking daily, eating mostly plants, staying close to other people and having a purpose are each supported by independent evidence. Take the habits, leave the wine, and do not pay extra for the label.
