The longevity business will sell you cold plunges, peptide injections and blood tests with three-figure price tags. The habits with the strongest evidence behind them are older, cheaper and far less exciting.

That is the good news. The things most strongly linked with a long life are things you already know how to do. This guide covers what they are, how big the effect looks, how much your genes limit you, and why starting late still counts.

## The study that put a number on it

In 2018, researchers at Harvard published an analysis of about 123,000 US health professionals who had been followed for up to 34 years. They scored each person on five habits and compared how long people lived.[^1]

The gap was large. At age 50, women with all five habits could expect to live about 14 years longer than women with none, to roughly 93 instead of 79. For men the difference was about 12 years, roughly 88 instead of 76. People with all five were also 82% less likely to die of cardiovascular disease and 65% less likely to die of cancer during the study.[^1]

> [!STAT] 14 years
> The extra life expectancy at age 50 for women with five low-risk habits, compared with women who had none. For men the figure was about 12 years.[^1]

A follow-up asked the question most people care about more: are the extra years healthy ones? Women with four or five of the habits at 50 lived about 10 more years free of cancer, cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes than women with none. Men gained about 7 to 8 disease-free years.[^2]

These are observational studies of health professionals, with habits reported by questionnaire. They cannot prove that the habits caused the difference, and people with healthier habits often have other advantages too. What makes the result persuasive is that each habit is also supported by its own body of evidence.

## The five habits

| Habit | How the study defined low risk |
| --- | --- |
| Not smoking | Never smoked |
| Physical activity | At least 30 minutes a day of moderate or vigorous activity |
| Diet quality | In the top 40% on a healthy-eating score |
| Body weight | Body mass index of 18.5 to 24.9 |
| Alcohol | Moderate: 5 to 15 g a day for women, 5 to 30 g for men |

Each habit added years on its own, and the benefit grew with every one added.[^1]

### Not smoking

This is the biggest single lever. In a study of more than 200,000 US adults, smokers lost at least a decade of life compared with people who had never smoked. Those who quit before 40 avoided about 90% of the excess risk.[^3] Our guide to [quitting smoking and vaping](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping) covers the methods that work.

### Moving regularly

The study's threshold of 30 minutes a day matches what the World Health Organization recommends: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle strengthening on two days. Any amount is better than none.[^4] See [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need).

### Eating well

The healthy-eating score rewarded vegetables, fruit, whole grains, nuts, legumes and unsaturated fats, and marked people down for red and processed meat, sugary drinks and salt.[^1] A separate modeling study estimated that a 20-year-old who moved from a typical Western diet to an optimized one could gain more than a decade of life expectancy, with the largest gains from eating more legumes, whole grains and nuts and less red and processed meat.[^5] That figure comes from a model, not a trial, but it points in the same direction as the [Mediterranean diet](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide) evidence.

### Body weight

The study used body mass index, which is a blunt tool: it cannot tell muscle from fat or show where fat is carried. Our guide to [BMI, waist and body composition](/articles/bmi-waist-and-body-composition) explains better measures. The other four habits are worth having at any weight.

### Alcohol: the habit that has aged worst

The 2018 study counted moderate drinking as low risk, reflecting the evidence of its time. That view has shifted. A 2023 meta-analysis of 107 studies and about 4.8 million people corrected for known flaws in older research, such as counting people who quit drinking for health reasons as abstainers. After those corrections, low-volume drinkers did not live longer than lifetime non-drinkers, and risk rose at higher intakes.[^6] The World Health Organization now states that no form of alcohol consumption is risk-free.[^7]

The practical version of this habit: if you drink, less is better, and there is no health reason to start.

## Two more that belong on the list

**Sleep.** Adults should sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society.[^8]

**Social connection.** A meta-analysis of 148 studies and about 309,000 people found that those with stronger social relationships were 50% more likely to survive over the follow-up period. The authors noted that the size of the effect was comparable to that of quitting smoking.[^9] Time with other people is not a soft extra. See [loneliness and social connection](/articles/loneliness-and-social-connection).

## How much is down to your genes?

Less than most people assume. A classic study of 2,872 pairs of Danish twins estimated that about a quarter of the variation in lifespan is inherited.[^10] A 2018 analysis of family trees covering hundreds of millions of people argued that even this is too high. People tend to choose partners who resemble them in ways that affect lifespan, which inflates the estimates, and the true inherited share may be below 10%.[^11]

Either way, most of the variation is not written in your DNA. It comes from circumstances, behavior and luck, and only one of those is partly under your control.

## Healthspan: the years that feel like living

Lifespan is how long you live. Healthspan is how long you live in good health. Across 183 countries, the gap between the two averaged 9.6 years in a 2024 analysis, and the United States had the widest gap, at 12.4 years.[^12]

That is why the disease-free years in the follow-up study matter. The same habits that add years also appear to push illness later.[^2]

## It is not too late

The gains shrink with age, but they do not vanish.

- **Smoking:** people who quit between 45 and 54 gained about six years of life, and those who quit between 55 and 64 gained about four.[^3]
- **Diet:** in the modeling study, changing diet at 60 was estimated to add about eight years, and at 80 about three.[^5]
- **Activity:** the WHO guidelines apply to older adults too, and add balance and strength work on three days a week from age 65.[^4]

> [!TIP] Pick one
> You do not need all five habits by Monday. Each one added years on its own in the study. Choose the one that feels most doable, make it routine, then add the next. Our guide to [building habits that stick](/articles/how-to-build-habits-that-stick) can help.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Habits work alongside medical care, not instead of it. Keep up with blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar checks, cancer screening and vaccinations. Ask a doctor for help if you want to stop smoking or cut down on drinking and are finding it hard, because effective treatments exist. Stopping heavy drinking suddenly can be dangerous and should be supervised.

## The bottom line

Do not smoke, move most days, eat mostly plants and whole foods, keep alcohol low, sleep at least seven hours and stay close to other people. None of it is new and none of it needs a subscription. The evidence is observational but large and consistent, and the benefits apply whenever you start.
