Think of muscle as a retirement account. You pay in through your younger decades, mostly without noticing. Withdrawals begin automatically in midlife. And the size of the balance at 75 decides a great deal: whether a week in hospital is a setback or the start of a long decline, whether a stumble is a stumble or a broken hip.

Unlike most retirement accounts, this one accepts deposits at any age. This piece explains what happens to muscle over time, why strength predicts so much about later life, and how to keep adding to the balance.

## What age does to muscle

After about age 30, muscle mass falls by roughly 3% to 8% per decade, and the decline speeds up after 60.[^1]

The medical term for this loss, once it becomes severe enough to cause problems, is sarcopenia. European guidelines, revised in 2019, define it mainly by low strength, with low muscle mass used to confirm the diagnosis. The reason for putting strength first is that it predicts poor outcomes better than muscle size does. Sarcopenia raises the risk of falls and fractures, makes daily tasks harder, and is associated with loss of independence and earlier death.[^2]

Age is not the only cause. Inactivity, illness, time spent in bed and eating too little protein all speed the decline.[^2] That is the hopeful part, because most of those can be changed.

## Why grip strength keeps making headlines

Grip strength is cheap and quick to measure, so researchers use it as a stand-in for whole-body strength. The results are striking.

The PURE study measured grip in about 140,000 adults aged 35 to 70 in 17 countries and followed them for a median of four years. Every 5 kg (11 lb) lower grip strength was associated with a 16% higher risk of death from any cause and a 17% higher risk of death from cardiovascular disease. Grip strength predicted death better than systolic blood pressure did.[^3]

> [!STAT] 16%
> The higher risk of death associated with each 5 kg lower grip strength in a study of about 140,000 adults in 17 countries.[^3]

This is an association, and nobody thinks squeezing a hand gripper extends your life. Grip is a marker of how strong the rest of you is. The lesson is to train your whole body.

> [!TIP] Two checks you can do at home
> European guidelines flag probable sarcopenia when grip strength is below 27 kg in men or 16 kg in women, or when it takes more than 15 seconds to stand up from a chair five times without using your arms.[^2] The chair test needs only a stopwatch. If you are over the limit, mention it to your doctor and start strength work.

## Strength, falls and independence

More than one in four adults aged 65 and over falls each year.[^12] Weak legs and poor balance are among the main reasons, and both respond to training. A Cochrane review of 108 trials found that exercise programs, mostly built on balance and strength work, reduced the rate of falls by 23%.[^9] Our guide to [balance and fall prevention](/articles/balance-and-fall-prevention) has drills to try.

Strength also sets the margin between what daily life demands and what you can do. Rising from a low chair, carrying shopping and climbing stairs each need a certain amount of force. The more you have in reserve, the longer those tasks stay easy.

## It is never too late: the 90-year-olds who lifted

In 1990, researchers in Boston put 10 frail nursing home residents, with an average age of 90, through eight weeks of high-intensity resistance training for their legs. Strength rose by an average of 174%. Thigh muscle area grew by 9%, and walking speed improved.[^4]

That study was tiny and had no comparison group. So the team ran a randomized trial in 100 nursing home residents with an average age of 87. After 10 weeks, leg strength had risen by 113% in those who trained, against 3% in those who did not. Walking speed and stair-climbing power improved as well. A nutritional supplement without exercise made no difference.[^5]

The message has held up for three decades. The American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 guidance on resistance training concludes that the largest gain comes from moving from no training to some.[^8]

## Protein: older muscles need a stronger signal

The standard adult recommendation for protein is 0.8 g per kg of body weight a day. An international expert group, PROT-AGE, concluded that older adults need more to maintain muscle:[^6]

| Who | Daily protein |
| --- | --- |
| Healthy adults over 65 | 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg |
| Those who exercise regularly | 1.2 g per kg or more |
| Those with an acute or chronic illness | 1.2 to 1.5 g per kg |

For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, the first row works out to 70 to 84 g a day. The group also suggested spreading protein across meals, with about 25 to 30 g at each.[^6] Our [protein calculator](/tools/protein) and [protein guide](/articles/how-much-protein-do-you-need) cover food sources.

One exception: people with severe kidney disease who are not on dialysis may need to limit protein, and should follow their doctor's advice.[^6]

## Train for power, not only strength

Power is the ability to produce force quickly. It is what lets you catch yourself when you trip, or get across a road before the lights change.

The National Strength and Conditioning Association recommends that programs for older adults include power exercises done at higher speeds with moderate loads, around 40% to 60% of the most you could lift once.[^7] The ACSM's 2026 guidance likewise suggests moderate loads moved as fast as possible during the lifting phase, and links power training with better everyday mobility.[^8]

In practice this is simple. Stand up from a chair as fast as you safely can, then sit down slowly. Step up briskly onto a low step. Lift a moderate weight quickly and lower it under control.

## How to start safely

For healthy older adults, the National Strength and Conditioning Association suggests two or three sessions a week, with two to three sets of one or two multi-joint exercises for each major muscle group, working up to fairly heavy loads.[^7] The World Health Organization adds balance and strength-focused activity on three or more days a week from age 65.[^10]

Start well below that and build up over months. Good first exercises are sit-to-stands, push-ups against a wall or counter, rows with a resistance band, step-ups, heel raises and carrying a weight in each hand. Our [beginner strength plan](/articles/strength-training-for-beginners) and [exercise library](/exercises) show how.

If you have osteoporosis or low bone density, get individual advice first. Experts recommend protecting the spine by avoiding exercises that flex, bend or twist it.[^11]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Check with a doctor before starting if you have a heart condition, uncontrolled high blood pressure, a recent operation or a joint replacement. Ask for an assessment if you have lost weight without trying, find it newly difficult to rise from a chair or climb stairs, are walking noticeably slower or have had a fall. Sudden weakness on one side of the body, especially with facial drooping or trouble speaking, is an emergency: call your local emergency number.

## The bottom line

Muscle and strength decline with age, but the rate is largely up to you. Do resistance training two or three times a week, include some fast movements, and eat enough protein. Starting at 40 is better than starting at 70, and starting at 90 still works.
