Longevity has become a product category. There are powders that promise to recharge your cells, capsules derived from red wine, and online clinics that will prescribe a transplant drug to healthy people who want to live to 120.

The science behind these products is real, and some of it is exciting. It is also mostly about mice. This piece goes through the best-known longevity supplements and drugs, says what has been shown in humans, and points to what has stronger evidence than any of them.

## How to judge a longevity claim

Picture a ladder. On the bottom rung, a compound does something interesting in cells or worms. Next, it helps mice live longer. Then it shifts a marker in human blood or muscle. On the top rung, it prevents disease, disability or death in a randomized human trial.

Marketing tends to quote the lower rungs as if they were the top one. So far, none of the products below has reached the top.

## The contenders

### NAD boosters: NMN and NR

NAD is a molecule every cell uses to produce energy and repair damage. Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) are building blocks the body converts into it.

In the most cited human trial, 25 postmenopausal women with prediabetes took 250 mg of NMN or a placebo daily for 10 weeks. Muscle insulin sensitivity improved in the NMN group. Blood sugar, blood pressure, body weight and liver fat did not change.[^1] In a trial of NR, 40 men with obesity took a high dose for 12 weeks and showed no improvement in insulin sensitivity or other metabolic measures.[^2]

Neither study was designed to detect an effect on aging, and no trial has tested whether these supplements prevent any disease.

### Resveratrol

Resveratrol, a compound found in red wine and grapes, launched the modern longevity supplement market after promising laboratory results. Human data have been disappointing. In a study of 783 older adults in Tuscany, people with higher levels of resveratrol from their diet were no less likely to develop heart disease or cancer, or to die, over nine years of follow-up.[^3]

### Metformin

Metformin is a widely used diabetes medicine. Interest in it as an anti-aging drug grew from observational hints that people taking it had lower rates of cancer and death than expected. Observational hints are easy to get wrong, so researchers proposed a trial called TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin), designed to enroll about 3,000 adults aged 65 to 79 and track how quickly they develop major age-related diseases.[^4] It has not reported results.

There is also a reason for caution. In the MASTERS trial, older adults who took metformin during 14 weeks of supervised strength training gained less muscle than those who took a placebo.[^5] A drug that blunts the benefits of exercise is an odd choice for healthy aging.

### Rapamycin

Rapamycin has the strongest animal data of any candidate. In a landmark study, mice given the drug from late middle age lived longer: by about 9% in males and 14% in females.[^6]

In humans, rapamycin is a prescription drug that suppresses the immune system and is used after organ transplants. No trial has tested whether it extends human life, and its long-term safety in healthy people is unknown. Taking it for longevity is an experiment, not a treatment.

### Senolytics

Senolytics are compounds meant to clear out senescent cells: old cells that have stopped dividing but linger and release inflammatory signals. The most studied combination pairs dasatinib, a leukemia drug, with quercetin, a plant pigment.

Human evidence is at the earliest stage. In a preliminary report, nine people with diabetic kidney disease took a three-day course, and markers of senescent cells in their fat and skin fell 11 days later.[^7] That shows the drugs can reach their target. It does not show that anyone was healthier as a result, and dasatinib has serious side effects.

### Taurine

Taurine made headlines in 2023, when researchers reported that blood levels fell with age in mice, monkeys and people, and that supplements extended the lives of mice by about 10% to 12%.[^8] The authors themselves called for human trials before anyone drew conclusions.

In 2025, a study led by US government scientists found that taurine levels did not consistently decline with age across several groups of people and animals, casting doubt on its value as a marker of aging.[^9] No human longevity trial has been completed.

### Omega-3 and vitamin D

These two have something the others lack: a large randomized trial in older adults. DO-HEALTH enrolled 2,157 generally healthy people aged 70 and over in five European countries and tested vitamin D (2,000 IU a day), omega-3 (1 g a day) and a home strength program for three years. None of them significantly improved any of the six main outcomes: systolic and diastolic blood pressure, physical performance, cognitive function, fractures and infections.[^10]

> [!NOTE]
> This does not make vitamin D or omega-3 useless. People who are deficient in vitamin D benefit from correcting it, and omega-3 has specific uses in heart health. See [vitamin D deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-d-deficiency) and [omega-3, fish oil and your heart](/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-and-your-heart).

| Compound | Best human evidence so far | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NMN, NR | Small trials; one narrow benefit, no outcome data | Unproven |
| Resveratrol | No link with longevity in a cohort study | Disappointing |
| Metformin | No completed aging trial; blunted muscle gains | Not for healthy people |
| Rapamycin | None on lifespan; immune-suppressing drug | Experimental |
| Senolytics | Pilot study of nine people | Far too early |
| Taurine | No human trial; conflicting marker data | Unproven |

## Why the shelves are full anyway

Supplements reach the market by a different route from medicines. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration does not approve dietary supplements for safety or effectiveness before they are sold. The company is responsible for making sure its product is safe and its label is truthful, and the agency mostly acts after problems emerge.[^11]

That leaves room for products whose contents and doses vary. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements advises looking for a quality seal from an independent testing organization, and notes that such a seal confirms what is in the bottle, not that the product works.[^12]

## The real risks

- **Interactions.** Supplements can interfere with prescription medicines or with surgery.[^12]
- **Unknown long-term effects.** A 10-week study says little about 10 years of daily use.
- **Prescription drugs without supervision.** Rapamycin and metformin are medicines with side effects that need monitoring.
- **Opportunity cost.** Money and attention spent on capsules are not spent on things that work.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every supplement you take, especially if you use blood thinners, diabetes medicines or drugs that affect the immune system, or if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or due to have surgery. Do not take prescription drugs obtained online for longevity without medical supervision. Seek urgent help for signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face or throat or difficulty breathing.

## What has better evidence than any pill

The habits most strongly linked with a longer life are free: not smoking, regular exercise, a good diet, enough sleep and strong relationships. Our guide to [the habits that predict a longer life](/articles/habits-that-predict-a-long-life) sets out the numbers, and [who needs vitamins and supplements](/articles/vitamins-and-supplements-who-needs-them) covers the cases where a supplement is justified.

## The bottom line

None of these pills has been shown to extend human life. Animal results are a reason for scientists to run trials, not a reason for you to buy. If you want to spend money on longevity, spend it on good food, a gym membership or decent walking shoes, and ask your doctor before adding any supplement to prescribed medicines.
