There is no pill that prevents dementia and no habit that guarantees you will avoid it. Age is the biggest risk factor, and genes matter. For a long time that led to a shrug: nothing to be done.

That view is out of date. Researchers now have good evidence that a large share of dementia risk is tied to things that can be changed, some by you and some by governments. This guide covers what those factors are, how solid the evidence is, and where the hype outruns the science.

## The headline number: 45%

Dementia is an umbrella term for conditions, Alzheimer's disease being the most common, that damage memory, thinking and the ability to manage daily life. About 57 million people were living with it in 2021, and there are nearly 10 million new cases every year.[^2]

In 2024, the Lancet Commission on dementia, an international panel of experts, reviewed the evidence and identified 14 modifiable risk factors. It estimated that about 45% of dementia cases worldwide could in theory be prevented or delayed if all of them were eliminated.[^1]

> [!STAT] 45%
> The share of dementia cases worldwide linked to 14 modifiable risk factors, according to the 2024 Lancet Commission.[^1]

Read that number carefully. It is an estimate for whole populations, it rests largely on observational studies, and it assumes that every factor could be removed entirely. It does not mean that an individual who ticks every box is 45% protected. It does mean the scope for prevention is far bigger than most people think.

## The 14 risk factors

The Commission grouped the factors by the stage of life at which they matter most. The percentages show roughly how much of the world's dementia each one accounts for.[^1]

| Life stage | Risk factor | Share of cases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early life | Less education | 5% |
| Midlife | Hearing loss | 7% |
| Midlife | High LDL cholesterol | 7% |
| Midlife | Depression | 3% |
| Midlife | Traumatic brain injury | 3% |
| Midlife | Physical inactivity | 2% |
| Midlife | Diabetes | 2% |
| Midlife | Smoking | 2% |
| Midlife | High blood pressure | 2% |
| Midlife | Obesity | 1% |
| Midlife | Excessive alcohol | 1% |
| Later life | Social isolation | 5% |
| Later life | Air pollution | 3% |
| Later life | Untreated vision loss | 2% |

Two things stand out. First, a factor's share depends on how common it is as well as how harmful, which is why hearing loss and high LDL cholesterol top the list. High LDL cholesterol and untreated vision loss were both added in the 2024 update.[^1]

Second, many of the items would fit on a list of heart risks. What damages blood vessels damages the brain, and midlife is when most of that damage starts.

## What the trials show

Risk factors come mostly from observational research. Randomized trials, which test whether changing a factor changes the outcome, are fewer and harder to run, because dementia develops over decades. Three are worth knowing.

**Hearing.** The ACHIEVE trial enrolled 977 adults aged 70 to 84 with untreated hearing loss and gave half of them hearing aids and audiology support. Over three years, the intervention did not slow cognitive decline in the group as a whole. In the participants at higher risk, who were older and had more cardiovascular risk factors, it slowed decline by 48%.[^3]

**Blood pressure.** In SPRINT MIND, 9,361 adults aged 50 and over with high blood pressure were treated to a systolic target of either below 120 or below 140 mm Hg. Intensive treatment reduced mild cognitive impairment by 19%. Probable dementia was 17% lower, but that difference was not statistically significant.[^4] The Lancet Commission recommends aiming for a systolic pressure of 130 mm Hg or less from age 40.[^1]

**Combined lifestyle changes.** The FINGER trial gave 1,260 older Finns at raised risk a two-year program of diet advice, exercise, cognitive training and monitoring of vascular risk. Their overall scores on cognitive tests improved 25% more than those of a comparison group.[^5] The trial measured test performance, not dementia diagnoses.

None of these is the final word. Together they suggest that acting on risk factors can protect thinking, particularly in people whose risk is already raised.

## What you can do

- **Look after your hearing and sight.** Get a hearing test if conversations in noisy rooms have become hard work, wear hearing aids if you need them, and protect your ears from loud noise. Have regular eye tests, and treat cataracts and other correctable problems.[^1]
- **Protect your blood vessels.** Keep blood pressure, LDL cholesterol and blood sugar in a healthy range, and do not smoke.[^1] Our guides to [blood pressure numbers](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) and [cholesterol](/articles/cholesterol-explained) explain the targets.
- **Move regularly.** Physical inactivity is on the list, and exercise also helps with blood pressure, diabetes, weight and mood.[^1]
- **Protect your head.** Wear a helmet for cycling and contact sports, and a seat belt in cars.[^1]
- **Stay connected, and treat depression.** Social isolation carries as large a share as less education. Depression is treatable, and getting help matters for its own sake.[^1] See [loneliness and social connection](/articles/loneliness-and-social-connection).
- **Drink less.** The Commission counts more than 21 UK units a week, roughly two bottles of wine, as excessive.[^1]
- **Keep learning.** Education early in life and mentally demanding work or hobbies later are linked with lower risk.[^1]

Air pollution is the one item individuals can do little about. It is mainly a matter for public policy.

## What does not work, or is not proven

**Most supplements.** A Cochrane review of vitamin and mineral supplements in healthy middle-aged and older adults found no evidence of a meaningful effect on cognitive decline or dementia.[^6] Ginkgo biloba, one of the most popular memory supplements, was tested in 3,069 adults aged 75 and over and did not reduce dementia.[^7]

> [!NOTE]
> One result is worth watching. In the COSMOS-Mind trial of more than 2,000 older adults, a daily multivitamin modestly improved scores on cognitive tests over three years compared with placebo.[^9] That is not the same as preventing dementia, and it does not make a multivitamin a substitute for the measures above.

**Brain-training games.** A Cochrane review of computerized cognitive training in healthy older adults found only low-quality evidence of small, short-term benefits, and nothing to show that it prevents dementia.[^8] Staying mentally active through work, learning and hobbies is associated with lower risk.[^1] You do not need an app for that.

**Anything sold as preventing Alzheimer's.** No supplement has been shown to do so. If a product makes that promise, treat it as a red flag.

## When to see a doctor

Forgetting where you left your keys is normal. See a doctor if memory or thinking changes start to disrupt daily life: repeating the same questions, getting lost in familiar places, struggling with tasks you used to manage, losing words, or changes in mood and personality.[^10] Our guide to [early signs versus normal aging](/articles/dementia-early-signs-vs-normal-aging) goes through the difference.

An assessment is worth having early. Some causes of memory problems can be treated, and a diagnosis opens the door to support and planning.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Sudden confusion that develops over hours or days is not dementia and should never be left to pass. It can signal an infection, a medication problem or a stroke. Seek urgent medical help, and call your local emergency number if it comes with facial drooping, weakness or trouble speaking.

## The bottom line

You cannot change your age or your genes, but about 45% of dementia is linked to factors that can be changed. The most useful steps are unglamorous: treat hearing loss, control blood pressure and cholesterol, stay active and connected, protect your head, and do not smoke or drink heavily. Save your money on supplements and brain games.
