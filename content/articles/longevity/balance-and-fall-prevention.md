Most people train their heart and some train their muscles. Almost nobody trains their balance, until the day a curb, a wet floor or a loose rug shows them it has gone.

Balance is a skill, and like any skill it fades without practice and improves with it. This guide explains why it matters, gives you a 10-second test, and sets out drills and home fixes that are proven to prevent falls.

## Why balance deserves a place in your routine

Falls are far more serious than their everyday name suggests. The World Health Organization estimates that 684,000 people die from falls each year, which makes falls the second leading cause of unintentional injury death worldwide, after road traffic injuries. Adults over 60 suffer the greatest number of fatal falls.[^1]

> [!STAT] 684,000
> The estimated number of people who die from falls worldwide each year.[^1]

They are also common. In the United States, more than one in four adults aged 65 and over reports falling in a given year.[^2] A fall that causes a hip fracture or a head injury can end an independent life, and even a harmless one can leave a fear of falling that makes people move less and grow weaker.

Staying upright depends on your eyes, your inner ears, the sensors in your feet and joints, your leg strength and your reaction speed. All of them decline with age. All of them decline faster with disuse.

## Test yourself: the 10-second one-leg stand

Stand next to a kitchen counter in bare feet. Lift one foot and rest it against the back of your other calf. Keep your arms by your sides, look straight ahead and count to 10. You get up to three attempts on either leg.[^3]

This is the test used in a Brazilian study of 1,702 people aged 51 to 75. About one in five could not hold the position for 10 seconds, and the proportion rose steeply with age. Over a median of seven years, those who failed were 84% more likely to die than those who passed, after accounting for age, sex, body mass index and health conditions.[^3]

That does not mean wobbling causes death. Balance is a marker of how well many body systems are working together. Failing the test is a prompt to start training and to mention it at your next appointment, not a prediction.

## The exercise that prevents falls

Not all exercise protects against falls. The kind that does has been tested extensively.

A Cochrane review of 108 randomized trials with 23,407 participants found that exercise reduced the rate of falls in older people living at home by 23%.[^4] The details matter:

| Type of program | Reduction in rate of falls |
| --- | --- |
| Balance and functional exercises | 24% |
| Balance and functional exercises plus resistance training | 34% |
| Tai chi | 19% |
| Walking programs alone | Uncertain |

Functional exercises are practice versions of daily movements, such as standing up from a chair or stepping over an obstacle. The evidence for tai chi was rated less certain than for the first two rows.[^4]

Two specific programs stand out. The Otago Exercise Program, a set of leg-strengthening and balance exercises done at home with a walking plan, reduced falls and fall injuries by 35% in a pooled analysis of four trials.[^5] And in a trial of 670 adults aged 70 and over at high risk of falling, a tai chi program done twice a week for 24 weeks cut falls by 58% compared with stretching, and by 31% compared with a mixed exercise class.[^6]

How much do you need? The World Health Organization recommends that adults aged 65 and over do varied activity that emphasizes balance and strength on three or more days a week.[^7] International falls guidelines add that programs should run for at least 12 weeks, and continue beyond that for a lasting effect.[^9] The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends exercise for adults aged 65 and over who are at increased risk of falls.[^2]

## Balance drills by level

Do these next to a counter or a sturdy chair, in bare feet or flat shoes. Hold on as lightly as you can. Ten minutes on most days is enough. Move up a level when you can do every drill in your current one steadily.

### Level 1: steady start

- **Feet-together stand:** stand with your feet touching for 30 seconds.
- **Semi-tandem stand:** place one foot half a step in front of the other. Hold 30 seconds on each side.
- **Sit-to-stand:** rise from a chair without using your hands, 10 times.
- **Heel raises:** rise onto your toes and lower slowly, 10 to 15 times.

### Level 2: narrower base

- **Tandem stand:** stand heel to toe, as if on a tightrope, for 30 seconds on each side.
- **One-leg stand:** hold for 10 to 30 seconds on each leg.
- **Heel-to-toe walk:** take 10 to 20 steps along the counter, placing each heel directly in front of the other foot's toes.
- **Sideways steps:** take 10 steps to the left and 10 to the right.

### Level 3: add a challenge

- **One-leg stand with head turns:** look slowly left and right while balancing.
- **Backward heel-to-toe walk:** 10 steps, with the counter beside you.
- **Clock reaches:** stand on one leg and tap the other foot forward, to the side and behind you.
- **A class:** tai chi and dance both train balance, and a group makes it easier to keep going.

> [!TIP] Attach it to something you already do
> Stand on one leg while you brush your teeth or wait for the kettle. Strong legs matter as much as steady ones, so pair these drills with strength training twice a week. See [strength and aging](/articles/muscle-strength-and-aging) and our [exercise library](/exercises).

## Fix what surrounds you

Training your body is half the job. The rest is removing avoidable risks.

**Your home.** A Cochrane review found that assessing and fixing home hazards reduced falls by 26% overall, and by 38% in people at higher risk, such as those who had already fallen.[^8] Start with the usual suspects: loose rugs, clutter and trailing cables, poor lighting on stairs and on the route to the bathroom at night, and slippery bathroom floors. Add handrails and non-slip mats where you need them.[^10]

**Your medicines.** Some medicines increase fall risk, including sedatives, sleeping tablets and some drugs for mood and blood pressure. Guidelines recommend a medication review for older people at risk of falling.[^9] Ask your doctor or pharmacist for one, and do not stop any medicine on your own.

**Your eyes.** Have your sight tested regularly, and get cataracts and outdated prescriptions dealt with.[^10]

**Your feet.** Wear shoes that fit well and grip, and avoid walking on smooth floors in socks or tights.[^10]

**Alcohol.** It slows reactions and affects balance, more so as you get older.[^10]

## When to get help

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you have had two or more falls in the past year, have been injured by a fall, feel unsteady on your feet or dizzy when you stand up, or have started avoiding activities for fear of falling.[^9] Call your local emergency number after a fall if the person cannot get up or bear weight, has severe pain, hit their head and is confused, drowsy or taking blood thinners, or if loss of balance comes on suddenly with facial drooping, arm weakness or trouble speaking.

Persistent dizziness or a spinning sensation is a separate problem with its own causes and treatments. Our guide to [dizziness and vertigo](/articles/dizziness-and-vertigo) explains them. If you have already had a fall or a fracture, ask about a bone health check too, as covered in [osteoporosis and bone health](/articles/osteoporosis-and-bone-health).

## The bottom line

Balance is trainable at any age, and training it is the best-proven way to prevent falls. Test yourself with the 10-second one-leg stand, practice balance drills for about 10 minutes on most days, keep your legs strong, and fix the hazards in your home. If you have fallen more than once or feel unsteady, see a doctor instead of working around it.
