The wellness industry has discovered measurement. For a monthly fee you can now track a hundred blood markers, estimate your "biological age" and lie in a scanner that photographs every organ. It feels like taking control.

Most of it is noise. A small set of ordinary measurements carries nearly all the useful information about your future health, and your doctor can order every one of them. Here are the seven worth tracking, how often to check them, and the popular tests to be careful with.

## What makes a marker worth tracking

A good marker passes three tests. It predicts outcomes you care about, such as heart attack, stroke, disability or early death. You can do something about it. And it is reliable enough that a change in the number means a change in you, not in the test. The seven below pass all three.

## The seven worth tracking

### 1. Blood pressure

High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, so measuring is the only way to find it. A normal reading is below 120/80 mm Hg, and US guidelines class 130/80 mm Hg or higher as hypertension.[^2]

The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all adults: every year from age 40, or earlier if you are at increased risk, and every three to five years for adults aged 18 to 39 with normal readings and no risk factors. High readings should be confirmed with measurements outside the clinic before a diagnosis is made.[^1] Our [blood pressure checker](/tools/blood-pressure) shows where a reading falls.

### 2. LDL cholesterol or ApoB

LDL cholesterol is the cholesterol carried by the particles that lodge in artery walls. European guidelines treat it as a direct cause of cardiovascular disease, with risk falling as the level falls.[^3]

ApoB is a related blood test that counts those particles instead of weighing their cholesterol. The same guidelines say ApoB can be used as an alternative to LDL cholesterol and may be preferred in people with high triglycerides, diabetes, obesity or very low LDL cholesterol. They also recommend measuring lipoprotein(a), an inherited risk marker, at least once in adult life.[^3]

The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute advises a cholesterol test about every five years for younger adults, every one to two years from middle age, and yearly after 65.[^4] Your target depends on your overall risk, so interpret the result with your doctor. See [cholesterol explained](/articles/cholesterol-explained).

### 3. HbA1c or fasting glucose

HbA1c, often shortened to A1C, reflects your average blood sugar over roughly the past three months. Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% indicates prediabetes, and 6.5% or higher indicates diabetes.[^5] The equivalent fasting glucose bands are below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L), and 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher.[^6]

The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening adults aged 35 to 70 who have overweight or obesity, and suggests that repeating the test every three years is reasonable when results are normal.[^6] Prediabetes is the stage at which lifestyle changes work best, as [blood sugar basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) explains.

### 4. Waist-to-height ratio

Fat carried around the middle is more harmful than fat elsewhere, and body mass index cannot see where fat sits. The fix is a tape measure. The NHS advises keeping your waist to less than half your height: a ratio of 0.5 or higher means your health risks may be increased.[^7]

Measure around your middle, roughly halfway between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hips, after breathing out normally. Our [waist-to-height calculator](/tools/waist-to-height) does the division.

### 5. Cardiorespiratory fitness

How well your heart, lungs and muscles deliver and use oxygen is among the strongest predictors of how long you live. The American Heart Association has argued that fitness should be treated as a clinical vital sign, noting that it can predict mortality more strongly than smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol or type 2 diabetes. Each 1-MET increase, a modest step up in fitness, is associated with a 10% to 25% improvement in survival.[^8]

You can have fitness measured in a lab, or follow the trend of the estimate on a sports watch. Our guide to [zone 2 and VO2 max](/articles/zone-2-and-vo2-max) explains how far to trust each.

### 6. Strength: grip and the chair-stand test

Low strength predicts falls, disability and death better than low muscle mass does. European guidelines flag probable sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle, when grip strength is below 27 kg in men or 16 kg in women, or when standing up from a chair five times takes more than 15 seconds.[^9]

The chair test needs only a stopwatch. For what to do about the result, see [strength and aging](/articles/muscle-strength-and-aging).

### 7. Kidney function

Chronic kidney disease is usually silent until it is advanced. Two tests find it early. The eGFR blood test estimates how well your kidneys filter: 60 or above is in the normal range. The urine albumin test checks for leaking protein: 30 mg/g or less is normal.[^10]

Testing matters most if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease or a family history of kidney failure.[^10]

| Marker | Healthy range for most adults | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | Below 120/80 mm Hg | Yearly from 40; every 3 to 5 years before that if normal |
| LDL cholesterol or ApoB | Depends on your overall risk | About every 5 years; more often from midlife |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7% | About every 3 years from 35 if you have overweight |
| Waist-to-height ratio | Below 0.5 | Any time, at home |
| Fitness | Higher for your age is better | Follow the trend |
| Chair stand | Five stands in 15 seconds or less | Any time, at home |
| eGFR and urine albumin | 60 or above; 30 mg/g or less | As your doctor advises, yearly if at risk |

## Tests to treat with caution

**Full-body MRI.** The pitch is appealing: find problems before symptoms start. But a systematic review of 12 studies covering 5,373 people without symptoms found that about a third had findings that needed follow-up, and it found no evidence that the scans improved survival or were cost-effective.[^11] Many such findings turn out to be harmless, but only after more scans, biopsies and worry.

**Biological age tests.** Epigenetic clocks estimate age from chemical tags on your DNA. They are valuable in research on large groups. For individuals they are shaky: one analysis found that technical noise alone can make the same blood sample differ by up to nine years on some clocks.[^12] No trial has shown that acting on your result improves your health.

**Giant blood panels.** The more things you measure in a healthy person, the more likely one of them will fall outside the normal range by chance. Each borderline result invites another test.

> [!WARNING] More testing is not more safety
> Testing healthy people can cause overdiagnosis: finding abnormalities that would never have caused harm, then treating them anyway. Screening tests earn a recommendation only when trials show that the benefits outweigh this cost. That is why the list of recommended tests is short. See [health screenings by age](/articles/health-screenings-by-age).

## What to do with your results

- **Record them.** A note on your phone with dates is enough. Trends matter more than single readings.
- **Confirm before you worry.** One high reading is a reason to repeat the test, not a diagnosis.[^1]
- **Act on the changeable ones.** Activity, diet, sleep, weight, alcohol and smoking move most of these markers. Medication is appropriate when your overall risk is high enough, which is a decision to make with your doctor.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor promptly if home blood pressure readings are consistently 130/80 mm Hg or higher, or if a blood test shows an HbA1c of 6.5% or more or an eGFR below 60. A blood pressure reading above 180/120 mm Hg with chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness, or changes in vision or speech is an emergency: call your local emergency number.[^2]

## The bottom line

You do not need a subscription to know where you stand. Check your blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, waist, fitness, strength and kidney function on a sensible schedule, write the results down, and work on the ones that are off. Be skeptical of tests that promise to find everything: in healthy people, they mostly find false alarms.
