You wait three weeks for the appointment, spend ten minutes in the room and remember your most important question in the parking lot. It happens to almost everyone, and it is not a personal failing. Appointments are short, the setting is stressful and human memory is not built for it.

The fix is not to become a "difficult patient". It is to arrive prepared, ask a few specific questions and check that you have understood the answers. This guide covers what to do before, during and after a visit, how to share a decision and what to say when you feel you are not being heard.

## Before: ten minutes of preparation

**Rank your concerns.** Write down everything you want to raise, then put the most important item first. The US National Institute on Aging suggests exactly this: make a list and prioritize it.[^1]

Leading with your main worry matters more than you might think. In a study of 112 recorded visits in the US, clinicians asked what the patient wanted to discuss in only 36% of them. When they did ask, they interrupted the answer in about two out of three cases, after a median of 11 seconds.[^7] Open with your headline.

> [!STAT] 11 seconds
> The median time before clinicians interrupted patients who were explaining why they had come, in one study of recorded US visits.[^7]

**Build a symptom timeline.** Note when the problem started, what it feels like, how often it happens, what makes it better or worse, what you have already tried and how it affects your day. A clear story is the most useful thing you can bring.

**List what you take.** Include every prescription medicine, over-the-counter product, vitamin and herbal supplement, with doses, or put them all in a bag and bring them along.[^1] Supplements count: they can interact with medicines and affect [blood test results](/articles/understanding-blood-tests).

**Update your history.** Mention anything that has changed since your last visit, such as an emergency department visit, a new diagnosis from another clinician or a serious illness in a close relative.[^1] If any [screenings](/articles/health-screenings-by-age) are due, add them to your list.

**Sort out the practical things.** Bring your glasses and hearing aids, and ask in advance if you need an interpreter.[^1]

Does preparation work? A Cochrane review of more than 30 randomized trials found that giving patients question lists or coaching before a consultation produced small increases in the number of questions they asked and in their satisfaction.[^11] The effects were modest, but so is the effort.

> [!TIP] A one-line opener
> Try: "I have three things today. The one I am most worried about is..." It tells the clinician how to spend the time before the clock starts running.

## During: get the answers you came for

**Ask three questions.** The Ask Me 3 program boils a visit down to three things you should know before you leave:[^4]

1. What is my main problem?
2. What do I need to do?
3. Why is it important for me to do this?

If you can answer all three on the way out, the appointment worked.

**Repeat it back.** Memory for medical information is poor. One review found that people forget 40% to 80% of what a clinician tells them almost immediately, and that nearly half of what they do remember is wrong.[^6]

The remedy is a technique clinicians are taught, called teach-back: the patient restates the plan in their own words, and the clinician corrects any gaps.[^5] You can start it yourself. Say: "Let me check I have this right. I take the new tablet in the morning, I stop the old one, and I come back in six weeks."

**Take notes, or bring someone.** Write down the key points, or ask for written instructions.[^3] A relative or friend can act as a second pair of ears, and it helps to tell them beforehand what you want from them: note-taking, a reminder of your questions or simply company.[^1]

**Be frank.** Clinicians can only work with what you tell them. Alcohol, smoking, sex, missed doses and remedies bought online are all relevant, and they have heard it before.[^1][^2]

## Deciding together: BRAN

Many medical choices do not have a single right answer. Shared decision-making means you and your clinician reach a decision together, combining the evidence with your own preferences and values.[^8]

A mnemonic used in UK shared-decision-making campaigns makes this easy to remember. For any test or treatment, ask about BRAN:

- **B**enefits: what is this likely to do for me?
- **R**isks: what could go wrong, and how often?
- **A**lternatives: what else could we do?
- **N**othing: what happens if we wait or do nothing?

Ask for numbers in a form you can picture. NICE, which writes clinical guidance for England, advises clinicians to give risks as "10 in 100" style figures and as absolute risks, not relative ones, because they are easier to understand.[^8] "How many people out of 100 like me are helped by this?" is a fair question.

It is also fine to ask, "Do I need to decide today?" For most non-urgent choices, the answer is no.

## After: results, records and second opinions

Before you leave, make sure you know what happens next: which tests are booked, how and when you will get the results, whom to contact with questions and which symptoms should bring you back sooner.[^3]

**Get your records.** In the US, federal law gives you the right to see and obtain copies of your health records.[^9] Many other countries have similar rules, and patient portals increasingly show results and visit notes. Reading them is a good way to catch errors and to remember what was agreed.

**Consider a second opinion.** Diagnosis is difficult, and it sometimes goes wrong. A 2015 report from the US National Academies concluded that most people will experience at least one diagnostic error in their lifetime.[^12] For a serious diagnosis, major surgery or a treatment that is not working, asking another clinician to review things is ordinary practice.

## Video and phone appointments

Remote consultations reward the same preparation, plus a little setting up. US government telehealth guidance suggests the following.[^10]

- Test your device, camera, microphone and connection ahead of time.
- Choose a quiet, private, well-lit place.
- Keep your medicines list, pharmacy details and any home readings, such as blood pressure or temperature, within reach.

Remote visits suit follow-ups and straightforward problems. If you think you need to be examined, say so when you book.

## If you feel dismissed

Sometimes a visit ends and the worry does not. You are allowed to say so. Useful phrases:

- "I am still concerned about this. What else could it be?"
- "What would you expect to see if it were something more serious?"
- "What should make me come back, and how soon?"
- "Can you help me understand why a test or referral is not needed?"

These are questions, not accusations, and a good clinician will welcome them. If your symptoms continue, book a follow-up and say that the problem has not gone away. You can also ask to see a different clinician, or bring someone to back you up.

> [!DOCTOR]
> No amount of preparation replaces urgent care. Chest pain, signs of a stroke, severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding or thoughts of harming yourself need your local emergency number, not a routine slot. Our guide to [emergency, urgent or wait](/articles/emergency-or-not) explains how to choose.

## The bottom line

Spend ten minutes before the visit ranking your concerns and listing your medicines. In the room, lead with what matters most, ask the three questions and repeat the plan back. For decisions, think BRAN. And if you leave unconvinced, say so and follow up: a good appointment is a conversation, and you are half of it.
