Modern gadgets will happily report your skin temperature, a "readiness score" and how stressed your watch thinks you are. Most of that is noise. A much shorter list of numbers has decades of evidence behind it, clear healthy ranges and, usefully, something you can do when a number drifts.

Here are eight worth knowing: what a healthy value looks like, how to measure it and how often to check. Ranges are for adults in general. Yours may differ if you are pregnant, have a long-term condition or take certain medicines, so treat a surprising result as a reason to talk to a clinician, not as a diagnosis.

## The list at a glance

| Number | Healthy for most adults | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | Below 120/80 mm Hg | Yearly from age 40 |
| LDL cholesterol | Below 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) | Every 4 to 6 years if low risk |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7% | Ask your clinician |
| Waist-to-height ratio | 0.4 to 0.49 | Every few months |
| Resting heart rate | 60 to 100 beats a minute | Weekly |
| Activity | 150 minutes or more a week | Weekly |
| Sleep | 7 hours or more a night | A two-week check now and then |
| Fitness test | Better than your last one | Every few months |

## 1. Blood pressure

High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, which is why it tops the list. The American Heart Association classes a reading below 120/80 mm Hg as normal.[^1] The threshold for a diagnosis differs between countries, as our guide to [blood pressure numbers](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) explains.

**Measure it:** use a validated automatic cuff on your upper arm. Sit quietly for five minutes with your back supported, feet flat and arm resting at heart level, then take two readings a minute apart.[^3] The [blood pressure checker](/tools/blood-pressure) shows where a reading falls.

**How often:** the US Preventive Services Task Force suggests every year from age 40, or earlier if you are at higher risk, and every three to five years for younger adults with normal readings.[^2]

## 2. LDL or non-HDL cholesterol

LDL cholesterol is the kind that builds up in artery walls. For healthy adults, MedlinePlus lists an LDL below 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) and a non-HDL cholesterol below 130 mg/dL (3.4 mmol/L) as healthy levels.[^4] Non-HDL is simply total cholesterol minus HDL, and some laboratories report it alongside LDL.

These are general reference points. If you already have heart disease or diabetes, your clinician will probably aim lower, as [cholesterol, explained](/articles/cholesterol-explained) sets out.

**How often:** the American Heart Association advises a test every four to six years from age 20 while your risk stays low, and more often if it does not.[^5]

## 3. HbA1c or fasting glucose

HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over about three months. Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% indicates prediabetes and 6.5% or higher indicates diabetes. For a fasting glucose test, the matching bands are 99 mg/dL (5.5 mmol/L) or lower, 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) and 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher.[^6]

**How often:** this one needs a blood sample, so the schedule is your clinician's call. It depends on your age, weight, family history and previous results.

## 4. Waist-to-height ratio

Fat carried around the middle is more closely tied to health risk than weight alone. The rule of thumb is to keep your waist to less than half your height. The UK's NICE classes a ratio of 0.4 to 0.49 as healthy, 0.5 to 0.59 as increased risk and 0.6 or more as high risk, for adults with a BMI under 35.[^7]

**Measure it:** wrap a tape around your middle, halfway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone. Breathe out normally and do not pull in. Divide your waist by your height in the same units, or use our [waist-to-height calculator](/tools/waist-to-height).

> [!STAT] Under 0.5
> The waist-to-height ratio to aim for. Put plainly: your waist should measure less than half your height.[^7]

## 5. Resting heart rate

For adults, a normal resting heart rate is 60 to 100 beats a minute. Fit, active people often sit lower, and a well-trained athlete may be nearer 40.[^8]

**Measure it:** sit quietly for a few minutes, find the pulse at your wrist, count the beats for 30 seconds and double it. First thing in the morning gives the steadiest figure. Emotions, medicines, body position and air temperature can all shift it, so one high reading means little.[^8] See [resting heart rate and HRV](/articles/resting-heart-rate-and-hrv) for more.

## 6. Weekly activity minutes

The World Health Organization recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on two or more days.[^9]

**Measure it:** add up a normal week. Brisk walks, cycling to work and heavy gardening all count. A phone or watch can do the sum for you, but a note on the fridge works too. Our guide to [how much exercise you need](/articles/how-much-exercise-do-you-need) shows what qualifies.

## 7. Sleep duration

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep seven or more hours a night on a regular basis.[^10]

**Measure it:** write down when you fall asleep and when you wake for two weeks, including weekends. The average is your number. You do not need to track it every night forever.

## 8. A simple fitness marker

Cardiorespiratory fitness, meaning how well your heart, lungs and muscles deliver and use oxygen, is one of the strongest predictors of long-term health. In 2016 the American Heart Association argued that it should be treated as a vital sign, noting that it may predict the risk of death better than smoking, high blood pressure or high cholesterol do. Even small gains were linked with roughly 10% to 30% fewer cardiovascular events.[^11] Those figures come from observational studies, so they show association, not proof.

**Measure it:** you do not need a laboratory. Pick one test, repeat it under the same conditions every few months and compete only with yourself.

- **A timed walk or run** over a fixed route, such as 1.6 km (1 mile).
- **The 30-second chair stand:** count how many times you can rise from a chair, arms crossed, in half a minute. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention uses it to check leg strength in older adults.[^12]

## How not to overdo it

Numbers are tools, and it is possible to have too many of them.

- **Look at trends.** Blood pressure, heart rate and sleep bounce around from day to day. Averages over a week or more tell the real story.
- **Measure the same way each time.** Same time of day, same device, same conditions.
- **Set a schedule and stick to it.** Unless your clinician has asked for daily readings, you do not need them.
- **Notice how tracking makes you feel.** If checking leaves you anxious rather than informed, check less. The goal is health, not a perfect spreadsheet.

> [!TIP] Bring your numbers along
> A short list of home readings, with dates, is far more useful to a clinician than "I think it was fine". Note the device you used and the time of day as well.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Book an appointment if your home blood pressure is consistently 130/80 mm Hg or higher, or if your resting heart rate is regularly above 100, or below 60 with dizziness or fainting when you are not an athlete.[^1][^8] A reading above 180/120 with chest pain, breathlessness, weakness, or a change in vision or speech is an emergency: call your local emergency number.[^1]

## The bottom line

Learn your blood pressure, cholesterol, HbA1c and waist-to-height ratio, because those four have clear targets and proven ways to improve them. Add resting heart rate, activity, sleep and one fitness test if you find them motivating. Measure consistently, watch the trend and bring the numbers to your next appointment.
