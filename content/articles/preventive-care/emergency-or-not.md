At two in the morning, with a feverish child or a pain you have never felt before, the hardest question is often not "what is this?" but "where do I go?" Pick too low and you lose time that matters. Pick too high and you spend the night in a waiting room.

This guide sorts health problems into three tiers: call an ambulance now, get seen today, or book a routine appointment. Services have different names in every country, so the tiers are described by what they do. One rule overrides everything else here: if you think a life may be at risk, call your local emergency number.[^1][^2] When in doubt, go up a tier, not down.

## The three tiers at a glance

| Tier | What it is for | Where to go |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency | A threat to life right now, or a risk of permanent harm | Your local emergency number or an emergency department |
| Urgent | Needs attention today, but is not dangerous | Urgent care or a walk-in clinic, a same-day appointment, an out-of-hours service or a nurse advice line |
| Routine | Mild, stable or long-standing problems | Your usual clinic or a pharmacist |

## Call your local emergency number now

These are the symptoms that emergency services ask you to call about without delay.[^1][^2]

- **Chest pain or pressure**, especially if it lasts more than a few minutes, spreads to an arm, the jaw, neck or back, or comes with breathlessness, a cold sweat or nausea.[^5]
- **Signs of a stroke:** one side of the face drooping, weakness in an arm, slurred or confused speech, or a sudden loss of balance or change in vision. Call even if the symptoms pass.[^6]
- **Severe difficulty breathing:** gasping, choking, being unable to get words out, or lips turning blue.
- **Heavy bleeding** that sprays, pours, forms a puddle or will not stop.
- **Sudden swelling** of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, which can signal a severe allergic reaction.
- **A seizure** in someone who has never had one, a seizure that does not stop, or a person who cannot be woken.
- **Sudden confusion:** someone who abruptly does not make sense or does not know where they are.[^10]
- **A sudden, extremely painful headache**, particularly with a stiff neck, loss of vision, drowsiness or trouble speaking.[^7]
- **Serious injury:** a road crash or other serious accident, a deep wound, a severe burn, or a head injury followed by loss of consciousness or confusion.
- **Signs of sepsis**, a life-threatening reaction to infection: confusion or slurred speech, skin, lips or tongue that look blue, gray, pale or blotchy, a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it, or very fast or difficult breathing.[^8]
- **A suicide attempt** or anyone in immediate danger of harming themselves.

The list is not complete. If something feels seriously and suddenly wrong, that is reason enough to call.

> [!WARNING] Do not drive yourself
> With chest pain or stroke symptoms, call for an ambulance rather than getting behind the wheel. Crews can begin treatment before you reach the hospital, and you will not be at risk of collapsing in traffic.[^5][^6]

While you wait, follow the call-taker's instructions and stay with the person. Call back if they get worse, switch on the lights if it is dark, and gather their medicines if you can do it quickly.[^1] Our [first aid basics](/articles/first-aid-basics) guide covers what to do in the minutes before help arrives, and [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs) covers the less obvious symptoms.

## Get seen today

The middle tier is for problems that should not wait several days but do not threaten your life. Typical examples:[^2][^3]

- Sprains, strains and suspected broken bones in an arm or a leg
- Cuts that may need closing, and minor burns and scalds
- Earaches, sore throats, skin infections and rashes
- Minor eye injuries
- Vomiting and diarrhea, or stomach pain that is not severe
- A fever that is not settling

Where you go depends on your health system: an urgent care or walk-in center, a same-day slot with your family doctor, an out-of-hours service or a video consultation.

Your health system may also run an advice line that asks about your symptoms and tells you where to get help. NHS 111 in the UK, by phone or online, is one example.[^4] Find out which service covers you before you need it.

> [!TIP] Save the numbers today
> Put three contacts in your phone now: your local emergency number, your nurse advice line or out-of-hours service, and your regular clinic. If your phone has a medical ID or emergency information feature, add your allergies and medicines to it.

A problem can also change tier. If an "urgent" problem gets worse, for example pain that becomes severe, breathing that becomes labored or a person who becomes drowsy or confused, treat it as an emergency.

## What can wait for a routine appointment

Routine does not mean unimportant. It means the problem is mild or stable enough that a few days will not change the outcome. Aches that have been around for weeks, skin changes, sleep problems, low mood without thoughts of self-harm, medicine reviews and screening tests all belong here.

Two cautions. First, book the appointment rather than waiting indefinitely: a breast lump or any lump that is growing, unexplained bleeding, unexplained weight loss or a cough that has lasted three weeks or more should be assessed soon, even though none is usually an emergency.[^12] Second, prepare for the visit so that you get an answer. Our guide on [getting more out of a doctor's appointment](/articles/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor) shows how.

## Children and older adults: lower your threshold

The very young and the old can become seriously ill quickly, and with fewer of the classic warning signs.

**Babies and children.** A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher needs urgent medical advice the same day. So does a baby of 3 to 6 months with 39 °C (102.2 °F) or higher, and any child whose fever has lasted five days or who seems dehydrated.[^9]

Call your local emergency number if a feverish child has a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade under a pressed glass, a first seizure, pale, blotchy or blue skin, a weak, high-pitched cry, trouble breathing, or is drowsy and hard to wake.[^8][^9] You know your child: if they seem very unwell and you cannot say why, that counts. See [fever: when to worry](/articles/fever-when-to-worry) for more.

**Older adults.** Serious illness can arrive quietly in later life. Sudden confusion is the classic example. It can be the first sign of an infection, a stroke, a reaction to a medicine or another problem that needs treatment quickly, so it calls for an emergency assessment, not a wait until morning.[^10]

## In a mental health crisis

A crisis of the mind is as real an emergency as a crisis of the body. If someone has harmed themselves or is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department.[^2][^4] If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm but are safe for the moment, contact a crisis line straight away. Find A Helpline is a free directory of crisis lines by country.[^11]

## What to have ready

Wherever you end up, a little information speeds everything up:

- A list of your medicines and doses, or the packets themselves
- Allergies and long-term conditions
- When the symptoms started and what you have taken for them
- Your ID and health card or insurance details
- A phone, a charger and the number of someone to contact

> [!DOCTOR]
> If you are torn between two tiers, choose the higher one. Call-takers and triage nurses sort patients by urgency all day: a call-taker can point you to other care if you do not need an ambulance, and emergency departments treat the most serious problems first.[^1][^2]

## The bottom line

If it might be life-threatening, call your local emergency number. If it needs attention today, use urgent care, a same-day appointment or an advice line. If it is mild and stable, book a routine visit, and do book it. And if you cannot decide, round up.
