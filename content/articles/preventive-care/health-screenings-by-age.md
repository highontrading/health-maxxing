Most of medicine waits until you feel unwell. Screening works the other way round: it goes looking for trouble in people who feel fine. That is a big ask, because a test offered to millions of healthy people has to do more good than harm, and surprisingly few pass.

This checklist covers the ones that have. It leans mainly on the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), an independent panel that grades the evidence behind each test, with notes on where other countries differ.[^1] Your own health service may use different ages and intervals, so treat this as a map and check local guidance before you plan the trip.

## How to use this list

Three ground rules first.

- **Screening is for people without symptoms.** A new lump, unexplained bleeding or a change that will not go away needs an appointment now, whatever your age.
- **"Average risk" is doing a lot of work.** A strong family history, a previous abnormal result or certain medical conditions can move every date earlier.
- **More is not better.** Every test produces false alarms, and some find slow-growing problems that would never have caused harm, which is called overdiagnosis.[^7] A test earns its place only when the benefit clearly outweighs those costs.

Here is the headline schedule from the USPSTF. The sections below fill in the detail.[^1]

| Screening | Who | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | All adults from 18 | Every 3 to 5 years, then yearly from 40 |
| Cervical | Ages 21 to 65 | Every 3 or 5 years, depending on the test |
| Diabetes | Ages 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity | No fixed interval |
| Breast | Women 40 to 74 | Every 2 years |
| Colorectal | Ages 45 to 75 | Yearly to every 10 years, depending on the test |
| Lung | Ages 50 to 80 with a heavy smoking history | Yearly |
| Bone density | Women from 65 | No fixed interval |
| Aortic aneurysm | Men 65 to 75 who have ever smoked | Once |

> [!NOTE] Where you live changes the dates
> These are mostly US recommendations. National programmes in the UK, Canada, Australia and elsewhere use their own ages, tests and intervals, and all of them are revised as new evidence arrives. Your local programme is the one to follow.

## Your 20s and 30s

**Blood pressure.** Screening starts at 18. If your readings are normal and you have no risk factors, the USPSTF suggests a recheck every three to five years until you are 40, then every year. People at higher risk are checked yearly from the start.[^2] Our guide to [blood pressure numbers](/articles/blood-pressure-numbers-explained) explains what the readings mean.

**Cervical screening.** US advice is a cervical cytology (Pap) test every three years from 21 to 29. From 30 to 65, the options are cytology every three years or a test for high-risk HPV, the virus linked to nearly all cervical cancer, every five years.[^3] The NHS starts a little later, inviting people from 25 to 64.[^9]

**Infections.** The USPSTF recommends HIV testing for everyone aged 15 to 65 and hepatitis C testing for adults aged 18 to 79. It also recommends chlamydia and gonorrhea testing for sexually active women aged 24 and under, and for older women at increased risk.[^1]

**Mood.** Screening for depression is recommended for all adults, and screening for anxiety for adults up to 64.[^1] In practice this is a short questionnaire, followed by a proper conversation if it flags something.

**Cholesterol.** The American Heart Association advises a first cholesterol test from age 20, repeated every four to six years while your risk stays low.[^10]

**Teeth and eyes.** Dental check-ups are not one-size-fits-all. The UK's NICE recommends an interval of anywhere from 3 months to 2 years for adults, set by your dentist according to your risk.[^11] For eyes, the US National Eye Institute advises a dilated eye exam every one to two years once you are over 60, and earlier or more often if you have diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of glaucoma.[^12]

## Your 40s

**Diabetes.** From 35 to 70, the USPSTF recommends a blood sugar test if you have overweight or obesity.[^1]

**Heart risk.** Between 40 and 75, a single cholesterol number matters less than your overall risk. The USPSTF recommends a statin for people in this age range who have at least one risk factor, such as high cholesterol, diabetes, high blood pressure or smoking, and an estimated 10-year risk of heart attack or stroke of 10% or more.[^1] Whether that applies to you is a calculation your clinician can run in a couple of minutes.

**Breast.** In 2024 the USPSTF lowered its starting age and now recommends a mammogram every two years from 40 to 74.[^4] The NHS invites women every three years from 50 until their 71st birthday.[^9] Both are defensible readings of the same trials. They differ on how to weigh earlier detection against more false alarms.

**Colorectal.** US screening now starts at 45 and runs to 75. You can choose between a home stool test every year, a stool DNA test every one to three years or a colonoscopy every 10 years, among other options.[^5] The NHS in England posts a home stool test every two years to people aged 50 to 74.[^9] The best test is the one you will actually do.

## Your 50s

**Lung.** A yearly low-dose CT scan is recommended from 50 to 80 for people with a 20 pack-year smoking history who still smoke or who quit within the past 15 years. A pack-year is one pack a day for a year, so 20 pack-years could be a pack a day for 20 years or two packs a day for 10.[^6]

**Prostate.** There is no blanket recommendation here. For men aged 55 to 69, the USPSTF says a PSA blood test should be an individual decision, made after a conversation about the trade-off: a small chance of avoiding death from prostate cancer against a larger chance of false alarms, overdiagnosis and side effects from treatment. From 70, it recommends against PSA screening.[^7]

Everything from your 40s carries on through this decade. [Cancer screening, explained](/articles/cancer-screening-explained) goes deeper into how to weigh these choices.

> [!TIP] Make your next visit count
> Write down which close relatives had cancer, heart disease or diabetes, and roughly how old they were. Add the dates of your last screenings. That one page tells your clinician whether the standard schedule fits you.

## From 65

**Bone density.** Women are advised to have a bone density scan from 65, and earlier after menopause if a risk assessment suggests a raised chance of fracture.[^1]

**Abdominal aortic aneurysm.** Men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked are advised to have a single ultrasound scan to look for a bulge in the body's main artery.[^1] In the UK, all men are invited in the year they turn 65.[^9]

**Winding down.** Screening has an end as well as a start. Routine cervical screening stops at 65 for people with adequate earlier results.[^3] Colorectal screening becomes a case-by-case decision from 76 to 85.[^5] For mammograms from 75, the USPSTF says there is not enough evidence to judge either way.[^4] Lung screening stops at 80, or once 15 years have passed since you quit.[^6]

## What is not on the list, and why

Some absences are deliberate. In 2023 the USPSTF concluded that there is not enough evidence to recommend for or against routine full-body skin examinations in adults without symptoms.[^8] That is a verdict on mass screening, not on paying attention. Knowing your own skin and showing a clinician any mole that changes still matters, and our guide to [skin cancer and mole checks](/articles/skin-cancer-and-mole-checks) shows what to look for.

Vaccines are not screening either, but they belong in the same conversation. The [adult vaccines guide](/articles/adult-vaccines-guide) covers what is due and when.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Screening schedules are for people who feel well. See a clinician promptly, whatever your age or your last result, if you notice a new lump, unexplained bleeding, a change in bowel habits that lasts more than a few weeks, a changing mole, unexplained weight loss or a cough that will not go away.

## The bottom line

Find your decade, check which tests apply to you and book the ones that are overdue. Tell your clinician about your family history, because it can change the schedule. And remember what screening is for: people with no symptoms. If something has changed in your body, skip the queue and get it looked at.
