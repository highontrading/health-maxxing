You open the results portal and find forty numbers, three of them in red, and a note that says "no action needed". Or no note at all. Blood tests are among the most common things medicine does to us and among the least explained.

This guide covers what the usual tests measure, what a reference range is (not quite what most people assume), which numbers have firm cut-offs and what a slightly abnormal result usually means. It cannot interpret your results, because that takes your history and a clinician. It can make that conversation a lot easier.

## What a "normal range" actually is

Most results arrive with a reference range beside them. Laboratories typically set it by testing a large group of healthy people and taking the middle 95% of their results.[^2] The range describes what is common. It is not a definition of healthy.

Two things follow. First, 5% of perfectly healthy people land outside the range on any given test. Run 20 unrelated tests on one healthy person and, by simple arithmetic, the odds are better than even that at least one comes back flagged.

> [!STAT] 1 in 20
> The share of healthy people who fall outside a standard reference range on any single test, purely by how the range is defined.[^2]

Second, ranges vary. Laboratories use different equipment and methods, and the right range can depend on your age and sex. Always judge a result against the range printed on your own report, not against a figure from the internet. That includes the typical figures below.[^1]

Some tests work differently. HbA1c, cholesterol and kidney function are judged against fixed cut-offs from clinical guidelines, chosen because the risk of disease rises beyond them.

> [!NOTE] Check the units
> The same result can be written two ways. A glucose of 100 mg/dL is 5.6 mmol/L, and a cholesterol of 200 mg/dL is 5.2 mmol/L. The US mostly uses mg/dL, and many other countries use mmol/L. Mixing them up is the fastest route to a needless fright.

## The common tests, decoded

### Full blood count

Also called a complete blood count, this counts the cells in your blood.[^3]

- **Hemoglobin** is the protein in red cells that carries oxygen. A low level is called anemia. Typical adult ranges are about 14 to 17.5 g/dL for men and 12 to 15.5 g/dL for women.
- **White blood cells** fight infection. A typical count is about 4,500 to 11,000 per microliter.
- **Platelets** help blood to clot. A typical count is about 150,000 to 450,000 per microliter.
- **MCV** is the average size of your red cells. It helps to work out why someone is anemic.

### Kidney function and salts

A basic metabolic panel measures salts such as sodium and potassium, along with glucose, calcium and two markers of kidney function.[^3] The most useful is creatinine, a waste product that the kidneys clear. The laboratory combines it with your age and sex to estimate how well your kidneys filter, a figure called eGFR.

An eGFR of 60 or more is in the normal range. A result below 60 may mean kidney disease, and one of 15 or lower may mean kidney failure.[^4] A low reading is usually repeated, and paired with a urine test for the protein albumin, before anyone reaches a diagnosis.[^4]

### Liver enzymes

ALT and AST are enzymes that leak into the blood when liver cells are irritated or damaged. Upper limits vary a good deal between laboratories. The American College of Gastroenterology argues that a truly healthy ALT is no more than about 29 to 33 units per liter for men and 19 to 25 for women, which is lower than many laboratories print.[^5]

Mild rises are common. The same guideline advises repeating the test to confirm the result before investigating further. The usual suspects are fatty liver disease, alcohol and medicines.[^5]

### Cholesterol

A lipid panel reports total cholesterol, LDL ("bad") cholesterol, HDL ("good") cholesterol and triglycerides. For healthy adults, MedlinePlus lists these levels:[^6]

| Measure | Healthy level |
| --- | --- |
| Total cholesterol | 125 to 200 mg/dL (3.2 to 5.2 mmol/L) |
| LDL | Below 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) |
| HDL | 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) or higher for men, 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) or higher for women |

These are not pass marks. The LDL level worth aiming for depends on your overall risk of heart disease, which our guide to [cholesterol](/articles/cholesterol-explained) explains.

### Blood sugar

Fasting glucose is a snapshot. HbA1c (also written A1C) reflects your average blood sugar over roughly the past three months. US cut-offs are:[^7]

| Category | HbA1c | Fasting glucose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Below 5.7% | 99 mg/dL (5.5 mmol/L) or lower |
| Prediabetes | 5.7% to 6.4% | 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) |
| Diabetes | 6.5% or higher | 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher |

Without clear symptoms, a diagnosis of diabetes usually needs a second test to confirm it.[^7] See [blood sugar basics](/articles/blood-sugar-basics) for what the categories mean.

### Thyroid

TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the signal your pituitary gland sends to tell the thyroid to work. It moves in the opposite direction to thyroid activity. A high TSH usually points to an underactive thyroid, and a low TSH to an overactive one.[^8] A typical reference range runs from about 0.4 to somewhere between 4 and 5 mIU/L, depending on the laboratory. [Thyroid 101](/articles/thyroid-101) has more.

### Iron, vitamin D and B12

- **Ferritin** reflects your iron stores. A level below about 30 micrograms per liter suggests that they are low. Ferritin also rises with inflammation, so a normal figure can mislead, and it is often read alongside other iron tests.[^9]
- **Vitamin D** is measured as 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The US National Institutes of Health considers 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) or more adequate for most people, below 30 nmol/L (12 ng/mL) too low and above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) possibly too high.[^10]
- **Vitamin B12** below about 200 to 250 pg/mL (148 to 185 pmol/L) counts as low in most laboratories. A borderline result can be clarified with a follow-up test called methylmalonic acid.[^11]

### CRP

C-reactive protein is made by the liver, and it rises when there is inflammation somewhere in the body, whether from an infection, an injury or a long-term inflammatory condition. It tells you that something is going on, not what or where.[^12]

## When a result is slightly off

A number just outside its range usually has a dull explanation.[^1]

- **Chance.** The 1-in-20 rule.
- **What you did beforehand.** Food, drink, vigorous exercise and stress can all shift results.
- **Medicines.** Many change blood values, which is often the reason the test was ordered.
- **A passing illness.** A cold last week can still show up.

Clinicians weigh four things: how far outside the range the result is, whether it is changing over time, whether related tests point the same way and how you feel. A common next step is simply to repeat the test.

The reverse also holds. A normal result does not rule everything out, so symptoms that persist still deserve attention.

## Before the test, and after

**Fasting.** A fasting glucose test means at least 8 hours without food or drink other than water.[^7] You may be asked to fast for 9 to 12 hours before a cholesterol test.[^6] An HbA1c test needs no fasting.[^7] Follow the instructions you are given.

**Questions worth asking.**

- What was this test looking for?
- Is this result meaningfully abnormal for me, or just outside the range?
- Could anything I took, ate or did have affected it?
- Does it need repeating, and when?
- What would we do differently because of it?

Our guide to [getting more out of a doctor's appointment](/articles/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor) has more on asking good questions.

> [!TIP] Keep your own record
> Ask for copies of your results and keep them together. A number that has been steady for ten years means something different from one that has drifted, and your old results are the only way to tell.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Blood tests do not replace how you feel. If you have chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, confusion or black or bloody stools, seek urgent care without waiting for results. If a result is far outside its range and nobody has contacted you, call and ask.

## The bottom line

Read each result against the range on your own report, check the units, and remember that a flag is a prompt to look closer, not a diagnosis. Ask what the test was for, whether the result matters for you and whether it should be repeated. Then keep your results, because the trend over the years tells you more than any single number.
