"Catch it early" is some of the most intuitive advice in medicine. It is also only partly right. For a handful of cancers, testing people who feel well clearly prevents deaths. For many others it does not, and for a few it has done more harm than good.

This explainer covers how screening works, why the statistics around it are slippery, which programs have solid evidence and how to think about your own choices. The recommendations come mostly from the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF). Other countries draw the lines differently, so check what your national program offers.

## What screening is, and what it is not

Screening means testing people who have no symptoms, either to find cancer early or to find changes that could become cancer.[^1] That second job matters. Cervical and colorectal screening can prevent cancer altogether, because abnormal cells and polyps can be removed before they turn into anything worse.[^3][^4]

Screening is not the same as testing someone who has a symptom. If you have found a lump or noticed blood in your stool, you need diagnostic tests, promptly, and the screening schedule no longer applies to you.

For screening to be worthwhile, several things have to be true at once. The cancer needs an early stage that a test can detect. The test has to be accurate and safe enough to offer to large numbers of healthy people. And treating the cancer earlier has to lead to better outcomes than treating it when symptoms appear.[^1] Most cancers fail at least one of those conditions, which is why the list of screening programs is short.

## Why survival statistics can fool you

The obvious way to judge a screening test is to ask whether people whose cancers were found by screening live longer after diagnosis. Unfortunately that comparison is rigged in the test's favor, in three ways.[^2]

**Lead-time bias.** Imagine a cancer that will cause death at 71. Found through symptoms at 68, it gives three years of survival. Found by screening at 63, it gives eight. Survival has more than doubled, yet the person has not gained a single day. All that changed was the date of the diagnosis.

**Length bias.** Fast-growing cancers spend little time in the stage where they are detectable but silent, so a test done every year or two tends to pick up the slower, less dangerous ones. Cancers found by screening therefore look more curable than average, whether or not the screening helped.

**Overdiagnosis.** Some cancers grow so slowly, or not at all, that they would never have caused symptoms in a person's lifetime. Screening finds them anyway. Because nobody can tell them apart from the dangerous kind at the time, they get treated.

South Korea offers the starkest example. After thyroid ultrasound became a common add-on to health checks, thyroid cancer diagnoses in 2011 were 15 times what they had been in 1993, while deaths from the disease barely changed. Almost all of those people were treated, usually with surgery.[^10]

This is why researchers judge screening by one hard number from randomized trials: do fewer people in the screened group die of the cancer?[^2]

> [!NOTE]
> A false positive and overdiagnosis are different problems. A false positive is an alarm that further tests show was not cancer. Overdiagnosis is a real cancer, correctly identified, that would never have mattered.

## The programs with good evidence

### Cervical

Cervical screening is the clearest success, because it finds precancerous changes that can be treated before cancer develops. The USPSTF recommends a cytology (Pap) test every three years from 21 to 29. From 30 to 65, the options are cytology every three years or a test for high-risk HPV every five.[^3] Our guide to [breast and cervical screening](/articles/breast-and-cervical-screening) has the details.

### Colorectal

Screening is recommended from 45 to 75 in the US, and you get a choice of tests. The USPSTF judges with high certainty that screening from 50 to 75 has a substantial net benefit, and with moderate certainty that starting at 45 has a moderate one.[^4]

| Feature | Stool tests | Colonoscopy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How often | FIT (a test for hidden blood) every year, or stool DNA every 1 to 3 years | Every 10 years |
| What is involved | A sample collected at home | Bowel preparation and a visit to a clinic |
| If something is found | You need a colonoscopy to follow up | Polyps can often be removed during the test |

How much does it help? A randomized trial in Poland, Norway and Sweden invited people to a single colonoscopy and followed them for 10 years. The risk of [colorectal cancer](/conditions/colorectal-cancer) was 18% lower in the invited group (0.98% against 1.20%), although only 42% of those invited actually had the test. Among people who did, the researchers estimated the reduction at about 31%.[^12]

### Breast

The USPSTF recommends a mammogram every two years from 40 to 74.[^5] An independent UK panel that reviewed the trials in 2012 concluded that inviting women to screening cuts deaths from breast cancer by about 20%. It also put numbers on the trade-off. For every 10,000 UK women invited from age 50 for 20 years, about 43 deaths from breast cancer are prevented and about 129 women are overdiagnosed.[^8]

> [!STAT] 3 to 1
> The UK panel's estimate of how many women are overdiagnosed for each breast cancer death that screening prevents.[^8]

### Lung

Lung screening is only for people at high risk. The USPSTF recommends a yearly low-dose CT scan for adults aged 50 to 80 with a 20 pack-year smoking history who still smoke or who quit within the past 15 years.[^6]

In the US trial behind that advice, which enrolled more than 53,000 people at high risk, those screened with low-dose CT had 20% fewer deaths from lung cancer than those screened with chest X-rays. The price was a lot of false alarms: more than 9 in 10 positive scans turned out not to be cancer.[^9] Screening is also no substitute for [quitting](/articles/how-to-quit-smoking-and-vaping), which remains the bigger win.

### Prostate

Here the balance is close enough that the USPSTF does not recommend routine screening. For men aged 55 to 69, it calls the PSA blood test an individual decision.[^7]

Its estimate is that screening 1,000 men for about 13 years prevents roughly 1 death from prostate cancer and 3 cases of cancer that has spread. Many more men have false alarms, biopsies and treatment for cancers that would never have troubled them, and that treatment can cause erectile dysfunction and urinary incontinence. From 70, the USPSTF recommends against PSA screening.[^7] Our [prostate health guide](/articles/prostate-health-guide) walks through the decision.

## What about the new blood tests?

Multi-cancer detection tests look for fragments of DNA and other signals that tumors shed into the blood. The appeal is obvious: one blood sample, many cancers, including some that have no screening test at all.

The evidence is not there yet. The US National Cancer Institute says it is not known whether screening with these tests reduces deaths from cancer, or how any benefit compares with the harms of false alarms, follow-up scans and overdiagnosis.[^11] Until trials answer those questions, a negative result is not an all-clear, and these tests are not a substitute for the screening that is already proven.

## How to decide

Useful questions for any screening test:

- What is my chance of getting this cancer, and does my family history change it?
- Out of 1,000 people like me, how many avoid dying of it because of screening?
- How often does the test raise a false alarm, and what happens next if it does?
- What are the downsides of the follow-up tests and of treatment?

Then weigh the answers against what matters to you. Two well-informed people can look at the same numbers and choose differently, and both can be right. That is the point of what guideline writers call shared decision-making.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Screening is for people without symptoms, and a normal result does not protect you from what comes later. See a clinician promptly about a new lump, unexplained bleeding, a change in bowel habits lasting more than a few weeks, difficulty swallowing, a cough or hoarseness that persists, a changing mole or unexplained weight loss, however recent your last screening.

## The bottom line

Take up the screening that has strong evidence when your program offers it: cervical, colorectal, breast and, if you have a heavy smoking history, lung. Treat prostate screening as a real choice, and treat multi-cancer blood tests as research in progress. And never let a normal screening result talk you out of getting a symptom checked.
