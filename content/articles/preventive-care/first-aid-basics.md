Most emergencies do not happen in front of a doctor. They happen in kitchens, offices and parking lots, in front of whoever is there. For the first few minutes, that person is the emergency service.

The good news is that the most useful first aid is simple. This guide covers what the American Heart Association, the Red Cross and the NHS advise in the situations where minutes matter. Treat it as a primer, not a qualification: a certified course with hands-on practice is the real training, and nothing here replaces one.

## The first 30 seconds

Whatever has happened, start the same way.[^5]

1. **Check for danger.** Traffic, fire, electricity, water. You cannot help if you become the second casualty.
2. **Call for help.** Call your local emergency number, or get someone else to do it. Put the phone on speaker so the call-taker can coach you.
3. **Check for a response and for breathing.** Tap the person's shoulders and shout. Look at the chest for normal breathing.

## Unresponsive and not breathing normally

If someone does not respond and is not breathing, or is only making occasional gasps, treat it as cardiac arrest. Gasping is not normal breathing.[^3][^5] For a teenager or adult who has suddenly collapsed, the American Heart Association recommends hands-only CPR for bystanders, with no mouth-to-mouth required.[^1]

1. Call your local emergency number and send someone to fetch an automated external defibrillator (AED).
2. Kneel beside the person. Put the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, your other hand on top, and straighten your arms.
3. Push hard and fast: 100 to 120 compressions a minute, at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep. Let the chest rise fully after each push.[^3][^5]
4. When the AED arrives, switch it on and follow its spoken instructions. It decides whether a shock is needed.
5. Keep going until professionals take over or the person starts to breathe normally. If someone else is there, take turns, because good compressions are tiring.

> [!STAT] 2 to 3 times
> How much immediate CPR can raise a person's chance of surviving a cardiac arrest.[^1]

If you are trained, give 30 compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths. Breaths matter more for babies, children and anyone who has drowned, and for them the guidelines still call for CPR with breaths.[^3] The technique differs for small bodies, which is one of the best reasons to take a course.

## Choking

Someone who can still cough, speak or breathe has a partly blocked airway. Encourage them to keep coughing and stay with them.

Someone who cannot cough, speak or breathe needs help at once. For adults and children over one, current guidance is to alternate back blows and abdominal thrusts.[^2][^3]

1. Ask someone to call your local emergency number.
2. Stand to the side and slightly behind the person. Support their chest with one arm and bend them forward at the waist.
3. Give up to 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
4. If that fails, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts. Stand behind them, place a fist just above the navel, grasp it with your other hand and pull sharply inward and upward.
5. Repeat 5 and 5 until the object comes out. If the person becomes unresponsive, lower them to the floor and start CPR.

Babies under one need a different method: back blows and chest thrusts. Abdominal thrusts should not be used on babies or on anyone who is pregnant, for whom chest thrusts are used instead.[^2][^3] Both variations are best learned hands-on. NHS advice is that anyone who has received abdominal thrusts should be checked by a clinician afterward.

## Severe bleeding

Bleeding that spurts, pools or soaks through clothing can quickly become life-threatening. Call your local emergency number, then do this.[^4][^12]

- Press firmly and directly on the wound with a clean cloth, a dressing or, failing that, your hands. Wear gloves if you have them.
- Keep the pressure constant. Do not lift the pad to look. If blood soaks through, put another pad on top and keep pressing.
- If something is stuck in the wound, leave it there and press on either side of it.
- For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or a leg, guidelines recommend a tourniquet above the wound if one is available. If not, keep pressing.[^4]

## Burns

With burns, speed matters more than anything in your [first-aid kit](/articles/home-first-aid-kit).[^7]

- Cool the burn under cool or lukewarm running water as soon as you can. The NHS advises 20 minutes, and US first-aid guidelines suggest 5 to 20.[^7][^4]
- Remove clothing and jewelry near the burn, unless it is stuck to the skin.
- Do not use ice, iced water, creams or greasy substances such as butter.
- Cover the burn loosely with plastic wrap or a clean non-stick dressing, and keep the person warm.[^7][^4]

Get emergency care for any burn that is bigger than the person's hand, looks white or charred, or is on the face, genitals or bottom, and for all chemical and electrical burns. Blistered burns need medical attention too, as do burns in children under 10 and in people with diabetes, heart, lung or liver disease or a weakened immune system.[^7]

## Unresponsive but breathing

Someone who is unresponsive but breathing normally should be rolled onto their side with the head tilted gently back, which keeps the airway open. This is the recovery position. Call for help, stay with them and keep watching their breathing. If it stops, start CPR. If you suspect a neck or back injury, keep the head and spine in a straight line as you turn them, ideally while a helper steadies the head and neck.[^6]

## Seizures

A convulsive seizure looks frightening. Your job is to prevent injury and to time it.[^4][^8]

- Cushion the person's head, and move them only if they are in danger.
- Note the time the seizure starts.
- Do not hold them down, and do not put anything in their mouth.
- When the jerking stops, roll them into the recovery position and stay until they have fully recovered.

Call your local emergency number if it is their first seizure, if it lasts more than 5 minutes, if another follows before the person has regained consciousness, or if they are badly hurt or have trouble breathing afterward.[^8]

## Severe allergic reaction

[Anaphylaxis](/conditions/anaphylaxis) comes on fast: swelling of the throat or tongue, wheezing or trouble breathing, and feeling faint, often with a raised, itchy rash. It can follow a food, an insect sting or a medicine.[^9]

- If the person has an adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injector, use it at once in the outer thigh. Help them if they cannot manage it.
- Call your local emergency number and say "anaphylaxis".
- Lie them down, with legs raised if that helps. If breathing is hard, let them sit up. Do not let them stand or walk.
- If there is no improvement after 5 minutes, use a second auto-injector if there is one.
- Even if they improve, they still need to be assessed in hospital.[^9]

## Stroke and heart attack

**Stroke: think FAST.** Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call your local emergency number. Call even if the symptoms pass, and note when they started. A newer version, BE FAST, adds Balance problems and Eyes (sudden vision changes or loss).[^10]

**Heart attack.** Typical signs are pain or discomfort in the center of the chest that lasts more than a few minutes or goes away and comes back. Discomfort can spread to an arm, the back, neck or jaw, and may come with shortness of breath, a cold sweat, nausea or lightheadedness.[^11] Call for an ambulance and have the person sit and rest. First-aid guidelines support chewing an aspirin while you wait, provided the person is an alert adult, is not allergic and has not been told to avoid it. The call-taker can advise.[^4] If the person collapses and stops breathing normally, start CPR.

Our guide to [heart attack and stroke warning signs](/articles/heart-attack-and-stroke-warning-signs) covers the less obvious symptoms.

> [!WARNING] Do not wait and see
> People often delay calling in the hope that chest pain, stroke symptoms or an allergic reaction will pass. With all three, minutes matter. If you are unsure whether it is an emergency, call your local emergency number and let the call-taker decide.

> [!TIP] Get the real training
> A certified course takes a few hours and lets you practice on a manikin while an instructor corrects your technique. Red Cross and Red Crescent societies, St John Ambulance and heart associations run them in most countries.[^2] Skills fade, so refresh them every couple of years.

## The bottom line

Call early. Push hard and fast on the chest of anyone who is unresponsive and not breathing normally, press on bleeding, cool burns with running water and never put anything in the mouth of someone having a seizure. Then book a course: reading this took a few minutes, but a few hours of hands-on practice is what makes your hands remember it when it counts.
