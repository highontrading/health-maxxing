"Dizzy" is one of the vaguest words patients bring to a doctor, and one of the most common. In a US national survey, about 15% of adults reported a balance or dizziness problem in a single year.[^1]

Behind that one word sit very different experiences: the room spinning when you roll over in bed, a head rush on standing up, a sense of walking on a boat. Sorting out which one you have is most of the diagnosis. This piece explains the main types, the usual causes, what treats them, and the uncommon patterns that signal an emergency.

## What kind of dizzy?

| What it feels like | Name | Usual sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You or the room are spinning, tilting or moving | Vertigo | Inner ear, or less often the brain's balance pathways |
| You might faint; vision dims, especially on standing | Lightheadedness | Blood pressure, dehydration, heart rhythm, medicines |
| Unsteady on your feet, without spinning | Imbalance | Inner ear, nerves in the feet, vision, muscles, brain |

People often find their own dizziness hard to put into words, so clinicians now lean on two more reliable clues: **timing** (seconds, hours or days; one episode or many) and **triggers** (head movement, standing up or nothing at all).[^12] Note both before your appointment.

## The common inner-ear causes

Your inner ear contains fluid-filled canals that sense head movement. When one side sends the wrong signal, your brain reads it as motion, and you get vertigo, often with nausea.

| Condition | Episodes last | Typical trigger or feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BPPV | Under a minute | Rolling over, looking up, bending down |
| Vestibular migraine | Minutes to 3 days | History of migraine, light or sound sensitivity |
| Ménière's disease | Around 2 to 3 hours | Hearing loss, ringing and pressure in one ear |
| Vestibular neuritis | Constant for days | Sudden onset, often after a viral illness |

### BPPV: loose crystals

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo is the most common cause of vertigo. Tiny calcium crystals that belong in one part of the inner ear break loose and drift into a balance canal, where they send false signals whenever the head changes position. The result is intense spinning that lasts less than a minute.[^2]

A clinician confirms it by moving you quickly from sitting to lying with your head turned, and watching your eyes. Treatment is a sequence of head positions, most often the Epley maneuver, that rolls the crystals back out of the canal. Guidelines recommend this over scans or medication, neither of which is routinely needed.[^2]

> [!STAT] 56% vs 21%
> The share of people whose vertigo resolved completely after an Epley maneuver, compared with a sham maneuver or no treatment, in a Cochrane review of 11 trials.[^3]

BPPV often returns, and it can be treated again. Once you have had a diagnosis, you can be taught to do the maneuver at home.[^2]

### Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis

Here the balance nerve or inner ear becomes inflamed, usually by a virus. Severe, constant vertigo with nausea comes on over hours and lasts for days, then settles over a few weeks. If hearing is affected too, it is called labyrinthitis. Anti-sickness medicines help for the first few days only. After that, getting up and moving helps the brain recalibrate.[^5]

### Ménière's disease

This long-term inner-ear condition causes attacks of vertigo together with hearing loss, tinnitus and a feeling of pressure in the affected ear. Attacks typically last two to three hours. Medicines can ease attacks and may help prevent them, and hearing can decline over time, so it needs specialist follow-up.[^6]

### Vestibular migraine

Migraine can cause vertigo with or without a headache. The diagnosis is made in people with a history of migraine who have repeated episodes of moderate or severe dizziness lasting from five minutes to 72 hours, at least half of them accompanied by migraine features such as headache, sensitivity to light and sound, or visual aura.[^7] It is managed like other forms of [migraine](/articles/headaches-and-migraine).

## Lightheadedness on standing, and medicines

If the problem is a head rush when you stand, the likely cause is a brief fall in blood pressure. It is more common with dehydration, after long bed rest, in older age and with certain medicines, including some for blood pressure, depression and prostate symptoms. Rising slowly, drinking enough fluid and reviewing your medicines with a doctor usually help.[^8]

Dizziness is a listed side effect of many drugs.[^1] If it started soon after a new prescription or a dose change, tell your prescriber. Do not stop a medicine by yourself.

## What to expect at an appointment

A good history does most of the work, so expect questions about timing, triggers, hearing and medicines. The examination may include watching your eye movements, a positioning test for BPPV, checks of balance and walking, and blood pressure measured lying down and standing. Hearing tests, blood tests, specialized balance tests or a scan are added only when the pattern calls for them.[^1][^8] Our guide on [how to talk to your doctor](/articles/how-to-talk-to-your-doctor) can help you prepare.

## When dizziness could be a stroke

A small proportion of people who go to an emergency department with dizziness are having a stroke in the back of the brain, and dizziness or loss of balance may be the main symptom.[^12]

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number if dizziness or vertigo starts suddenly along with any of these: double vision or loss of vision; slurred speech or trouble swallowing; a drooping face; weakness, numbness or clumsiness of an arm or leg; being unable to stand or walk; a sudden severe headache or neck pain; or sudden hearing loss in one ear.[^9][^12] Also seek urgent help for dizziness with chest pain, palpitations or fainting.

For constant vertigo lasting more than a day, the difference between an inner-ear problem and a stroke can be subtle. A set of three bedside eye-movement tests, known as HINTS, picked up strokes more reliably than an early MRI scan in the study that introduced it, when performed by trained specialists.[^10] That is a reason to be examined in person, and not to rely on a normal early scan or on self-diagnosis.

## Treatment that retrains balance

Beyond specific fixes such as the Epley maneuver, the main treatment for lingering inner-ear dizziness is vestibular rehabilitation: a tailored program of eye, head and balance exercises, usually taught by a physical therapist. A Cochrane review of 39 trials found moderate to strong evidence that it is safe and effective for people with dizziness from one-sided inner-ear problems.[^11]

The underlying principle is the opposite of instinct. Avoiding movement feels safer, but it delays recovery, because the brain compensates by practicing. Sedating anti-dizziness medicines are useful for the first few days of a severe attack and unhelpful after that.[^5]

> [!TIP] Staying safe during an episode
> Sit or lie down at once when a spell starts. Get out of bed in stages, turn on lights at night, and take care on stairs. Do not drive, climb ladders or use dangerous tools while you are having episodes.[^4] Dizziness raises the risk of falls, especially in later life, so our guide to [balance and fall prevention](/articles/balance-and-fall-prevention) is worth a look.

## The bottom line

Note what the dizziness feels like, how long it lasts and what triggers it, then get it assessed: most causes are benign and many are treatable, sometimes in a single visit. Keep moving as you recover. Treat sudden dizziness with vision, speech, strength or walking problems as a possible stroke and call for emergency help.
