You walk into a room and forget why. You read the same paragraph three times. You meet someone, hear their name, and lose it before the handshake ends. None of this means your brain is failing. Mostly it means your attention was somewhere else.

There is a large industry ready to sell you a fix, from puzzle apps to capsules. The evidence favors cheaper tools. This guide explains how memory works, what counts as normal forgetting, what really helps, and what does not.

## How memory works, briefly

Making a memory takes three steps. You **encode** information by paying attention to it. Your brain **stores** it, strengthening the trace over the following hours and days. Later you **retrieve** it.

Most everyday lapses are failures of the first step. If you were thinking about your inbox when you put your keys down, the location was never recorded, so there is nothing to retrieve. This is why focus and memory are best treated together: attention is the gateway.

Working memory, the mental notepad you use to hold a phone number or follow a conversation, is small and easily overloaded. Much of what follows is about protecting it.

## Normal forgetting versus a warning sign

Brains slow a little with age. The difference between ordinary lapses and something worth checking is mainly about whether daily life is disrupted.[^1]

| Usually normal | Worth an assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Forgetting a name or appointment and remembering it later | Forgetting recently learned information and asking the same questions repeatedly |
| Misplacing things now and then, and retracing your steps to find them | Putting things in unusual places and being unable to retrace your steps |
| Occasionally struggling to find the right word | Trouble following or joining a conversation |
| Needing help now and then with a gadget's settings | Difficulty completing familiar tasks, such as a regular route or recipe |

If the right-hand column sounds familiar, for you or for someone close to you, it is worth arranging a checkup.

## What reliably helps

### Sleep

Sleep loss damages attention before anything else. A meta-analysis of 70 studies found that short-term sleep deprivation impaired almost every area of thinking, with the largest effect on simple sustained attention: more lapses and slower reactions.[^2]

> [!STAT] 17-19 hours
> After this long without sleep, performance on some tests was equivalent to, or worse than, that at a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05%.[^3]

Sleep also appears to help fix the day's learning in place, which makes a late night a poor way to study. See [how much sleep you need](/articles/how-much-sleep-do-you-need).

### Movement

Many people feel sharper after a walk or a workout. The longer-term picture is more modest than headlines suggest. In a well-known trial of 120 older adults, a year of brisk walking three times a week increased the size of the hippocampus, a key memory structure, by about 2%, while it shrank slightly in a stretching group.[^4]

But when researchers re-examined 24 meta-analyses of exercise trials in healthy people, the average benefit for thinking skills was small and shrank further after accounting for weaker study designs and publication bias.[^5] Exercise is worth doing for a dozen reasons. A dramatically sharper memory is probably not one of them.

### One thing at a time

What we call multitasking is usually rapid switching, and each switch carries a cost. Laboratory studies show that people lose time whenever they move between tasks, and lose more as the tasks get more complex or less familiar.[^6] Those fractions of a second add up, and errors rise.

The remedy is structural. Work in protected blocks on one task, silence notifications, and keep your phone out of sight while you do. If [caffeine](/articles/caffeine-benefits-and-limits) helps you concentrate, use it early in the day so it does not cost you sleep.

### Learn smarter

A review of ten common study techniques rated two as highly effective: **practice testing**, which means recalling material from memory instead of rereading it, and **distributed practice**, which means spreading study over time instead of cramming. Highlighting and rereading, the most popular methods, were rated as low in usefulness.[^7]

> [!TIP] Make it effortful
> Close the book and write down what you remember. Use flashcards or explain the idea aloud. Come back to it tomorrow, then in a few days, then next week. The slight struggle to recall is the part that makes the memory stick.

### Fix what is fixable

Several treatable problems imitate a poor memory.

- **Hearing loss.** If you do not hear it, you cannot remember it. In a trial of 977 older adults with untreated hearing loss, hearing aids and audiology support did not slow cognitive decline in the group as a whole over three years, but did slow it substantially in the participants at higher risk of decline.[^8] Treating hearing loss is worthwhile for communication alone.
- **Depression and anxiety.** Difficulty concentrating, remembering and making decisions is a core symptom of depression, and it usually improves with treatment.[^9]
- **Medicines, alcohol and poor sleep.** Sedating medicines, heavy drinking and untreated sleep apnea can all cloud thinking, as our [dementia guide](/articles/dementia-early-signs-vs-normal-aging) explains. Ask a doctor or pharmacist to review what you take.

## What does not live up to the marketing

**Memory supplements.** Ginkgo is the most studied. In a large trial of older adults it neither prevented dementia nor slowed cognitive decline, and US health authorities say there is no conclusive evidence that it helps any condition. It can also interact with blood-thinning medicines.[^12] Products marketed as nootropics generally have less evidence, not more.

**Brain-training games.** In 2016 the company behind Lumosity agreed to pay $2 million to settle US Federal Trade Commission charges that it had deceived customers with unfounded claims about improving performance at work and school and delaying age-related decline.[^11] A comprehensive scientific review the same year reached a careful verdict: training makes people better at the trained tasks, with less evidence of improvement on closely related tasks and little evidence of benefit for everyday thinking.[^10]

That does not mean mental activity is pointless. Learning a language, an instrument or a craft is enjoyable and demanding, and it comes with social contact. Just do not expect a puzzle app to do the job of sleep.

## A simple toolkit

- Put keys, glasses and phone in one fixed place.
- Write it down: lists and calendars free working memory for thinking.
- To learn a name, repeat it aloud and link it to something you already know.
- Do the hardest thinking when you are most alert, in a block with no notifications.
- Protect seven or more hours of sleep, and move every day.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if memory problems are affecting daily life, getting worse, or noticed by people close to you, or if they come with low mood, personality change or difficulty with familiar tasks. Many causes are treatable. Sudden confusion, or sudden trouble speaking or understanding, is an emergency: call your local emergency number.

## The bottom line

Sleep enough, move regularly, do one thing at a time and test yourself on what you want to remember. Get your hearing and mood checked if either is slipping. Save your money on memory pills and brain games, which have yet to match the results of an early night.
