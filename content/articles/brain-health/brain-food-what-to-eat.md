Blueberries for memory. Walnuts because they look like tiny brains. Fish oil, turmeric, and a capsule with a lightning bolt on the label. Few areas of nutrition attract as much hopeful marketing as "brain food".

The real evidence is less exciting and more useful. Diet does appear to matter for the aging brain, but through overall patterns followed for years, not through star ingredients. This piece covers what those patterns are, how strong the evidence is, which foods have the most support, and where supplements fit.

## Patterns beat single foods

Two eating patterns dominate the research. The **Mediterranean diet** is built on vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, nuts and fish, with little red meat and few sweets. The **MIND diet** is a variation developed by researchers in Chicago, combining the Mediterranean and blood-pressure-lowering DASH diets and singling out foods that had been linked with brain health.[^1]

| Eat more often | MIND target |
| --- | --- |
| Green leafy vegetables | 6 or more servings a week |
| Other vegetables | At least 1 a day |
| Berries | 2 or more servings a week |
| Nuts | 5 or more servings a week |
| Beans and lentils | More than 3 meals a week |
| Whole grains | 3 or more servings a day |
| Fish | At least once a week |
| Poultry | At least twice a week |
| Olive oil | As the main cooking fat |

The diet also limits red meat, butter and margarine, cheese, pastries and sweets, and fried or fast food.[^1]

## How strong is the evidence?

**Observational studies are encouraging.** In the study that introduced the MIND diet, researchers followed 923 older adults for about four and a half years. Those whose diets matched MIND most closely had a 53% lower rate of Alzheimer's disease than those who matched it least, and even moderate adherence was linked with a 35% lower rate.[^1]

That kind of study cannot prove cause. People who eat this way also tend to exercise more, smoke less, and have more education and income, and early brain changes may alter what people eat.

**Randomized trials are mixed.** In a Spanish trial, 447 older adults at high cardiovascular risk were assigned to a Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil, a Mediterranean diet with nuts, or advice to follow a low-fat diet. After about four years, cognitive test scores had improved in the Mediterranean groups and declined in the control group.[^3]

But the largest direct test of the MIND diet did not confirm its promise. In a three-year US trial of 604 older adults with a family history of dementia, both the MIND group and the control group were coached to cut calories modestly. Cognition improved in both groups, with no significant difference between them, and brain scans looked similar.[^2]

> [!NOTE] How to read a negative trial
> The MIND trial does not show that diet is irrelevant. Both groups lost weight and improved, three years is short for a disease that develops over decades, and the participants were not impaired to begin with. It does show that the dramatic numbers from observational studies should be taken as an upper limit, not a promise.

The World Health Organization weighed this evidence in its guidelines on reducing dementia risk. It says a Mediterranean-like diet may be recommended, and that a healthy, balanced diet should be recommended to all adults.[^4]

## Foods with the most support

**Leafy greens.** Among 960 older adults followed for nearly five years, those who ate about one serving of green leafy vegetables a day had slower cognitive decline than those who rarely ate them. The difference was equivalent to being about 11 years younger.[^5]

**Berries.** In a study of more than 16,000 older women, those who ate the most blueberries and strawberries had slower rates of cognitive decline, equivalent to delaying cognitive aging by up to two and a half years.[^6]

**Fish.** People who eat more fish tend to have a lower risk of cognitive decline and dementia in observational studies.[^7] Oily fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel are the main dietary source of the omega-3 fats DHA and EPA, and they have benefits for the heart as well, covered in our guide to [omega-3 and fish oil](/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-and-your-heart).

**Olive oil and nuts.** These were the two foods supplied in the Spanish trial, which makes them among the few with randomized evidence behind them.[^3]

All of the single-food findings except the last are observational, with the usual caution about cause and effect. The practical value is that they describe the same plate. Our [Mediterranean diet guide](/articles/mediterranean-diet-guide) shows how to build it.

## Where supplements fit

Here the evidence is far less kind.

**Omega-3 capsules.** A Cochrane review of three trials with 3,536 cognitively healthy older adults found that omega-3 supplements gave no benefit for cognitive function.[^8] Fish seems to help where fish oil does not, perhaps because of what fish replaces on the plate.

**B vitamins.** Folic acid and vitamins B6 and B12 lower blood levels of homocysteine, which is linked with dementia. Lowering it has not helped. A meta-analysis of 11 trials including 22,000 people found no significant effect of B vitamins on cognitive function.[^9]

The exception is true deficiency. Lack of vitamin B12 can cause memory problems, confusion and nerve damage, and it is more common in older adults, people who eat no animal products, and people taking metformin or long-term acid-reducing medicines.[^10] If that could be you, ask for a blood test. Our page on [vitamin B12 deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-b12-deficiency) explains more.

**Multivitamins.** This is the one area where recent trials have shifted the picture. The US COSMOS program tested a daily multivitamin against placebo in three groups of older adults. Pooled together, the results showed modest benefits for overall cognition and memory, which the researchers estimated as equivalent to about two years less cognitive aging.[^11]

> [!STAT] About 2 years
> The estimated slowing of cognitive aging from a daily multivitamin in pooled COSMOS trials of older adults. The effect was small, and no trial has shown that multivitamins prevent dementia.[^11]

Treat that with interest and caution. The participants were mostly well-educated white Americans, the benefit was small, and the WHO guidelines, written before these results, advise against taking vitamin B, vitamin E, omega-3 or multi-nutrient supplements to reduce the risk of cognitive decline.[^4] A multivitamin is unlikely to hurt, but it is not a substitute for the food.

## Alcohol is not brain food

The original MIND score gave a point for a daily glass of wine. Later evidence argues against that. In a 30-year British study with brain scans, people who drank moderately, at 14 to 21 UK units a week, had about three times the odds of shrinkage in the hippocampus, a key memory region, compared with abstainers, and light drinking showed no protective effect.[^12] See [alcohol and your health](/articles/alcohol-and-your-health) for the wider picture.

## Putting it on a plate

> [!TIP] Small swaps, repeated
> Add a handful of greens to one meal a day. Keep frozen berries for breakfast. Eat fish once or twice a week. Snack on unsalted nuts. Cook with olive oil. Swap white bread and rice for whole-grain versions, and use beans in place of meat a few times a week.

Food works alongside the other things that protect the brain: controlling blood pressure and diabetes, staying active and not smoking.[^4] Our guide to [dementia prevention](/articles/dementia-prevention-what-we-know) puts diet in that wider context.

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if you or someone close to you has memory or thinking problems that affect daily life, or symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency such as numbness, tingling or unusual tiredness. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking supplements if you use blood-thinning medicine, since high doses of fish oil can affect clotting.[^7]

## The bottom line

Eat the way the Mediterranean and MIND patterns suggest: plenty of vegetables, especially leafy greens, plus berries, beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and regular fish. Expect a modest, long-term benefit, not a transformation. Get tested for B12 if you are at risk, keep alcohol low, and be skeptical of any capsule that promises a sharper mind.
