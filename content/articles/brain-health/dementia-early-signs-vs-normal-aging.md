Few health worries cut as deep as this one. A lost word or a forgotten appointment can set off a private fear, in yourself or in someone you love, that something serious has begun.

Most of the time it has not. But the fear is not foolish, and knowing what to look for is better than either panicking or looking away. This guide explains the difference between ordinary aging and early warning signs, the treatable conditions that can look like dementia, how diagnosis works, what treatment can offer today, and how to support someone you are worried about.

## Dementia is not one disease

Dementia is an umbrella term. It describes a decline in memory, thinking, language or behavior that is serious enough to interfere with everyday life, and it has many possible causes.[^1]

About 57 million people worldwide were living with dementia in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases each year. It becomes more common with age, but it is not a normal or inevitable part of aging.[^1]

| Type | Typical early features |
| --- | --- |
| Alzheimer's disease | Trouble remembering recent events and conversations. The most common cause, involved in an estimated 60% to 70% of cases |
| Vascular dementia | Slowed thinking and difficulty planning, sometimes after a stroke or in steps |
| Dementia with Lewy bodies | Alertness that fluctuates, visual hallucinations, movement changes, acting out dreams |
| Frontotemporal dementia | Changes in personality, behavior or language, often starting before age 65 |

Many older people have more than one type at once, which is called mixed dementia.[^3][^4]

## Normal aging versus warning signs

Aging brains are a little slower at recalling names and learning new things. What separates that from dementia is not the occasional lapse but a pattern that disrupts daily life.[^2]

| Typical with age | Worth an assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Forgetting a name or appointment, then remembering it later | Forgetting recent events or asking the same question repeatedly |
| Making an occasional error with bills | Struggling to follow a familiar recipe or keep track of regular payments |
| Needing help sometimes with a new device | Difficulty with long-familiar tasks, such as a usual route or the rules of a favorite game |
| Losing track of the day of the week, then working it out | Losing track of dates, seasons or where you are |
| Misplacing things and retracing your steps to find them | Putting things in odd places, being unable to retrace steps, sometimes accusing others of stealing |
| Sometimes searching for a word | Stopping mid-conversation, repeating yourself, or calling things by the wrong name |
| Feeling flat about social plans now and then | Withdrawing from hobbies and friends, or marked changes in mood, personality or judgment |

Two points help in real life. First, people close to the person often notice changes before they do. Second, change from someone's own previous level matters more than comparison with anyone else.

## Mild cognitive impairment: the in-between

Some people have memory or thinking problems that are greater than expected for their age, and that show up on tests, while they still manage everyday life independently. This is called mild cognitive impairment, or MCI.

It is common, affecting roughly 7% of people in their early 60s and about a quarter of those in their early 80s. It raises the risk of dementia but does not guarantee it: among people over 65 with MCI, about 15% developed dementia within two years, while many stayed stable and some improved.[^5]

No medicine has been shown to stop MCI progressing. US neurology guidance recommends regular exercise, reviewing medicines that might be contributing, and monitoring over time.[^5]

## Look for treatable causes first

Not all memory loss is dementia. Part of any proper assessment is checking for conditions that can impair thinking and can often be treated:[^3][^6]

- **Depression and anxiety**, which commonly cause poor concentration and forgetfulness.
- **Medicines**, especially sedatives, strong painkillers and drugs with anticholinergic effects, which include some bladder, allergy and sleep remedies.
- **[Vitamin B12 deficiency](/conditions/vitamin-b12-deficiency)** and **[an underactive thyroid](/conditions/hypothyroidism)**, both found with simple blood tests.
- **Poor sleep and [sleep apnea](/articles/snoring-and-sleep-apnea)**.
- **Heavy drinking**, and **hearing or vision loss** that makes people seem confused.
- **Delirium**, a sudden state of confusion caused by infection, dehydration, medicines or other illness. It develops over hours or days, not months, and needs urgent medical attention.

This is the strongest argument against waiting. An early appointment can find something fixable.

## How a diagnosis is made

There is no single test for dementia. An assessment usually includes:[^3][^6]

1. **A detailed history**, ideally with a relative or friend who can describe what has changed.
2. **Short tests of memory and thinking.**
3. **A physical examination and blood tests** to look for other causes.
4. **Referral to a memory clinic or specialist** where needed, often with a brain scan.

Newer tools can show the biology of Alzheimer's disease directly. Blood tests that measure Alzheimer's-related proteins have become accurate enough that, in 2025, the Alzheimer's Association issued its first guideline on them. It recommends them only for people with cognitive symptoms who are being assessed in specialist care, where the most accurate tests can reduce the need for brain scans or spinal fluid tests. They are not recommended for people without symptoms.[^8]

> [!WARNING] The limits of testing yourself
> Online memory quizzes and direct-to-consumer tests cannot diagnose dementia. They cannot separate it from depression, poor sleep or medicine side effects, and a biomarker result without symptoms does not mean you have or will get dementia. Even formal screening of older adults with no symptoms is not recommended by the US Preventive Services Task Force, which found insufficient evidence that it helps.[^7] If you are worried, see a doctor.

## What treatment can offer today

There is no cure, but there is more to offer than many people expect.

**Symptom medicines.** Long-established drugs can modestly improve or stabilize memory and daily functioning for a time in Alzheimer's disease and some other dementias.[^6]

**Anti-amyloid antibodies.** Two newer drugs, lecanemab and donanemab, remove amyloid protein from the brain and are given by regular infusion to people in the early stages of Alzheimer's disease whose amyloid has been confirmed. In their main trials, each slowed decline over 18 months by roughly a quarter to a third compared with placebo. On an 18-point clinical scale, the difference with lecanemab was about half a point, which many people would not notice day to day.[^9][^10]

The risks are significant. Brain swelling or small bleeds appeared on scans in a substantial minority of participants: swelling in about 13% of those given lecanemab and 24% of those given donanemab. Most cases caused no symptoms, but a few were serious or fatal, and risk is higher in people with certain genes.[^9][^10] Treatment requires regular brain scans, and regulators and health systems in different countries have reached different conclusions about whether the benefits justify the risks and cost.

**Support that is not a pill.** Structured group activities known as cognitive stimulation, help with daily living, treating hearing loss and other health problems, and support for family caregivers all improve quality of life.[^6] A diagnosis also gives time to plan finances, legal matters and future care while the person can make their own decisions.

## Supporting someone you are worried about

- **Choose a calm moment** and describe specific things you have noticed, without labels. "I have noticed you seem to be finding the bills harder" lands better than "your memory is going".
- **Offer to go along** to the appointment, and bring a list of changes and of all their medicines.
- **Keep the person at the center.** People living with dementia remain themselves. Speak to them, not about them, and let them do what they still can.
- **Look after yourself.** Caring is demanding. National Alzheimer's and dementia associations run helplines and local support groups, and Alzheimer's Disease International lists them by country.[^11]

> [!DOCTOR]
> See a doctor if memory or thinking changes are affecting daily life, getting worse, or worrying you or the people around you. Seek urgent medical help for confusion that comes on suddenly over hours or days, and call your local emergency number for sudden confusion with a drooping face, arm weakness or speech difficulty.

Many of the same habits that protect the heart appear to lower dementia risk, which we cover in [dementia prevention: what we know](/articles/dementia-prevention-what-we-know).

## The bottom line

Occasional forgetfulness that you can recover from is part of getting older. Changes that disrupt daily life are not, and they deserve a proper assessment, because the cause may be treatable and an early diagnosis opens up support and choices. Skip the online quizzes and talk to a doctor.
