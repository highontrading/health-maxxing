A clash of heads on a soccer field. A fall from a bike. A car that stops faster than its driver. The person gets up, says they are fine and carries on, and an hour later they have a headache and cannot remember the score.

That is a concussion, and how it is handled over the next few days matters. The advice has changed a good deal in the past decade. This guide covers how to recognize a concussion, the signs that mean an emergency, what early recovery should look like, and the stepwise route back to school, work and sports.

## What a concussion is

A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by a blow to the head, or a hit to the body that makes the head and brain move rapidly back and forth. It disturbs how the brain works for a while, without the kind of structural damage that shows on a standard scan.[^1][^2]

Two myths cause trouble. You do not need to be knocked out: most concussions happen without loss of consciousness.[^2] And you do not need to hit your head, because a hard jolt to the body can be enough.

## Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may show up straight away, or hours to days later.[^2]

| Type | What you may notice |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Headache, dizziness, balance problems, nausea, blurred vision, sensitivity to light or noise, tiredness |
| Thinking | Feeling foggy or slowed down, trouble concentrating, memory gaps around the injury, confusion |
| Mood | Irritability, sadness, anxiety, feeling more emotional than usual |
| Sleep | Sleeping more or less than usual, trouble falling asleep |

People watching may notice that the injured person appears dazed, moves clumsily, answers slowly or forgets instructions.[^2] In sports, the rule is simple: if in doubt, sit them out. Anyone with a suspected concussion should leave play at once and not return that day.[^1]

## Red flags: get emergency help

A small number of head injuries involve bleeding in or around the brain, which is life-threatening and can develop over hours.

> [!DOCTOR]
> Call your local emergency number or go to an emergency department after a head injury if the person has: a headache that keeps getting worse; repeated vomiting; a seizure; increasing confusion, agitation or unusual behavior; drowsiness or difficulty waking; slurred speech, weakness, numbness or loss of coordination; one pupil larger than the other; any loss of consciousness; clear fluid from the nose or ears; or severe neck pain.[^1][^2][^3] In babies and toddlers, add crying that cannot be consoled and refusing to feed.[^2]

Some people should be assessed even after a seemingly minor knock: anyone taking blood-thinning medicine or with a bleeding disorder, older adults, people who have had brain surgery, and anyone who was drunk or had taken drugs when it happened.[^3][^4]

## The first 48 hours

Older advice was strict rest in a dark room until every symptom had gone. Trials have overturned it. In a randomized trial of young people with concussion, those told to rest strictly for five days reported more symptoms and recovered more slowly than those advised to rest for one to two days.[^5]

The current international consensus recommends **relative rest** for the first 24 to 48 hours: ordinary daily activities at home, light walking, and cutting back on anything that clearly worsens symptoms.[^1] Screens fall into that category early on. In a trial of 125 teenagers and young adults, those who avoided screens for the first 48 hours recovered in a median of 3.5 days, compared with 8 days for those who used them freely.[^6]

Practical points for the first day or two:[^3]

- Have an adult stay with the injured person for the first 24 hours.
- Sleep is fine and helps recovery. There is no need to keep someone awake once red flags have been ruled out.
- Use paracetamol (acetaminophen) for headache, as directed on the label.
- Avoid alcohol, and do not drive until you feel fully recovered.

## Back to school, work and sports

After the first day or two, activity is increased in steps. Mild, brief worsening of symptoms is acceptable. If symptoms flare more than mildly, drop back and try again the next day.[^1]

**Learning and work come first.** Start with reading and other mental tasks at home, move to part-time attendance with breaks and reduced workload, then return full-time. Most students are back within days, and nobody should return to full sports before returning to full learning.[^1]

**Return to sport follows six steps**, each lasting at least 24 hours:[^1]

| Step | What it involves |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Symptom-limited daily activity, such as walking |
| 2 | Aerobic exercise: light at first, then moderate, on a stationary bike or at walking pace |
| 3 | Individual sport-specific exercise, with no risk of head impact |
| 4 | Non-contact training drills |
| 5 | Full-contact practice, after medical clearance |
| 6 | Return to competition |

Steps 4 to 6 begin only once symptoms have fully resolved, including during and after exertion.[^1] The whole process takes a week at the very least. The NHS advises avoiding contact sports for at least three weeks.[^3]

> [!WARNING] Do not rush back
> A second blow before the brain has recovered can slow recovery and raise the chance of longer-term problems. In rare cases in young athletes, it has caused dangerous brain swelling.[^2]

## If symptoms last

Most people recover within two to four weeks. Symptoms beyond four weeks are called persisting symptoms, and they call for a proper assessment, not just more waiting.[^1]

Recovery can be actively helped. In a trial of adolescent athletes, prescribed aerobic exercise kept just below the level that provoked symptoms led to faster recovery than stretching: a median of 13 days compared with 17.[^7] Rehabilitation for the neck and balance system helps when [dizziness](/articles/dizziness-and-vertigo) or neck pain persists, and [headaches](/articles/headaches-and-migraine), sleep and mood can each be treated.[^1]

## Children and teenagers

Children's concussions are managed on the same principles, a little more cautiously. US pediatric guidance advises against routine brain scans for children with concussion, since imaging is for those with signs of a more serious injury, and recommends a gradual, supported return to school before sports.[^8] Tell the school, so that workload and screen time can be adjusted for a while.

## Repeated impacts and CTE

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, is a degenerative brain disease linked to repeated head impacts over many years, including blows that never cause a diagnosed concussion. It can be confirmed only by examining the brain after death.[^9]

The most quoted study found CTE in 110 of 111 brains of former National Football League players. Those brains were donated, often by families who had seen worrying changes, so the figure greatly overstates how common CTE is among players in general, as the authors themselves cautioned.[^10] How often it occurs, and at what level of exposure, is still unknown.

The sensible reading is that risk comes from cumulative exposure, not from a single well-managed concussion. That is a good reason to reduce unnecessary head impacts in training and to take every concussion seriously, without assuming one knock means lasting damage.

## The bottom line

Suspect a concussion after any blow followed by headache, dizziness, confusion or fogginess, whether or not the person blacked out. Rule out the red flags, rest relatively for a day or two, then build back in steps: learning first, sports last, contact only after symptoms have gone and a clinician has cleared it. If symptoms persist beyond four weeks, ask for a review.
